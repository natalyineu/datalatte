To create a fitness studio newsletter, pick one goal (bookings, retention or referrals), choose a fixed schedule, build one reusable template with a single call to action, collect addresses with consent, and add your postal address and an unsubscribe link. Compare results with a benchmark: MailerLite's December 2024 to November 2025 data shows health and fitness emails with a 47.81% open rate, 1.45% click rate and 0.30% unsubscribe rate.

Use clicks, bookings and visits as your real measures, because open rates are inflated by email privacy features. Sources are listed at the end, and anything not published is stated as such.

## What Should the Newsletter Do for Your Studio?

Choose one main job. Filling next week's classes needs a schedule at the top and a booking link. Retention needs member stories and instructor introductions. Referrals need a clear invitation to bring a friend. A newsletter that tries all three tends to have no clear action, so rotate the secondary content and keep one primary link in each issue.

Our [email marketing overview](/blog/email-marketing-fitness-studio) lists the other email types, such as welcome and win-back series, that should run alongside a newsletter.

## What Goes in Each Issue?

Use a repeatable layout so writing takes under an hour. A workable structure, which is a suggestion and not a tested formula:

1. Headline and one line on what is inside
2. This week's schedule highlights, with a booking link
3. One instructor or member story, with permission
4. One short practical tip
5. A single call to action button

Keep paragraphs short and test on a phone before sending. No source reviewed here gives a best length or best send day, so run your own comparison for a few weeks.

## How Do You Build and Grow the List?

Collect emails at sign-up, at trial booking and through a front-desk QR code to a sign-up form. Tell people what they will get and how often. Do not buy lists. In the US, the FTC says each email that breaks CAN-SPAM can carry a penalty of up to $53,088.

Members are not exempt from the rules. The FTC states that subscribers and members can opt out of marketing email too, so every issue needs an opt-out that works.

## What Do the Rules Require in Every Issue?

The FTC's CAN-SPAM guide asks for accurate sender details, an honest subject line, a clear indication that the message is an ad, your valid postal address, and a clear way to opt out. Opt-out requests must be honoured within 10 business days.

Gmail's sender guidelines add technical requirements. All senders need SPF or DKIM authentication and should keep spam rates below 0.3%, with Google advising below 0.10%. Senders of more than 5,000 messages a day also need DMARC and one-click unsubscribe. A studio list is usually far below that volume, but the authentication your email tool sets up still matters.

## How Do You Know It Is Working?

Track four things: clicks on the booking link, bookings made after each send, unsubscribes per send, and visits from subscribers compared with non-subscribers. Compare unsubscribes with MailerLite's 0.30% for health and fitness. A rate far above it suggests too many emails or content that does not match what people signed up for.

## FAQ

### How often should I send a fitness studio newsletter?

No source sets a number. Pick a schedule you can keep, such as every week or every second week, and watch unsubscribes against the 0.30% benchmark.

### What should the subject line say?

Say what is inside. The FTC requires subject lines that match the content of the message, so avoid bait.

### Do I need permission to email members?

Collect addresses with clear notice and include an opt-out. In the US, CAN-SPAM covers marketing email even to members. Rules outside the US were not reviewed.

### Is the open rate reliable?

Not fully. Privacy features pre-load emails, so opens run high. Clicks and bookings are more useful.
