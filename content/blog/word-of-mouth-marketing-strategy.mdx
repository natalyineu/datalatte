A word-of-mouth strategy for a local business has four parts: deliver something worth mentioning, make it easy to mention, ask at the right moment, and follow the rules on reviews and rewards. BrightLocal's 2026 survey of 1,002 US adults found 97% read reviews for local businesses, and trust in reviews sits at 49%, so a recommendation now often shows up online.

This guide is for owners of cafes, salons, studios and trade businesses who want more customers through recommendations. It covers asking for reviews and referrals, what Google and the FTC forbid, and a worked example. Where a number is not published, the article says so.

## What Is Word-of-Mouth Marketing for a Local Business?

It is customers telling other people about you, in person or online. For a local shop the online side is mostly Google reviews, social posts and messages to friends. BrightLocal says trust in reviews "still sits at a significant 49% today", which it reads as nearly half of consumers trusting online strangers about as much as people they know.

No source checked here gives a reliable share of new customers a typical local business wins from word of mouth, so the article does not claim one. Measure your own by asking every new customer how they found you. For the limits of relying on it alone, see [why word of mouth alone will not grow your business](/blog/why-word-of-mouth-alone-wont-grow-your-local-business-anymore).

## How Do You Give People Something to Talk About?

Recommendations follow experiences that stand out: a clear signature item, fast service, a small unexpected gesture, or a result they can show. Pick one thing for staff to do the same way every time. Write it down so a new hire repeats it.

Fix what makes people complain first. Public negative reviews are also word of mouth. Reply to each one calmly, and see our [guide to managing online reviews](/blog/managing-online-reviews-small-business). Then make sharing easy: a Google review link on receipts and confirmation messages, and a name or tag worth posting on social media.

## How and When Should You Ask for Reviews?

Ask after a good moment: a finished appointment, a pickup, a thanks from the customer. Send a direct link to your Google review page by text or email, or print a QR code. BrightLocal's report gives a figure that 83% of people asked to leave a review went on to leave one, but the same report gives a different figure (65%) elsewhere, so use asking as a habit, not a forecast.

Google's policy allows merchants to encourage genuine reviews as long as they do not offer incentives or try to influence the rating or content. Our guide to [automating review requests](/blog/how-to-automate-google-review-requests) shows how to do it without breaking those rules.

## What Are You Not Allowed to Do?

Google's Maps policy says fake engagement is removed, that reviews paid for directly or in kind are prohibited, and that merchants may not offer incentives for posting a review or for removing a negative one. It also forbids discouraging negative reviews or selectively soliciting positive ones, and pressuring users to review while on the premises.

The FTC's consumer reviews rule took effect on October 21, 2024. Its Q&A says you cannot suggest that reviews must be positive, that incentives are allowed only when they are not tied to a particular sentiment, and that it bars false accusations against reviewers and intimidation to change reviews. Courts can impose civil penalties for knowing violations, and the page gives no dollar amount.

## Should You Run a Referral Program?

Referral rewards are separate from reviews. Rewarding a friend who brings a friend to buy is common, but never tie a reward to a review. Keep the offer simple: a credit for both people after the new customer's first paid visit.

Cost the offer before launching. Our [referral marketing guide](/blog/referral-marketing-guide) has templates, and the [coffee shop loyalty ideas](/blog/coffee-shop-loyalty-program-ideas-without-an-app) show how small rewards can work without an app.

## A Worked Example: What a Referral Reward Costs

All inputs are **assumptions** for a salon.

| Input (assumption) | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Average first-visit price | $80 |
| Gross margin on that visit | 50% |
| Reward for referrer and friend | $10 credit each |
| Cost of the program per referral | $20 |
| Margin on first visit | $40 |

The first visit leaves $40 of margin and costs $20 in credits, so the program returns $20 per referral before repeat visits. If the margin were 20% ($16), the same reward would lose $4. Check your margin before choosing the reward.

## FAQ

### Is word-of-mouth marketing free?

Not completely. Time, staff training, review links and any rewards have a cost. Rewards for referrals must be costed per customer.

### Can I give a discount for a Google review?

Google's policy says merchants may not offer incentives for posting a review. Do not tie any reward to reviews.

### How many reviews do I need?

No verified target exists. Compare your count and rating with the three nearest rivals on Google Maps and aim to stay current.

### How do I track word of mouth?

Add a "How did you hear about us?" field to booking and ask at the till. Review the answers monthly.
