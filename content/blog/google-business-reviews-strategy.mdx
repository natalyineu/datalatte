## Why Do Google Reviews Matter for Local Shops?

Local searchers read reviews before they choose a business. The [OneEveryMap article on multi-location social media](https://oneverymap.com/insights/what-is-multi-location-social-media-management) reports, citing a BrightLocal study, that 71% of consumers use Google to read local reviews. For a small shop, the review profile works like a second storefront that people see before they reach the door.

Reviews also act as a feedback loop. Each comment shows what customers praised or complained about, which helps you fix a slow checkout or an unclear price list, not just watch the star average. A steady flow of recent reviews is more useful than a large pile of old ones, because it shows the business is still active and still being used.

## What Are the Ground Rules for Asking?

Ask every customer the same way. Inviting only the people you expect to praise you distorts your profile, and it is a pattern to avoid. Pick one point in the visit, use the same wording each time, and let the customer decide.

Do not offer a discount, free item or prize in exchange for a review. Keep loyalty rewards in a separate program. Platform rules on incentives change, so check Google's current contributor policy on its help pages before you launch anything involving a reward.

Fake reviews are a separate problem. [GatherUp's pricing page](https://gatherup.com/pricing/) states that no provider can guarantee fake reviews are removed, and that the review platform makes the final removal decision. Plan to reply to unfair reviews rather than relying on removal.

## How Do You Build the Habit in Four Steps?

Most review gaps come from nobody asking, not from unhappy customers. A routine turns asking into something that happens on every visit, without relying on memory.

1. Create your Google review link from your Business Profile, then make a QR code from it. Print it on receipts and add it to follow-up texts.
2. Ask at a fixed point, such as checkout, with the same script every time. Ask everyone, not only the happy-looking customers.
3. Reply to every review within a set window, in two or more personal sentences that mention what the customer said.
4. Run a weekly check: count new reviews, list unanswered ones, and note any you want to flag. GrowDVM describes [weekly campaign reviews](https://www.growdvm.com/services-google-ads) as a standard part of its client work, and the same weekly rhythm suits reviews.

## How Should You Reply to Reviews?

Replies are public, so write them for the next customer reading the page. Name the service, thank the customer for specific detail, and keep complaints short. A reply that acknowledges the problem and says what changed works better than a generic thank-you.

Questions are different from reviews. A [DMLY guide to Business Profile automation](https://dmly.io/google-business-profile-automation-guide/) notes that the Q&A section under a listing is not one of the five Reputation tabs, so you answer customer questions directly on Google rather than through a tool's review inbox.

Keep a simple reply log, even in a spreadsheet. Note the date, the reviewer's concern and the fix. Over a few months, the log shows which complaints repeat, which is more useful for running the shop than any star rating.

## What If a Review Is Fake or Unfair?

You can flag a review that you believe breaks Google's rules, but you should expect a decision rather than a guaranteed removal. Gather what you have first: the booking record, the staff note or the message thread that shows the review does not match a real visit.

Batch your flags. Google's help says a single one-time appeal in the Reviews Management Tool can cover up to 10 reviews, so one well-documented appeal can address several reviews at once. Reply to the flagged reviews calmly while the appeal is pending, and avoid arguing in public.

Read the [Google help page on reporting inappropriate reviews](https://support.google.com/business/answer/4596773?hl=en-GB) before you submit. The same steps apply whichever review tool you use.

## Is a Review Tool Worth It? A Worked Example

All inputs below are **assumptions** for illustration. Replace them with your own figures.

Assumption: a group of three salons, each with one location, needs review requests and replies across all three sites. Assumption: the average ticket is $60.

| Option | Calculation | Monthly | Yearly |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| GatherUp Multiple Locations | 3 × $60 | $180 | $2,160 |
| GatherUp Reveal Review Defense | 3 × $45 | $135 | $1,620 |
| Difference | $180 minus $135 | $45 | $540 |

The cheaper plan may cover different features, so check the scope before you switch. Break-even is simple: at $180 a month, the group needs three extra $60 bookings a month to cover the cost of the Multiple Locations plan (assumption: the bookings come from better reviews). If your group books fewer than three extra visits a month, a free routine with a QR code and a weekly check may be enough.

Before you pay for any tool, check whether it shows real data. The DMLY guide notes that Reputation tabs show sample data until a Google Business Profile is connected, so connect a live profile during the trial.

## FAQ

### Can I offer a discount for a review?

No. Keep review requests and loyalty rewards separate. Check Google's current contributor policy on its help pages before you launch any incentive.

### Can a provider remove fake reviews for me?

Not with a guarantee. GatherUp states that no provider can promise that responsibly, and the review platform makes the final removal decision.

### How many reviews can one appeal cover?

In the Reviews Management Tool, a single one-time appeal can cover up to 10 reviews, so batch your flags with the evidence for each.

### Should I test a review tool before buying?

Yes. Connect a live Google Business Profile during the trial, because tabs can show sample data until a profile is attached.
