A plastic surgery practice should start with three things: a complete Google Business Profile with real patient reviews, a small Google Search test on branded and procedure terms, and ads and photos that follow the ASPS Code of Ethics. The code prohibits atypical results shown without disclosure, guarantees, and unverifiable "best surgeon" claims. Paid social comes later, because patient photos also raise privacy questions.

## What Do the ASPS Rules Allow in Plastic Surgery Ads?

The ASPS Code of Ethics (updated January 2025) lets members advertise, but bans false, deceptive or misleading claims. For ads and websites, the practical limits are these:

- Photos of patients whose results are not typical need a clear and noticeable disclosure.
- Before-and-after photos cannot use different light, poses or photographic techniques to misrepresent results.
- Testimonials are barred if the endorser's experience is not typical, or if the endorser has not received services.
- No guarantee that satisfaction or a cure will result, and no claim of superiority unless it can be factually substantiated.
- "Board-certified specialist" claims need a board recognized by the American Board of Medical Specialties (or the Canadian equivalents the Code names).
- Paid testimonials need clear disclosure of compensation. The member must approve every ad and keep a copy for one year, and is personally responsible for violations by an agency.

The Code also lists ads that appeal primarily to fears, anxieties or emotional vulnerabilities. Those apply to members of ASPS. State medical boards may add rules, which are not covered here.

## How Does Patient Privacy Change Your Photos and Testimonials?

Patient results are the strongest content in this field and the riskiest. HIPAA's de-identification rule lists "full face photographic images and any comparable images" as an identifier. And 45 CFR 164.508 states that a covered entity must obtain an authorization for any use or disclosure of protected health information for marketing, with narrow exceptions for face-to-face communication and nominal gifts.

In practice, get signed, specific written consent for each photo or story, and keep it with the chart. Reply to online reviews without confirming that the reviewer was a patient. Ask your attorney to review your consent form, since this guide cannot say how your state applies it.

## Where Should a Plastic Surgery Practice Start: Maps, Search or Social?

Start with the places where people already look for a surgeon.

1. **Google Business Profile.** Check the name, category, address, hours, photos, procedures list and booking link. For setup, use our [Google Business Profile checklist](/blog/google-business-profile-optimization-checklist).
2. **Reviews.** BrightLocal's 2026 survey of 1,002 US adults found 97% read reviews for local businesses and 74% only care about reviews from the last three months. Keep a steady flow. Google's policy bans incentives for reviews, so do not offer discounts. See [how Google Business Profile reviews work](/blog/google-business-profile-reviews).
3. **A procedure page per service.** One page each for rhinoplasty, breast augmentation and the like, with who it suits, recovery, risks and a consult link. The ASPS Code bars omitting facts the public ought reasonably to know before choosing a surgeon.
4. **Search ads.** Add them after steps 1 to 3 are working. See the test below.
5. **Paid social.** Add last, once your consent and compliance process is set.

## What Do Google Ads Cost for Surgeons?

LocaliQ's 2026 benchmarks show a closest-match row called Physicians and Surgeons: $4.76 per click, a 6.61% click-through rate, a 12.43% conversion rate and $40.04 per lead. The all-industry average is $5.42 per click and $66.69 per lead. No plastic-surgery-only row exists, and elective procedures can differ widely, so use these as a rough range.

Health is also a sensitive interest category in Google's personalized advertising policy. Advertiser-curated audiences such as Customer Match and remarketing lists are not supported there, and users under 18 are not eligible for personalized ads. Plan for search campaigns built on keywords and location, not on patient lists.

## How Do You Run a $500 Search Test?

**Worked example (all inputs are assumptions):** a $500 monthly budget on Google Search with your name and a few procedure terms plus your city. Use the Physicians and Surgeons benchmark as a guide, since branded terms may cost less.

| Input | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Monthly budget | $500 |
| Click cost (benchmark) | $4.76 |
| Clicks | about 105 |
| Conversion rate (call or form) | 12.43% |
| Leads | about 13 |
| Cost per lead | about $40 |
| Share of leads that book a consult (assumption) | 25% |
| Booked consults | about 3 |

Set a bar before you start: at least 3 booked consults from the $500. If you meet it, add a second campaign for procedure and "near me" terms. If not, check the landing page, call tracking and your Google Business Profile before raising spend. A lead here means a call or form, not a patient, and benchmarks are averages, not promises.

## Can You Advertise Procedures on Meta?

Meta's Advertising Standards say ads promoting cosmetic products, procedures or surgeries must be targeted to people at least 18 years old, and prohibit content implying or trying to generate negative self-perception. Meta's Health and Wellness section does not mention before-and-after images, and third-party sites disagree on the current rules, so check the Transparency Center before you build creative.

Because Meta's rules and the ASPS Code differ, run each ad past both. A photo that Meta allows can still breach the Code if the result is atypical and not disclosed.

## What Should You Track?

Track four numbers monthly: calls and forms from Google Business Profile, search clicks and leads from ads, consults booked, and cases booked. Ask front desk staff to tag the source on each inquiry.

Compare marketing cost per consult with revenue per case. No source used here publishes average plastic surgery fees, so use your own numbers. For comparison with a neighboring specialty, see [marketing for cosmetic dentists](/blog/marketing-for-cosmetic-dentists).

## FAQ

### What is the best way to market a plastic surgery practice?

Start with a complete Google Business Profile, real reviews and procedure pages. Then run a small search campaign. Check every claim against the ASPS Code of Ethics.

### Can I use before-and-after photos in ads?

The ASPS Code permits them if the lighting, poses and technique are consistent, atypical results are clearly disclosed, and the patient has given written consent. HIPAA treats full-face photos as identifiers. Confirm your state rules with an attorney.

### Can I say I am board certified?

Only if the board is recognized by the American Board of Medical Specialties or the Canadian bodies named in the ASPS Code.

### How much should I spend on Google Ads?

There is no standard figure. The worked example above uses a $500 test with a 3-consult bar. LocaliQ's Physicians and Surgeons row shows $4.76 per click on average.

### Can I offer a discount for a Google review?

No. Google's contribution policy bans incentives for reviews. The FTC rule also bans paying for reviews conditioned on sentiment.

### Do fake reviews hurt a practice?

They can, and Google lets you report ones that break policy. See our guide on [dealing with fake Google reviews](/blog/how-to-deal-with-fake-google-reviews).
