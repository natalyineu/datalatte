## Which platform should a salon start with?

Start with the platform your clients already use, then check it against your own analytics. Pew Research Center's [2026 social media fact sheet](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/fact-sheet/social-media/) surveyed 5,511 US adults between January 30 and June 18, 2026, with an overall margin of error of plus or minus 1.8 percentage points. Its headline usage figures are below.

| Platform | US adults who use it (Pew 2026) | Local salon use |
|---|---|---|
| YouTube | 84% | Repost clips and search-friendly tutorials |
| Facebook | 70% | Event and booking-slot posts |
| Instagram | 51% | Primary home for Reels and before-and-after work |
| TikTok | 37% | Test for trend-led colour and styling |

These are national usage rates, not reach figures for a single salon. The fact sheet does not give age breakdowns in the figures cited here, so a specific age split should not be assumed. Check follower ages in each app before choosing a second platform.

A simple eight-week test keeps the choice grounded in evidence:

1. Week 1: pick one platform and note current follower ages from its analytics.
2. Weeks 2 to 8: post three clips a week using the formats below.
3. Week 8: count traced bookings per platform and keep the stronger one.
4. Only then, add a second platform.

## What video formats work for a local salon?

Pick formats that answer a booking question, not formats that only chase trends. Four repeatable ones cover most salon work:

1. Before and after: one transformation per clip, with the service name and starting price in the caption.
2. Stylist tip: a short note on home care for the colour or cut just finished.
3. Open-slot reminder: a specific day, time and service, with the booking link in the bio.
4. Process clip: hands, tools and mixing, so people who do not want to be on camera can still post.

Keep each video focused on one service. Clips are usually shot vertically, and the same file can be adapted for several apps. ## How do I fit video into one hour a week?

Batch the work into one block so filming does not interrupt client appointments. The worked example below uses assumed inputs: a solo stylist with 5 working days and a phone.

- Assumption: 15 minutes to film 3 clips during services, with client permission.
- Assumption: 30 minutes to trim, caption and add the service name and price.
- Assumption: 15 minutes to schedule posts and reply to comments.

That gives 3 posts a week, or about 12 in a month. Captions are usually the slowest step, so a reusable template saves time. The guide to [ChatGPT prompts for local business marketing](/blog/chatgpt-prompts-for-local-business-marketing) includes caption prompts that can be adapted to a salon's services. Written or verbal consent should be obtained before posting any client's face or identifiable hair.

## How do I know if video is booking appointments?

Measure bookings, not views. Track three numbers each week: link clicks from the bio, new bookings that mention a video, and profile visits. Add a "How did you hear about us?" question to the booking form so the answer is recorded at the point of sale.

Use a simple decision rule. After 12 posts, if at least one booking can be traced to video, continue. If none can, change the call to action before changing platforms. Name a day, time and price instead of writing "book now." A clip with thousands of views and no bookings has not produced a result for the business.

Worked example with assumed inputs: 12 posts produce 3 traced bookings at an assumed $90 average ticket. That is $270 of revenue for about 4 hours of work, or roughly $67 an hour before product and time costs. Replace the $90 with the salon's own average ticket to get a realistic figure.

## Should I pay to promote salon videos?

Only after a video has worked organically. Meta's help centre recommends A/B testing when measuring changes to advertising. Each version is shown to a separate segment of the audience, so nobody sees both. The same guidance warns against informal testing, such as switching ad sets on and off by hand, because that can overlap audiences and produce unreliable results. Using the same budget for both versions keeps the comparison fair, as set out in [Meta's A/B testing guide](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/1738164643098669).

Set a hypothesis before starting, for example: "a before-and-after clip with a price in the caption will get more booking requests than a process clip." Then compare cost per result across the two versions. Once one version wins, a second audience can be tested. For paid channels beyond social, the [Google Ads service for local businesses](/services/google-ads) covers people who are already searching.

Worked example with assumed inputs: an assumed $60 is spent on one proven video, which receives 2 booking requests. That is $30 per request. If only 1 request arrives, the cost is $60 per request, and the boost should stop while a new call to action is tested.

## FAQ

### Do I need to appear on camera?

No. Film hands, tools, the mirror and the finished result. Many salon videos never show a face, and the process clip format is built for that.

### How often should a salon post video?

Three posts a week is a workable target for one hour of effort. Consistency matters more than volume, so choose a number that can be kept up for 12 weeks.

### Is Instagram or TikTok better for bookings?

Pew's 2026 data shows Instagram used by 51% of US adults and TikTok by 37%. Neither figure is salon-specific, so test both on a small scale and judge them on traced bookings.

### How long until video brings bookings?

There is no reliable benchmark in the sources for this. Results should be judged after about 12 posts, using traced bookings rather than views.
