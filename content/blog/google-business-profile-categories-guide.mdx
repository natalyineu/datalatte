One field on your Google Business Profile, the primary category, was ranked the number one factor for local pack and Maps rankings in the 2026 Whitespark Local Search Ranking Factors study, with a score of 227. That study is a survey of local search experts, not a Google statement, so treat it as informed opinion. Google itself says only that the categories you choose "affect your local ranking on Google." Either way, it is a free field that takes half an hour to get right, and a lot of small businesses have it wrong.

This guide is for coffee shops, salons, groomers, studios and other local businesses in the US, UK, Australia and Canada. It covers how to choose a primary category, how many extras to add, how to check competitors, which mistakes risk a suspension, and how to judge whether a change helped.

A note on the category list before we start. Google does not publish one tidy, official list of every category for you to download. You see the options in the category field of your profile as you type, and the list varies by country and language. So this article gives examples of the kind of wording to look for, not a definitive list. Always confirm the exact name in your own dropdown.

## What Google Actually Says About Categories

Three pages from Google do most of the work, and they are short:

- **Choose specific.** Google's [help page on adding or editing your category](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7249669) says to pick a specific category from the list that shows up, and gives "Nail salon" rather than "Salon" as its example. If the specific one you want is not available, pick a general category that describes the business.
- **You cannot invent one.** The same page says you cannot create your own category.
- **Do not tag every product.** For additional categories, Google says to use them for special departments or services and not to select a category for every product or service.
- **Keep it small and honest.** The [guidelines for representing your business](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177) say to use as few categories as possible to describe your overall core business, to be as specific as possible but representative of your main business, and not to use categories solely as keywords.

Notice what is missing: Google's pages do not give a number for how many additional categories you can add. Many SEO sites say one primary plus up to nine additional, but that figure is second-hand and sources disagree on it, so check your own dashboard. Whatever the cap, Google's instruction is "as few as possible."

Google's page on [how local results are ranked](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091) says results are mainly based on relevance, distance and prominence, and that complete, detailed business info helps with relevance. It lists "what kind of business you are" as part of that info. Categories are one of the cheapest ways to tell Google that.

## Step 1: Write Down How Customers Search

Customers do not search with your preferred words. Start from their phrases:

- Coffee shop: "coffee shop near me", "cafe", "espresso bar"
- Salon: "hair salon", "balayage", "barber"
- Groomer: "dog groomer", "mobile pet grooming"
- Studio: "yoga studio", "pilates studio", "personal trainer"

Now type each of those into the category field of your profile and see what Google suggests. The suggestions are the real, current vocabulary for your country. Screenshot them.

## Step 2: Check What the Competitors Use

Search your main phrase in Google Maps on a phone, standing in or near your service area. Open the top three to five profiles. The category shown next to the star rating is generally the primary category. Secondary categories are less visible in Maps itself; third-party tools and browser extensions (for example from local SEO vendors) can show them. Tool features and pricing change, so check what is currently offered before paying for anything.

Use what you find as a sanity check, not a copy-and-paste. If all five of them use a category you had never considered and it truly fits your business, add it. If they list "Wedding venue" and you are a café that has never hosted a wedding, do not.

## Step 3: Pick the Primary Category

Choose the most specific option that matches how you make most of your money. Ask: if the window of my shop could carry only one label, which would bring in the right customers?

| Your business | Wording to look for in the dropdown | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Independent café | "Coffee shop" or "Cafe" | Pick the one that matches how you trade |
| Hair studio | "Hair salon" | More specific than a general beauty option |
| Men's cuts | "Barber shop" | Matches men's grooming searches |
| Dog grooming shop | A grooming category, rather than a pet store one | Grooming is the main revenue |
| Pilates space | "Pilates studio" | Specific beats a general gym label |

These names are examples of the wording type, not a quote of Google's list. Names differ between countries and change over time, so choose from what your dropdown offers.

If you hesitate between two, run both through the search test in Step 1. The one that matches more of your real customers' phrases wins.

## Step 4: Add Secondary Categories Only for Real Services

Add an extra category only if all three are true:

1. You provide that service regularly.
2. A customer could book or buy it from you today.
3. It appears in your dropdown.

Examples of when this is fair: a coffee shop with a serious bakery counter might add a bakery category; a studio that runs both yoga and pilates might make one primary and the other secondary; a groomer with a van might add a mobile grooming category. Examples of when it is not: a hair salon that sold two gift hampers last Christmas adding "Gift shop".

Aim for a small handful, not the maximum. Remember Google's wording: extras are for departments or services, not for every product.

## Step 5: Avoid the Mistakes That Cause Trouble

- **Adding categories you do not offer** to chase searches. It wastes the customer's time and conflicts with Google's guidance to represent your business as it really is.
- **Going too broad** when a specific option exists. Google's own "Nail salon, not Salon" example is this exact mistake.
- **Stuffing keywords into the business name** to make up for a weak category. The guidelines say the name should be your real-world name as used consistently on your storefront and website, and that extras such as taglines are not permitted and can lead to suspension.
- **Changing the primary category every month.** Make a considered change, then leave it alone for several weeks so you can measure it.
- **Changing it without checking what disappears.** Some profile features depend on the category and country. If a button disappears after a change, you may have moved to a category that does not support it. Practitioners report this, but Google does not publish a page mapping categories to features, so test before and after.

## Step 6: Make the Rest of the Profile Match

Categories work with the other signals, not instead of them.

- **Services:** list the specific services under each category, with short descriptions and prices where you can.
- **Attributes:** tick only the ones that are true (online appointments, wheelchair accessible entrance, dog-friendly).
- **Website:** make sure there is a page for each major service that uses the same words your category does. Our guide to local SEO for pet groomers shows this alignment for one niche.
- **Other listings:** keep Apple Maps, Bing Places and Yelp consistent with the same category logic.

## Step 7: Measure the Change

Before editing, write down three numbers from your profile's performance report: searches, calls, and direction requests over the last 28 days. Then:

1. Change one thing only (the primary category, or the extras, not both plus the name).
2. Save a note of the date.
3. Wait four to six weeks (a rule of thumb, not a Google figure), then compare the same 28-day window.
4. Check how you show up for your top three phrases from a phone in your area.

Seasonality will muddy results. A café comparing July to January will see changes that have nothing to do with categories, so compare against the same period a year before if you have it.

## The Math

Is a category fix worth thirty minutes? It depends on how many people see your profile and what a customer is worth. Every number in this table is an **assumption** for illustration; replace it with your own from the performance report.

| | No change | Modest gain | Strong gain |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Profile views per month (assumption) | 2,000 | 2,200 (+10%) | 2,500 (+25%) |
| Share who call, book or ask for directions (assumption) | 5% | 5% | 5% |
| Actions per month | 100 | 110 | 125 |
| Share of actions who become paying customers (assumption) | 30% | 30% | 30% |
| New customers per month | 30 | 33 | 37.5 |
| Average first-visit value (assumption) | $45 | $45 | $45 |
| Monthly revenue from profile | $1,350 | $1,485 | $1,687 |
| Extra revenue per month | $0 | $135 | $337 |
| Extra revenue per year | $0 | $1,620 | $4,050 |

The +10% and +25% lifts are not benchmarks. There is no reliable published average lift from a category change, and the change could just as easily be zero or negative if you move away from a category that was working. The point of the table is the shape of the decision: the cost is thirty minutes, the downside is manageable if you record your before numbers, and even the modest case pays more than it costs. A wrong category choice on a profile that already gets 2,000 views a month is quietly expensive.

## Do This Today

1. Write down the five phrases you would want a stranger to type to find you, then type each into your profile's category field and screenshot the suggestions.
2. Open Google Maps on your phone, search your main phrase, and note the primary category of the top three profiles.
3. Record your last 28 days of searches, calls and direction requests from the profile's performance report.
4. Set the most specific accurate primary category, remove any extras you cannot honestly deliver today, and add up to a small handful of real extras. Write the date down.
5. Put a calendar reminder for six weeks from now to compare the same numbers, and another for six months to re-check your categories.

## FAQ

**Q: How many categories should I choose?**
A: One primary plus as few extras as describe your real core business. Google's guidelines say to use as few categories as possible. Many SEO sources say the maximum is nine extras, but Google's help pages do not state a number, so check your own account.

**Q: Can I create my own category?**
A: No. Google's help page says you cannot create your own category. If the specific one you want is missing, choose a general category that describes the business and add detail in the description and services.

**Q: Do categories really affect ranking?**
A: Google says the categories you choose affect your local ranking. The Whitespark 2026 expert survey ranks the primary category as the top local pack factor. Distance, reviews and the rest of your profile still matter.

**Q: Will changing my primary category hurt my results?**
A: It can change which searches you appear for, sometimes for a while. Change it only if the new one describes your business better, change only one thing at a time, and compare against the numbers you recorded first.

**Q: Why can't I find the category I want?**
A: The list varies by country and language and is updated by Google. Try different wording in the field, pick the closest accurate option, and describe the rest in your services.
