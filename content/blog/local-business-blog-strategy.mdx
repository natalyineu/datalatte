A local business blog strategy works when each post answers one question a real customer asks, using details only you can give: your prices, your service area and your process. Start from customer questions, not keyword lists. Structure each post so the answer comes first, keep prices and hours current, and measure the calls and enquiries that follow.

Writing style matters too. ## Does a Local Business Need a Blog?

Not every local business needs one. A blog pays off when customers ask questions before they buy, such as what a service costs, how long it takes or what to prepare. It does little when nobody has time to keep it accurate. Ten current posts beat fifty thin ones.

Service pages should still carry your hours, services and reviews. A blog adds pages that match the questions those pages leave open. ## Which Topics Should a Local Business Blog Cover?

Pull topics from questions customers already ask: phone calls, emails, chat messages and the questions people post about your business. Group them into three types:

- Cost and process questions, such as price ranges and the steps involved.
- Local questions, such as local rules, seasons and neighbourhoods you know well.
- Decision questions, such as which option suits a situation and what to ask a provider.

Seasonal questions suit a planned calendar, which is covered in the [holiday marketing strategy for local business](/blog/holiday-marketing-strategy-local-business). Community stories can also become articles. The [guide to local press coverage](/blog/how-to-get-local-press-coverage-for-your-small-business) explains how to pitch them to local outlets. Community material is worth using because the Strong Automotive research cited above links it to higher engagement.

## How Should Each Post Be Structured?

Answer the question in the first paragraph, then add the detail. Use headings a reader would type, such as "How much does a bathroom refit cost?" Keep paragraphs short and write at a plain reading level. A benchmark roundup summarised by Adwave reports that landing copy at a 5th to 7th grade reading level converts at 11.1%, against 5.3% for college-level copy.

Formatting helps as well. Expert analysis cited by Adwave says bullet points can raise conversion rates by 20-30% compared with paragraph-only formats. Use a list for steps, requirements or what is included, and keep prose for reasons and context.

Specific information also prevents lost sales. A Nielsen Norman study cited by Adwave attributes 20% of unsuccessful online sales to incomplete or unclear product information. For a service, state what is included, what is not, and the typical price range.

## Should You Use AI to Draft Posts?

A draft can save time, but the finished post still needs your own facts. Add your real prices, photos and local detail, and check every number against a source before you publish. A post that could describe any town in the country adds little for a local customer.

Review each draft for claims that sound confident but cannot be traced, such as statistics with no source. Remove anything you cannot verify. Replace it with your own experience, such as how long a job took last month or what a typical quote included. Publish fewer, better posts rather than many generic ones, and keep a note of which sections were drafted with a tool and which you checked, so you know what to update later.

## How Often Should You Publish?

Choose a rate you can keep for a year. A practical starting point for many small teams is one post every two to four weeks. That is a planning suggestion, not a rule, and a smaller steady schedule beats a burst that stops after a month.

Plan the next six topics in a calendar, and review each older post twice a year for changed prices, hours and rules. Update the date only when the content has really changed. ## How Do You Get New Posts in Front of Customers?

Publishing is only half the job. Email gives you a direct route to people who already know you. Adwave's promotional email structure has four parts: subject, hook, offer and action. For a blog post, the offer becomes a useful answer, and the action should be one link.

Segment your list. Adwave suggests five simple segments: recent customers, frequent buyers, people who browsed but did not buy, inactive subscribers and local customers. Local customers are often the right audience for a post about your area or season.

Follow-up also matters for enquiries. Annuitas Group, cited by Gleantap, states that nurtured leads produce a 20% increase in sales opportunities compared with non-nurtured leads. Treat readers who asked a question as leads to follow up, not just visitors. Short social posts can point back to the article, and the [Instagram growth strategy for local business](/blog/instagram-growth-strategy-local-business-0-to-1000-followers) covers building that audience.

## What Does a Blog Cost in Time? A Worked Example

Every input below is an **assumption** for a solo owner. None is a sourced figure.

| Task | Hours per post (assumption) |
| --- | --- |
| Pick the question and gather facts | 1.0 |
| Write the draft | 2.0 |
| Check numbers and add photos | 1.0 |
| Publish and link internally | 0.5 |
| Total | 4.5 |

At one post a month, that is 4.5 hours a month, or 54 hours for 12 posts. At an assumed $40 an hour, the time is worth about $2,160 a year. Next, assume each new customer from the blog is worth $300 over a year (assumption). The blog then needs 7.2 customers to cover its time cost, so 8 customers covers it. If the blog brings in none, change the topics rather than the schedule.

## How Do You Know If the Blog Works?

Track three numbers for each post. First, impressions and clicks for its main question, from the performance report in Google Search Console. Second, calls or form fills that followed a visit. Third, customers who mention the article when they call. Log the third number by hand in a notes file, because it rarely appears in any report.

Allow a few weeks before judging a post. Keep posts that bring calls, rewrite posts that earn impressions but few clicks, and merge posts that answer the same question. ## FAQ

### How long should a local blog post be?

Write as long as the question needs. Answer it in the first paragraph, then add the detail that makes it useful.

### Will a blog help my Google Maps ranking?

A blog answers questions your profile and service pages leave open, which can bring people to your site. Keep your Business Profile complete alongside it, since the profile covers hours and services and the blog answers questions.

### Is AI-written content a problem?

A draft is fine if you add your own facts, prices and photos and check every figure. Posts that could describe any business add little for a local customer.

### What should I do first if I have little time?

Write one post answering the question customers ask most often, with your real price range, and link to it from the relevant service page.

### How long until a post brings enquiries?

Allow a few weeks before judging a post. Track calls and form fills from the first month, then decide whether to keep, rewrite or merge it.
