A local business blog works when each post answers one question that real customers ask, with details only you can give: your prices, your area, your process. Write for people first. Google says it has no preferred word count, that trust is the most important part of E-E-A-T, and that content people find useful affects search presence more than any other tip in its SEO guide.

This guide gives a four-step plan: choose topics, structure each post, keep posts current, and measure them. Time estimates are labelled as assumptions.

## Does a Local Business Need a Blog?

Not always. A blog helps if customers ask questions before buying, such as what a service costs, how long it takes or what to do first. It does not help if you have no time to keep it accurate. Ten useful, current posts beat fifty thin ones.

Your Business Profile and service pages come first. They carry hours, services and reviews. A blog adds pages that match questions those pages do not answer. Our guide to the [Business Profile checklist](/blog/google-business-profile-optimization-checklist) covers the basics to finish before you write.

## What Should a Local Business Blog About?

Pick topics from real customer questions. Look at your phone, email and chat messages, the Questions section of your profile, and the search terms report if you run ads. Group them into three types:

- Cost and process questions: price ranges, steps, how long it takes.
- Local questions: rules, seasons, neighbourhoods, events you actually know about.
- Decision questions: which option to choose, what to ask a provider.

Google's helpful-content guidance asks whether a page offers original information and clearly shows first-hand expertise. A post on pricing from your own quotes meets that test. A generic list copied from other sites does not.

## How Should Each Post Be Structured?

Put the answer in the first paragraph, then the detail. Use headings that are questions the reader would type. Keep paragraphs short. Add one table or checklist if the topic suits it.

Google's SEO guide advises descriptive URLs, readable and well-organized writing, and link text that tells readers and Google what the linked page is about. Link each post to the service page it supports, and end with one next step, such as a call or a booking link. For wording ideas see [how to write website copy for a local business](/blog/how-to-write-website-copy-local-business-examples).

## Can You Use AI to Write Posts?

Google does not ban generative AI. It says these tools can help with research and to add structure to original content, but using them to generate many pages without adding value may violate its spam policy on scaled content abuse. It also says outputs can contain errors and should be fact-checked, including titles, descriptions and structured data.

For a small blog that means: draft with a tool if you like, then add your own prices, photos and local detail, check every number against a source, and publish fewer, better posts. Google suggests considering a note on how content was created.

## How Often Should You Publish?

No source gives a required frequency. Choose a rate you can keep. A common small business plan is one post every two to four weeks; that is a practical suggestion, not a Google rule. Consistency matters because Google says some changes take hours and others several months, and suggests waiting a few weeks before judging a change.

Set a calendar of the next six topics, and review old posts twice a year for changed prices, hours and rules. Update the date only when the content really changed.

## What Does a Blog Cost in Time? A Worked Example

All figures are **assumptions** for a solo owner.

| Task | Hours per post |
| --- | --- |
| Pick the question and gather facts | 1.0 |
| Write the draft | 2.0 |
| Check numbers and add photos | 1.0 |
| Publish and link internally | 0.5 |
| Total | 4.5 |

At one post a month, that is 4.5 hours a month, or 54 hours a year for 12 posts. If you value your time at $40 an hour, assumed, that is about $2,160 a year. Compare it with what one extra customer is worth to you. If a customer is worth $300 over a year and the blog brings in eight, it pays; if it brings none, change the topics rather than the schedule.

## How Do You Know If the Blog Works?

Track three numbers per post in Google Search Console and your phone log: impressions and clicks for the post's main question, calls or form fills that followed a visit, and customers who say they read it. Search Console's performance report is free.

Give each post at least a few weeks before judging, as Google advises. Keep posts that bring calls, rewrite ones with impressions but no clicks, and merge posts that answer the same question. ## FAQ

### How long should a local blog post be?

Google says it has no target word count. Write as long as the question needs.

### Will a blog improve my Google Maps ranking?

Google says local rankings rely mainly on relevance, distance and prominence, and prominence includes links to your website and reviews. A blog can earn links and answer queries, but it is not a direct map ranking lever.

### Is AI-written content penalised?

Google says generative AI is not banned, but mass-producing pages without adding value may violate spam policy. Fact-check everything.

### What should I do first if I have no time?

Write one post answering the question customers ask most often, with your real price range, and link it from the relevant service page.

### How long until a post ranks?

Google says some changes take hours and others several months. Wait a few weeks before judging.
