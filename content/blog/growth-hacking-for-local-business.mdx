For a local business, growth hacking means running small, cheap, measurable tests on the channels customers already use. The best-documented starting points are a complete Google Business Profile, a steady flow of genuine reviews, a referral offer, an email or SMS list you own, a local partnership, and one tracked paid test. Each tactic below shows the source rule it has to follow.

No published study ranks these tactics by return for local shops, so this guide does not claim one. It gives a way to test them with your own numbers. Market figures are from BrightLocal's 2026 US survey. The worked example uses assumptions.

## Tactic 1: Is Your Google Business Profile Complete?

Start here because it is free and Google documents it. Google says local ranking is based mainly on relevance, distance and prominence, and recommends verifying the business, keeping information accurate, choosing categories, adding photos and videos, and responding to reviews. It also says there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking.

Check hours, special hours, categories (one primary and up to nine more), phone number and booking link. Our [Business Profile checklist](/blog/google-business-profile-optimization-checklist) lists the fields, and the [Maps ranking factors guide](/blog/google-maps-ranking-factors-what-moves-needle-2026) shows what experts rate highest.

## Tactic 2: How Do You Get More Reviews Legally?

BrightLocal's survey found 97% of consumers read reviews for local businesses, 68% want at least 4 stars, and 74% only consider reviews from the last three months. So you need a steady flow, not a one-time burst.

Google lets you share a link or QR code that opens the review form. The rules are strict. Google says offering discounts, free items or other perks in exchange for reviews is fake and misleading content. In the US, the FTC's 2024 final rule bans buying reviews and offering incentives conditioned on a particular sentiment, as well as undisclosed insider reviews and review suppression. Ask everyone, at the moment they are happiest, and never pay for or write reviews yourself.

## Tactic 3: Can a Referral Offer Work for a Small Shop?

A referral offer gives an existing customer a reason to bring a friend, for example a credit for both after the friend's first visit. No source checked here measures referral conversion for local shops, so test it.

Keep it separate from reviews. A reward for a referral is fine; a reward for a review is not. Give each customer a unique code or a named referrer so you can count results. Our [loyalty program guide for coffee shops](/blog/coffee-shop-loyalty-program-ideas-without-an-app) shows one way to run a simple scheme without an app.

## Tactic 4: Why Build an Email or SMS List?

Social reach is rented; a list is yours. Collect addresses at the till, booking page and Wi-Fi sign-in, with a clear statement of what you will send. Marketing messages need consent or a legal exemption in many places, including the UK, so read your local rules first. Our [email marketing guide for fitness studios](/blog/email-marketing-fitness-studio) shows what a simple sequence looks like and applies to most appointment businesses.

Measure the list with two numbers: sign-ups per week and bookings from each send.

## Tactic 5: What Local Partnerships Cost Almost Nothing?

Swap offers with a neighbouring business whose customers match yours, such as a gym and a juice bar, or a groomer and a vet's front desk. Each business hands out a card or a code, and you count redemptions. The cost is printing. Agree dates and a cap in writing so neither side is surprised.

## Tactic 6: How Do You Run a Paid Test Without Wasting Money?

Pick one channel, one offer and one landing page, and set a fixed test budget you can afford to lose. Track calls and bookings, not clicks. Click prices vary by industry and are published in sources such as WordStream by LocaliQ, so read the category figure before you set a budget. Our [local marketing budget guide](/blog/local-marketing-budget-guide) shows how to split spend.

Stop the test on a date decided in advance, and compare cost per booking with your target.

## Worked Example: Scoring Three Tests

All numbers are **assumptions** for an imaginary salon. The point is the method.

| Test | Cost (assumption) | New bookings (assumption) | Cost per booking |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Review QR code at checkout | $20 printing | 5 | $4 |
| Partner card swap | $30 printing | 3 | $10 |
| Paid ad test | $300 | 6 | $50 |

On these made-up numbers, the QR code wins on cost per booking, but it also depends on happy customers existing already. The ad test costs more but scales with budget. Repeat each test for a second month before deciding. One month of data can mislead.

## FAQ

### What is growth hacking in plain terms?

It is running small, quick, measurable experiments to find what brings customers cheaply. For a local business, that mostly means profile, reviews, referrals and lists.

### Which tactic should a new local business try first?

A complete, verified Google Business Profile, because it is free and Google documents the steps. Then add a review request routine.

### Is it legal to pay customers for reviews?

No. Google prohibits perks in exchange for reviews, and the FTC's 2024 rule bans buying reviews and incentives conditioned on sentiment.

### How many reviews do I need?

No source gives a required number. BrightLocal found most consumers want 4 stars or more and recent reviews, so aim for steady new reviews.

### How long should I test a tactic?

Long enough to collect a meaningful count of bookings. Many shops need at least a full month, but this is a rule of thumb, not a sourced figure.
