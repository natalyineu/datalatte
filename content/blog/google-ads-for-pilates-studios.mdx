Take a $600 monthly budget. If a click costs $5, 5 in 100 clicks become an intro-class enquiry, and 1 in 4 enquiries becomes a regular client, you get 1.5 new clients a month and each one costs about $400 in ads. Those three inputs are my assumptions, not published Pilates benchmarks, and changing any of them moves the answer between roughly $125 and $1,550. This guide shows you which dials you control and how to set the campaign up so the numbers land on the good side.

It is written for studio owners in the US, UK, Australia and Canada. Dollar figures are US-based; in pounds or Australian and Canadian dollars, run the same math with your own click prices.

## Why Pilates Math Is Different

A gym can add members almost without limit. A reformer studio cannot. If you run classes of 8 on 8 reformers, every empty spot in a class is revenue you will never get back, and every full class is a ceiling. That changes how you use ads:

- **Fill the weak slots, not all slots.** Midday and late-evening classes are usually the empty ones. Advertise those times and the intro offer that points to them.
- **Cap the budget by capacity.** If your classes are already 90% full, more leads just create a waiting list. Pull the budget back or push the new clients toward the quiet slots.
- **Retention beats acquisition.** A client who stays a year is worth far more than one who buys a single intro class. Value your new clients by how long they stay, not by the first sale.

## Campaign Setup, Step by Step

### 1. Start with one Search campaign for one location

Search ads show when someone types "reformer pilates near me", so you pay to reach people who are already looking. Add other campaign types later, once Search is producing booked intro classes.

### 2. Target the real catchment area

Google lets you [target a radius around a location](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/6317) as well as cities and areas. Pull your last 50 new clients, plot where they live or work, and set the radius to cover most of them. In a dense city that is often a short trip; in a suburb it can be much wider.

### 3. Group keywords by what the person wants

Google's [match type guide](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/2497836) describes broad, phrase and exact match. On a small budget, stick to phrase and exact so you control what triggers your ads.

| Ad group | Example keywords | Landing page |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reformer | "reformer pilates near me", [reformer pilates your town] | Reformer schedule + intro offer |
| Beginner | "pilates for beginners", "first pilates class", "intro pilates" | Beginner class + what to expect |
| Specialty | "prenatal pilates", "pilates for back pain", "private pilates sessions" | One page per specialty |
| Studio search | "pilates studio near me", "pilates classes [neighborhood]" | Timetable + pricing |
| Your brand | [your studio name] | Home or booking page |

Negatives to add on day one: `free`, `youtube`, `at home`, `app`, `jobs`, `teacher training` (unless you offer it), `certification`, `equipment`, `reformer for sale`, `machine`. If you do offer teacher training, give it its own campaign and keep it away from your client ads. Our negative keywords guide shows how to apply lists at campaign level.

### 4. Write ads that match the search

Google's [responsive search ad rules](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/7684791) allow up to 15 headlines of 30 characters and up to 4 descriptions of 90 characters. Use them, and put the town, the class type and the offer in the headlines:

- `Reformer Pilates in [Town]`
- `Intro Reformer Class: [Your Offer]`
- `Small Classes, Max 8 People`
- `Beginners Welcome, Book Online`

Do not promise health outcomes you cannot back. Google's [unreliable claims policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/15936857) bars claims that entice people with an improbable result, even if it is possible, and says guaranteed results require a clear refund policy. "Fix your back pain in two weeks" is the kind of line that gets ads disapproved. "Beginner-friendly classes with a certified instructor" is safe.

### 5. Send each ad group to a matching page

Someone searching "reformer pilates classes" should land on a reformer page with the schedule, the price, one intro offer and one booking button, not your home page. Keep the form short and show a real photo of your studio, hours and a map. The [landing page best practices](/blog/landing-page-best-practices) checklist covers the layout.

## Track Calls and Bookings Before You Spend

Without tracking you are paying for clicks, not clients. Set up three conversion actions: the booking or form confirmation page, calls from the ad, and calls from the landing page.

Google's [call measurement help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/6095882) says calls can be counted as conversions either by AI analysis of recordings or by a minimum call duration you set, which filters out calls unlikely to be real enquiries. It also requires a call asset or location asset with a verified phone number and call reporting turned on. For website calls, [Google's website call conversions page](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/6095883) explains that a phone snippet swaps your number for a Google forwarding number and tracks only one number per page by default. Our conversion tracking guide and call tracking guide cover both.

Then close the loop by hand. When someone books an intro class, ask how they found you. Ad platforms count leads; only your front desk knows which ones stuck.

## Ad Scheduling: Match Ads to Booking Hours

Google lets you [limit ads to specific hours and days or adjust bids by time](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/2404244), and the Ad schedule page shows performance by day and hour. Pilates bookings often follow a pattern, such as evening planning for the next morning, but that is a hypothesis, not a rule. Check your own booking software for when sign-ups and calls actually arrive, run for four weeks, then cut hours that spend without bookings and add bid adjustments to the hours that work. If nobody answers the phone after 7 pm, do not pay for calls then.

## Retargeting: Bring Back the Schedule Browsers

Most visitors check the timetable and leave. A remarketing list lets you show them a reminder of your intro offer. Google's [remarketing reach help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/7166948) says a list needs a minimum of 100 active visitors or users within the last 30 days before ads show. A studio with a few thousand monthly visitors can clear that; a brand-new site may not. Our [remarketing setup guide](/blog/google-ads-remarketing-setup-local-businesses) walks through the steps. Keep frequency low and membership short. Someone who has not come back in a few weeks is unlikely to convert.

## Local Services Ads: Probably Not Yet

Google's 2023 announcement that [Local Services Ads expanded](https://blog.google/outreach-initiatives/small-business/local-services-ads-expands/) added five categories: education, people care, pet care, wellness and health care. It does not name fitness or Pilates. Check the category list in the sign-up flow for your country before spending time on it. For most studios, Search ads plus a strong Business Profile are the reliable route.

## The Math

Assume a $600 monthly budget and run three scenarios. Every row marked "assumption" is my estimate for illustration. Replace them with your own numbers after a month of data. Only the $2.77 to $6.17 click range comes from a published source (Webtonic's roundup).

| | Tough | Base | Strong |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Monthly budget (assumption) | $600 | $600 | $600 |
| Cost per click (assumption) | $7.00 | $5.00 | $3.50 |
| Clicks | 86 | 120 | 171 |
| Click to enquiry rate (assumption) | 3% | 5% | 8% |
| Enquiries | 2.6 | 6 | 13.7 |
| Cost per enquiry | $233 | $100 | $44 |
| Enquiry to regular client (assumption) | 15% | 25% | 35% |
| New regular clients | 0.4 | 1.5 | 4.8 |
| Ad cost per client | $1,556 | $400 | $125 |
| Monthly spend per client (assumption) | $120 | $120 | $120 |
| Months of spend to cover the ad cost | 13.0 | 3.3 | 1.0 |

How to read it: the last row is ad cost per client divided by monthly spend. It uses revenue, not profit, so real payback is slower once instructor pay, rent and booking fees are counted. The Base cost per enquiry of $100 sits above the $47 to $85 range PPCChief reports, which is deliberate for a small boutique studio. The Strong column is better than any benchmark above, so do not plan around it.

Three things follow:

- **Your break-even click price.** Maximum CPC = the most you will pay per new client x enquiry rate x client rate. If you will pay $300, at 5% and 25% your ceiling is $300 x 0.05 x 0.25 = $3.75 a click. Above that you lose money on average.
- **Your close rate is the biggest lever.** Going from 15% to 35% enquiry-to-client cuts cost per client by more than half without touching the ad account. That is a reply-speed and intro-class-experience problem.
- **Know how long clients stay.** If the average client leaves after three months, the Base scenario barely breaks even. Pull retention from your booking system before you scale spend.

Use [Keyword Planner](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/7337243) to get your local bid estimates and forecasts, which Google says are estimates affected by your bids, budget, ad quality and location targeting.

## Do This Today

1. Export your last 50 new clients with their postcode or zip code and draw your ad radius from that map.
2. Work out your numbers: average monthly spend per client, average months they stay, and the most you will pay for one new client. Use the break-even formula above.
3. Build one Search campaign for one location with the five ad groups above, phrase and exact match, and the negative list.
4. Create one landing page per ad group with one intro offer, then set up form, ad-call and landing-page-call conversions before switching ads on.
5. Set a 15-minute reply rule for every enquiry and block 20 minutes weekly to read the search terms report.

## FAQ

**Q: How much should a Pilates studio spend on Google Ads per month?**
A: There is no official figure. Work backwards: decide how many new clients you can seat, divide by your close rate, and multiply by your cost per click. Webtonic reports a fitness funnel (from an agency called Pilotium) built on $1,000 to $1,500 a month in mid-size markets, but its original is not public, so treat it as second-hand and start smaller if your classes are small.

**Q: Is Instagram better than Google for a Pilates studio?**
A: They catch different moments. Google reaches people already searching; Instagram builds familiarity before they search. Many studios run Google for same-week bookings and add Meta later. See [Meta ads](/services/meta-ads).

**Q: What is a good cost per lead for Pilates?**
A: There is no Pilates-specific published benchmark. The fitness range in Webtonic's roundup is about $25 to $85. Compare your number to what a client is worth over their time with you, not to a generic average.

**Q: Should I bid on competitor studio names?**
A: Test it with a small budget. It often brings low-quality clicks, and you must not use their trademarks in your ad text.

**Q: How long until I see bookings?**
A: Clicks start within hours and early enquiries can arrive in the first week. Give it four to six weeks, my rule of thumb rather than a Google figure, before judging, so you have enough clicks and intro-class outcomes to read.
