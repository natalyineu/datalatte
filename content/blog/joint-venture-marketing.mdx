Joint venture marketing is a shared promotion between two non-competing businesses that serve the same customers. Each partner promotes the other to its own customers under a joint plan with a clear goal and an agreed end date. Lean Marketing describes a joint venture as two parties combining resources with a complementary, non-competitive business to reach a particular goal. For a small local business the cash cost is usually low, but the arrangement only works when both sides agree on the offer, how results are counted and when it ends.

This guide covers how a joint venture differs from a partnership, the benefits and risks, how to choose a partner, a 5-step checklist, a comparison of offer types and a worked budget example with labelled assumptions.

## What is joint venture marketing for a local business?

A joint venture in marketing is a narrower idea than a business merger. [Chron's overview](https://smallbusiness.chron.com/joint-venture-marketing-30933.html) explains that the two companies keep their separate operations and focus only on a combined marketing plan designed to help both reach their goals. Nothing in the arrangement requires combining the whole business.

For a local shop, this usually looks simple. A hairstylist and a nail salon might agree to promote each other to their clients, with the aim of attracting new customers and raising profits for both. Lean Marketing uses this same example. Both businesses serve people who already visit the area for beauty services, but neither competes with the other.

Owners planning across several channels can compare the wider market context in the [Australia local marketing guide](/blog/local-marketing-australia-small-business-2026). The same joint venture logic applies in the US, UK and Canada.

## How is a joint venture different from a partnership?

Many owners use the two words interchangeably, but they differ. Lean Marketing lists four differences: purpose, formation, members and duration. A partnership aims to run the business for the long haul and earn more profit. A joint venture is built around one particular goal, so it has more flexibility and usually a shorter life.

Formation also differs. A partnership is legally bound by an agreement on its terms. A joint venture does not strictly need a written agreement, according to Lean Marketing. That is a reason to write one anyway, because a clear page protects both sides when the campaign ends.

Chron makes a related point: a partnership combines all facets of the business, while a marketing joint venture focuses on a shared plan only. For a promotion, that narrower scope is the right fit.

## What benefits can a small business expect?

Lean Marketing lists seven benefits. They include a new source of investment for a campaign, shared costs, learning about a different niche, faster entry into new markets, room for growth, access to a partner's skills and assets, and extra credibility from a well-known partner. The article presents these as potential gains, not guarantees.

For most local owners, cost sharing and credibility matter most. A campaign costs less when a partner covers part of the printing or the email send. A new business gains trust faster when a known neighbour recommends it.

The benefits depend on the partner's audience being real. Shared costs do not help if the partner's customers never see the offer, so the tracking step below matters as much as the idea.

## How do you pick the right partner?

Lean Marketing stresses that the partner should be complementary and non-competitive. Choose a business that serves the same customer for a different need, such as a yoga studio and a juice bar. Avoid direct rivals, because a rival may take customers rather than send them.

The textbook source warns about fit beyond the product. Partners need compatible management practices and cultural perspectives. It also says honesty, integrity and communication matter, and that both parties must focus on the future of the partnership rather than just immediate returns.

A practical filter: check whether the partner replies to messages within a day, keeps the standards your customers expect, and has an audience of similar size. Start with two candidates so you can compare results instead of guessing.

## What does a 5-step joint venture checklist look like?

1. Set one goal with a number and a date, such as 20 new customers in 60 days.
2. Write the offer in one sentence that both sides can repeat to customers.
3. Agree who posts, who emails, who prints materials and the date each task is due. Scheduling tools such as those compared in [the Hootsuite and Sprout Social review](/blog/hootsuite-vs-sprout-social-small-business-local-business) can help plan shared posts.
4. Put the terms in a one-page written agreement that states the goals, each party's responsibilities and the end date. Chron notes that a venture agreement should spell these out.
5. Review at 30 and 60 days. Continue, change the offer or end the arrangement at the agreed date.

Keep the review simple: redemptions per partner, revenue from those customers and any complaints. If one partner cannot share numbers, treat that as an early warning sign.

## Which offer type fits your business?

Three offer types cover most local partner deals. The table compares them on the factors that usually decide the choice. The cells are general guidance, not sourced statistics.

| Offer type | Best for | Who carries the cost | How to track it | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Discount on a shared card | Low-ticket items such as coffee or classes | Both partners, split by agreement | Unique printed code per partner | Discounting without repeat visits |
| Commission on tracked sales | Higher-value services such as consultations or repairs | Paid from the sale by the business owner | Tagged link or unique referral code | Commission rate set too high for margin |
| Free add-on from the partner | Products with low cost to give away | The partner, usually a small amount | Redemption noted at the till | Customer sees the perk as low value |

Pick one offer type for the first campaign. Changing both the offer and the tracking method in the same month makes results impossible to read.

## What does a worked budget example look like?

The inputs below are assumptions for illustration, not results from any business. Assume a cafe and a bookshop each hand out 200 printed cards. Assume 5% redeem, which gives 10 visits per side. Assume a $2 discount per visit and an average ticket of $9.

Each shop receives 10 visits worth $90 in gross sales and gives up $20 in discounts, leaving $70 of net revenue. Assume a 60% gross margin on that net revenue, which gives $42 in gross profit. Assume printing costs $20 per partner. The result is an estimated $22 gain per shop before any repeat visits.

The number that matters most is repeat visits. A one-off discount can attract trial, but the campaign only pays off when those customers return at full price. Measure return visits at day 60, using the same tracking code you used for the original offer. Revenue tracking tools such as those in [the FreshBooks and QuickBooks comparison](/blog/freshbooks-vs-quickbooks-small-business-local-business) can help you match redemptions to sales.

## What risks should you check before signing?

Chron points out that joint ventures must avoid breaking anti-trust laws. When large competitors combine forces, they can restrict competition and make it harder for smaller rivals to gain ground. A campaign between two small non-competing businesses rarely raises this issue, but it is worth checking if the partner is large.

The textbook source warns that a partner may gain know-how that lets it compete after the venture ends. For a local promotion the risk is smaller but real. Avoid sharing customer lists, and limit what each side learns about the other's customers to the agreed scope.

Chron also notes that a venture may end when its contract expires or when both sides agree to stop. Write the end date into the one-page agreement. Rules on promotions, endorsements and email marketing vary by country, and the sources used here do not cover them, so check those with a qualified adviser before launch.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a partner take my customers?

It is less likely when the partner is non-competitive. Lean Marketing describes joint ventures as complementary, so avoid businesses that sell the same product or service to the same need.

### Do I need a written contract?

Lean Marketing says a joint venture does not strictly require a written agreement. A one-page written note is still sensible, because Chron notes that the agreement should spell out goals and each party's responsibilities.

### How many partners should I start with?

Start with two. That is enough to compare results and small enough to track properly. Add more only after the first review shows what works.

### What if my partner stops doing their part?

Refer to the written agreement. Send a dated reminder about the agreed tasks, and end the arrangement at the review point if they are still not done.

### Where should I start?

List the businesses your customers visit before or after you. Choose the two that fit best, then agree one offer and one end date. A [free audit](/free-audit) can help you rank local partner options.
