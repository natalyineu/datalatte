Joint venture marketing means two non-competing businesses promote each other to their own customers, with a written offer and a way to count results. It costs little, but only works when both sides agree on the offer, the tracking and the end date.

This guide gives a 5-step plan, a worked example with clearly labelled assumptions, and the two legal rules most small owners miss: FTC disclosure and CAN-SPAM for joint emails.

## What is joint venture marketing for a local business?

The [US Chamber of Commerce describes co-marketing](https://www.uschamber.com/co/grow/marketing/what-is-co-marketing) as two separate companies collaborating on a joint promotional campaign. Its examples include affiliate links, shared distribution, co-branded content and event sponsorships. For a local shop, it usually means a shared offer, a cross-posted social post or a joint email.

Why it works: a partner's customers already trust them. Nielsen's 2015 global trust survey, as reported by the [Pakistan Advertisers Society](https://pas.org.pk/recommendations-from-friends-remain-most-credible-form-of-advertising-among-consumers/), found 83% of online respondents in 60 countries trust recommendations from friends and family. That figure is a decade old and second-hand, so use it as a direction, not a forecast.

## How do you pick the right partner?

Use the Chamber's criteria: brand identity, resources, history and consumer base. In practice, check four things:

- **Same customer, different need.** A yoga studio and a juice bar, or a groomer and a pet store.
- **No direct competition.** Two groomers will not help each other.
- **Similar standards.** A sloppy partner damages your reputation.
- **Similar audience size.** A partner with 200 followers cannot match your 5,000.

Start with two partners, not seven. You can track two, and you will see which one works.

## What is a 5-step joint venture plan?

1. **Set one goal.** Example: 20 new customers in 60 days.
2. **Write the offer.** One sentence each side can repeat, such as "Show your receipt from the other shop and get $2 off".
3. **Agree on actions.** Who posts, who emails, who prints cards, and by which date.
4. **Put it in writing.** The Chamber advises outlining the agreement in writing and considering a lawyer's review.
5. **Review at 30 and 60 days.** Share numbers. Continue, change the offer or stop.

Keep it to one page. If either side will not sign a one-page note, the partnership will not survive a slow month.

## How do you track results?

Give each partner a unique code and a tagged link. [Google Analytics requires three UTM parameters](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/1033863): `utm_source`, `utm_medium` and `utm_campaign`. For a partner link, a pattern like source = partner name, medium = referral and campaign = spring-offer works.

For in-store offers, use a printed card with the partner's name and count redemptions at the till.

Worked example (assumption, not a result): a cafe and a bookshop each hand out 200 cards. If 5% redeem, that is 10 customers per side. With a $2 discount and a $9 average ticket, each partner gives up $20 in discounts for 10 visits worth $90. Whether that is a win depends on your margin and on how many of those customers return, so measure repeat visits at day 60.

## What are the legal rules for partner promotions?

Two apply to most local campaigns.

**Disclosure.** According to the [FTC's influencer guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/disclosures-101-social-media-influencers), any financial or personal relationship with a brand must be disclosed, and free products or perks count. Put the disclosure beside the endorsement, and use clear words such as "ad" or "sponsored". The FTC says terms like "collab" alone are not clear enough.

**Email.** The [FTC's CAN-SPAM guide](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business) requires honest headers and subject lines, a valid postal address, a clear opt-out that you honour within 10 business days, and says each violating email can cost up to $53,088. If you email your list about a partner offer, you remain responsible. Do not hand your list to a partner without checking consent rules, and this is general information, not legal advice.

## Should you pay a commission or give a discount?

- **Discount:** easier to run, better for low-ticket items like coffee or classes.
- **Commission:** the partner earns a percentage of tracked sales, which suits higher-value services. The Chamber lists this as the affiliate model.
- **Free add-on:** a free sample from the partner costs the partner little and the customer sees value.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Can a partner take my customers?**

It is less likely when you pick a non-competing partner. The risk is a partner who sells the same thing, so avoid direct competitors.

**Q: How many partnerships should a small business run at once?**

Start with two and review them at 60 days. Add more only when you can track each one.

**Q: What if my partner does not do their part?**

Refer to the written agreement. If agreed posts or emails do not happen, send a dated reminder, then end the arrangement at the review point.

**Q: Do I need a contract?**

A one-page agreement is a sensible minimum, and the Chamber suggests having a lawyer look at the terms. Ask a local attorney if money or exclusivity is involved.

**Q: Do I have to tell customers about a paid partnership?**

Yes, if there is a material connection such as payment or free products. Follow the FTC guidance above.

**Q: Where do I start?**

List the five businesses your customers visit before or after you. Contact the two that fit best. A [free audit](/free-audit) can help you rank them.
