The ROPO effect (research online, purchase offline) describes shoppers who look a business up on a phone or computer and then buy in person, by phone or by booking a visit. One vendor-cited figure says [59% of consumers use Google to research a purchase they plan to make in-store or locally](https://adwave.com/resources/how-to-add-products-and-services-to-your-google-business-profile) (secondary). For a local business, the sale often happens offline, so the online part of the journey is easy to miss and easy to under-invest in.

This analysis pulls together what the available sources say about how common research-first shopping is, what shoppers want to know before they walk in, how a small business gets found and chosen, and how to measure results when the checkout is a till, a phone call or a booking made at the front desk. Most figures come from vendors and blogs that retell larger studies, so each is flagged as secondary and treated as directional.

## Key findings

- Research before buying is the normal case, not the exception: [97% of consumers search online for local businesses](https://contractormarketingpros.net/blog/best-digital-advertising-platforms-for-contractors/), per Google insights retold by an agency (secondary).
- Local searches turn into action quickly: [88% of mobile searches for local businesses result in a call or visit within one day](https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/local-marketing) (secondary).
- Google internal data, retold by a vendor, says [76% of people who run a local search visit a related business within 24 hours, and 28% of those searches end in a purchase](https://adwave.com/resources/pest-control-advertising) (secondary).
- Reviews carry weight: [87% of consumers read online reviews before choosing a local service business](https://adwave.com/resources/pest-control-advertising), per BrightLocal's 2024 survey as cited by a vendor (secondary).
- Wrong listing data costs customers: [53% say inaccurate listings drive them away](https://adwave.com/resources/hyperlocal-content-marketing), per Rio SEO as of 2025 (secondary).
- Most visibility comes from people who do not know the brand yet: [86% of Google Business Profile views come from discovery searches](https://adwave.com/resources/how-to-add-products-and-services-to-your-google-business-profile), a vendor figure with no named source (secondary).
- Store visits in Google Ads are [modeled estimates extrapolated from a sample and reported at the campaign level](https://www.meticulosity.com/blog/maximizing-store-visits-with-google-ads), not named individuals (secondary).
- Many small businesses are not present: [only 28% invest in online listings](https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/local-marketing), per LocaliQ as cited by HubSpot's blog (secondary).

## What is the ROPO effect and how does it differ from webrooming?

ROPO is the pattern of researching online and completing the purchase offline. Webrooming is the shopper-side word for the same behavior: browsing on the web, then buying in a shop. The reverse, showrooming, means inspecting a product in a store and buying it online. For a salon, groomer or coffee shop, "purchase" may be a booking confirmed by phone or a walk-in, not a product on a shelf.

A related term in the sources is Online to Offline. A Brazilian marketing agency describes it as [a journey that attracts customers online and leads them to the physical store](https://eficazmarketing.com/blog/exemplo-de-marketing-local/) (secondary), and suggests tactics such as discount coupons redeemable only in-store or pickup at the physical location. ROPO observes the behavior; Online to Offline designs for it.

The measurement problem is simple. A shopper reads reviews on a phone on Tuesday, taps a phone number on Wednesday and pays at the counter on Saturday. Web analytics sees at most one session. The till sees a sale with no origin. Closing that gap is the purpose of the rest of this guide, and the same logic runs through [footfall attribution for local stores](/blog/footfall-attribution-store-visits-local-business).

A cluster of statistics describes how quickly a local search becomes action. They repeat across blogs, usually without a link to the original study, so the repetition should not be mistaken for independent confirmation. Still, the direction is consistent: local intent is time-sensitive.

| Claim | Figure | Source type |
|---|---|---|
| Mobile searches for local businesses end in a call or visit within one day | 88% | [HubSpot blog](https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/local-marketing) (secondary) |

## What do shoppers look for before they buy in person?

The sources point to a short list of questions: is the place open, is it trustworthy, how do I get there, can I afford it and do they have what I need. Hours, address, phone and photos are exactly the fields that [Google Business Profile lets owners add](https://eficazmarketing.com/blog/exemplo-de-marketing-local/) (secondary), alongside user reviews and the service area. Local ad formats pull from the same profile: Google shows [address, hours and reviews in local campaigns from the advertiser's profile](https://bofu.ca/en/blogs/the-top-of-the-funnel/campagnes-locales-google-ads-visites-en-magasin) (secondary).

Trust signals matter before the visit:

- 75% read at least four reviews before deciding (Rio SEO, as of 2025, secondary).
- 59% expect a response within 24 hours (same source).
- 53% say inaccurate listings drive them away (same source).
- 61% of mobile searchers are more likely to contact a business with a [mobile-friendly site](https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/local-marketing) (secondary).

<BigStat value="53%" label="More than half of consumers say inaccurate listings drive them away" note="Rio SEO figure as of 2025, retold by Adwave. A wrong hour or old address can end the journey before the visit." source="Rio SEO via Adwave (secondary)" />

### Which question should be answered where?

The following table maps typical pre-visit questions to the place where the answer is most likely to be read. The mapping itself is a recommendation built from the profile fields and ad features described in the sources, not a measured ranking.

| Customer question | Best place to answer | Why this place |
|---|---|---|
| Are you open right now, and on holidays? | Google Business Profile hours, special hours | Hours appear in the profile and in local ads |
| How do I get there, where do I park? | Profile address, photos of entrance, directions button | Local campaigns optimize for directions requests |
| Can I call and get a quick answer? | Profile phone, mobile-friendly site with tap-to-call | Calls are a measured local action |
| Do you offer this service, and for how much? | Profile products and services, website price page | Descriptions allow up to 1,000 characters |
| Are you any good? | Reviews with owner replies | Four or more reviews are read before deciding |
| Is it in stock today? | Profile product listings, an updated page, social post | Listings should be updated when items are removed |
| Is there a deal for first-time customers? | Offer button, email signup, in-store coupon | Coupons redeemable in-store link online to offline |
| Can I book? | Booking link on the profile, phone number | Reduces drop-off between interest and action |

The profile's product and service entries have rules worth knowing. [Titles with promotional text such as "50% OFF" may be rejected, and listing a service such as "Free Consultation" under Products may be refused](https://adwave.com/resources/how-to-add-products-and-services-to-your-google-business-profile) (secondary). Photos of at least 1200 by 900 pixels are recommended, and [profiles with solid photo libraries often see a 30% to 50% bump in engagement](https://adwave.com/resources/how-to-add-products-and-services-to-your-google-business-profile) (vendor claim, secondary).

## How does a local business get found and chosen?

Being found and being chosen are two jobs. Found means appearing in Google Search and Maps for the searches near the business. Chosen means winning the comparison once a shopper sees three or four options side by side. The profile does both.

### Is the Google Business Profile the main lever?

The vendor figure says [86% of Google Business Profile views come from discovery searches](https://adwave.com/resources/how-to-add-products-and-services-to-your-google-business-profile), like "dresses near me", not from people who already know the brand (secondary, no named source). If that holds even roughly, a complete profile is how strangers meet the business. The remaining 14% would be people searching the name directly.

<DonutChart title="Where Google Business Profile views come from" labels="Discovery searches|Brand-name searches (remainder)" values="86|14" unit="%" centerLabel="Discovery" caption="Vendor figure, no named source (Adwave, secondary). The 14% is the arithmetic remainder, not a separate reported number." />

The same article states that for service businesses, clicks-to-call can account for [10% to 15% of all profile views](https://adwave.com/resources/how-to-add-products-and-services-to-your-google-business-profile) (secondary), and illustrates that with around 50 calls a month. That is an illustrative estimate, not a benchmark for every trade. For a deeper treatment of ranking factors, see the guide to [SEO for local service businesses](/blog/seo-for-local-service-businesses).

### Why do reviews and listing accuracy decide the choice?

Review volume and replies are signals shoppers read. With [75% reading at least four reviews](https://adwave.com/resources/hyperlocal-content-marketing) (secondary) and 59% expecting a reply within 24 hours, a profile with two reviews and silence loses to a rival with sixty and visible owner replies. Tools that automate requests are compared in the [review management software guide](/blog/review-management-software-small-business-compared).

Accuracy is the cheapest fix. Only [28% of small businesses invest in online listings](https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/local-marketing) according to LocaliQ as cited by HubSpot (secondary), which means a business that keeps its name, address, phone and hours consistent is ahead of most competitors by default. The same vendor recommends reviewing the full product and service list at least once a quarter and updating the profile whenever a service is added, a price changes or an item is removed.

<BigStat value="75%" label="Three in four consumers read at least four reviews before deciding" note="Rio SEO figure as of 2025, retold by Adwave; 59% also expect a reply within 24 hours." source="Rio SEO via Adwave (secondary)" />

### How much content does the profile need?

One vendor playbook suggests [posting a Google Business Profile update twice weekly](https://adwave.com/resources/hyperlocal-content-marketing), with three supporting posts per anchor asset (a profile update, an Instagram Reel and an email snippet) and a weekly review kept to thirty minutes (secondary). Treat that as a workload template rather than proof that the cadence wins.

A field study reported by Website That Sell, [also cited in that article](https://adwave.com/resources/hyperlocal-content-marketing), found businesses publishing 2 or more pages a month averaged 326% more traffic than those without a content strategy (secondary; method unknown). Emulent's directional benchmarks in the same article put local rankings at positions 15 to 30 in months 1 to 3, 5 to 15 in months 4 to 6, and 1 to 5 in months 7 to 12, with traffic growth moving from baseline to a 25% to 50% increase and later 100% or more.

## Which methods measure ROPO, and what does each need?

No single tool sees the whole journey. Measurement combines direct signals (calls, directions, coupon redemptions) with modeled ones (store visits) and with plain asking. The table compares the main methods on what each reports, what it needs and where it breaks.

| Method | What it reports | Data and setup needed | Budget and skills | Main limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Google Business Profile insights | Calls, direction requests, website clicks | Verified, complete profile | Free; basic admin skill | Counts actions, not sales |
| Call tracking numbers | Calls by source, sometimes recordings | Tracking numbers on site, profile and ads | Low to mid monthly fee; some setup | Number changes must stay consistent with listings |
| Store Visit Reports in Google Ads | Modeled visits from ad clicks | Linked, verified Business Profile; campaign and click thresholds | Ad spend; ad account skills | Modeled, campaign level only |
| Promo codes and in-store coupons | Redemptions tied to a channel | Unique code per channel; staff at the till | Near zero; staff training | Misses shoppers who do not use the code |
| "How did you hear about us" at checkout | Self-reported source | A prompt on the form, booking system or till | Free; consistency | Memory bias, simple categories |
| GA4 events and UTM links | Online steps (taps, clicks, forms) | Tag and event setup | Free; analytics skill | Cannot see the offline sale |
| Incrementality test (pause or geo test) | Whether sales moved when ads moved | Clear before and after periods | Patience, enough volume | Hard with low volume and seasonality |

On Google's side, a local campaign [automates ads optimized for directions, phone calls and physical visits measured with location data](https://bofu.ca/en/blogs/the-top-of-the-funnel/campagnes-locales-google-ads-visites-en-magasin) (secondary), across Search, Maps, Display and YouTube. The vendor description adds that Google adjusts bids and placements to maximize visits.

For comparing call tracking tools, see [call tracking for small businesses compared](/blog/call-tracking-small-business-compared). For online-side event setup, see [Google Analytics 4 for small business](/blog/google-analytics-4-for-small-business). The distinction between clicks and sessions, which often causes misreads in these reports, is covered in [sessions vs clicks](/blog/sessions-vs-clicks-whats-the-difference).

## How do store-visit conversions work, and where do they mislead?

Store visits are not counted at the door. One agency guide explains that [store visits are modeled estimates extrapolated from a sample and reported at the campaign level, never as named individuals](https://www.meticulosity.com/blog/maximizing-store-visits-with-google-ads) (secondary). Setup requires linking the Business Profile and verifying every location before Google can attribute modeled visits.

There is also a visibility threshold. The same page says Google only shows the metric when a location and campaign [clear its privacy and click thresholds](https://www.meticulosity.com/blog/maximizing-store-visits-with-google-ads), without giving values. A single-site groomer with a small ad budget may see no store-visit data at all, which is a limit of the tool, not a sign that nobody visits.

The vendor says Google's local campaign guidance recommends [tracking visits and conversions with Store Visit Reports](https://bofu.ca/en/blogs/the-top-of-the-funnel/campagnes-locales-google-ads-visites-en-magasin) (secondary). That is useful when volume suffices. When it does not, the lower-tech methods in the table (codes, a checkout question, call tracking) do the same job with less machinery.

Common pitfalls:

- Counting profile clicks as customers; many callers never book.
- Changing the phone number on one listing but not the others, which breaks consistency.
- Treating a modeled visit figure as an exact count.
- Running a promo code that staff forget to ask about.
- Judging a month in isolation when the purchase cycle is weeks long.

A broader view of how these pieces fit together appears in the guides to [advertising effectiveness measurement for small businesses](/blog/advertising-effectiveness-measurement-small-business) and [cross-channel attribution](/blog/cross-channel-attribution-tv-local-business).

## Which method fits which budget?

The sources do not price a ROPO measurement stack, so tiers below are an assumption: the order is sensible, the dollar bands are not sourced. For spending context in another trade, one vendor says a single-location pest control company [should expect to spend $1,500 to $5,000 per month on Google Ads](https://adwave.com/resources/pest-control-advertising) depending on competitiveness (secondary), which shows how wide ad budgets can run.

| If the budget is (assumption) | Use | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Time only, no spend | Complete Business Profile, review replies, checkout question, in-store code | Free signals cover the main ROPO questions |
| Small monthly fee | Add call tracking numbers and UTM links on the site | Separates profile, site and ad calls |
| Modest ad spend | Add Local Services Ads or search ads and watch profile actions | Pay per lead where available; see below |
| Larger ad spend with several locations | Add local campaigns and Store Visit Reports | Needs enough volume to clear thresholds |
| Any budget, after three months of data | Run a pause or geo test | Shows whether ads add visits beyond baseline |

On lead pricing, Local Service Ads [operate on a pay-per-lead model](https://contractormarketingpros.net/blog/best-digital-advertising-platforms-for-contractors/), where the advertiser pays when a customer makes direct contact (secondary), and the article lists eligible industries such as electricians, HVAC, plumbers and locksmiths as of 2026; pet grooming is not on that list. Pest control leads are quoted at [$15 to $50 each versus $5 to $20 per click for standard search ads](https://adwave.com/resources/pest-control-advertising) (WordStream 2024, secondary). Those prices belong to home-service trades and should not be carried over to a salon or café.

The same vendor suggests a [modest mix of three to five channels](https://adwave.com/resources/pest-control-advertising), with budget shifting toward what is working, and adding connected TV or lead marketplaces only after 60 to 90 days if existing channels produce more leads than the business can handle (secondary). Budget planning in general is covered in the [local marketing budget guide](/blog/local-marketing-budget-guide) and Google's automated formats in the [Performance Max guide for local business](/blog/google-performance-max-for-local-business-complete-guide-2026).

## What does a ROPO playbook look like by business type?

The playbooks below are recommendations assembled from the profile features, review behavior and measurement methods above. They are not tested results.

### Coffee shop

Hours and holiday hours matter most, because "open now" searches are the likeliest trigger. Keep photos of the entrance and interior current, answer reviews within a day and post a profile update twice weekly with a seasonal drink or a weekday offer. Measure with direction requests, a counter code on a loyalty offer and a weekly tally of new customers who mention Google.

### Salon

Services and prices are the research content. List each service with a short description in the profile, add a booking link and photos of finished work. Ask every new client how they found the salon at booking, and compare that tally with calls and booking-link clicks. First-visit offers can be redeemed at the chair, keeping the sale offline and the code traceable.

### Pet groomer

The decision is trust: owners read reviews about how pets were treated. Photos of clean facilities and calm dogs, review replies and clear service lists by size or coat type answer the first questions. Calls are likely to be the main action, so call tracking is more informative than store-visit modeling. The worked example below sizes this.

### Fitness studio

Prospects often compare several studios and want schedule, price and a trial. List class types and trial offers in the products and services fields, add a trial booking link and use an in-person trial pass with a unique code. Track trial sign-ups by source, then trial-to-member conversion at the front desk.

### Startup with a physical launch

Without reviews or history, trust gaps are widest. Verify the profile before opening, collect early reviews from launch-week visitors and ask for consent-based email signups with a launch offer. The Brazilian agency article says email lists [must come from customers providing their own address, not from bought or scraped lists](https://eficazmarketing.com/blog/exemplo-de-marketing-local/) (secondary). Test one paid channel only after the profile and review basics are in place; a testing routine is described in [A/B testing for local business](/blog/a-b-testing-for-local-business).

## What does a worked example look like for a pet groomer?

This model uses invented inputs, all labelled as assumption, to show how the measurement fits together. It is not a forecast and no input is a benchmark.

Inputs (assumption):

- Profile views per month: 1,000 at baseline, 1,500 after three months of profile work.
- Share of views that become calls: 8% at both points.
- Share of calls that book: 40% at baseline, 45% after faster callbacks.
- Share of views that tap directions: 4%; of those, 25% book or walk in.
- Share of views that click to the website: 5%; of those, 20% book.
- Average ticket: $70 per visit.

Baseline calculation (assumption): 80 calls, 32 bookings, $2,240 from calls; 40 direction taps, 10 visits, $700; 50 website clicks, 10 bookings, $700. Total monthly revenue traced to the profile: $3,640.

After three months (assumption): 120 calls, 54 bookings, $3,780; 60 direction taps, 15 visits, $1,050; 75 website clicks, 15 bookings, $1,050. Total: $5,880.

## What is a practical 90-day plan?

The plan sequences free fixes first, then measurement, then optional spend. It assumes a single-location business with no tracking in place.

| Days | Focus | Actions | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1-7 | Audit | Verify the profile; compare name, address, phone and hours across listings; note current reviews and rating | Clean listing baseline |
| 8-14 | Profile completion | Add services with descriptions, photos of at least 1200 by 900 pixels, booking link, special hours | Complete profile |
| 15-30 | Review habit | Ask each satisfied customer for a review; reply to all reviews within 24 hours | Review count and reply rate |
| 15-30 | Measurement basics | Add a "how did you hear about us" prompt; set up a unique in-store code; add UTM links to profile and site | First source tallies |
| 31-45 | Phone tracking | Add call tracking numbers on the site and ads, keep the main number on listings | Calls by source |
| 31-60 | Content cadence | Two profile updates a week; short weekly review limited to thirty minutes | Posting rhythm |
| 46-60 | GA4 events | Track phone taps, direction taps and booking clicks as events | Online step data |
| 61-75 | Optional paid test | One ad channel with a clear call or directions goal; link the profile for store-visit data if volume allows | First paid results |
| 76-90 | Review and decide | Compare sources, cost per booked customer and rebooking; keep, change or stop channels | Next-quarter plan |

For pausing decisions, use the criteria in [when to pause an ad campaign](/blog/when-to-pause-ad-campaign-local-business).

## Which metrics belong on the tracker?

Emulent's framework, as cited by Adwave, recommends tracking [about 7 metrics per location](https://adwave.com/resources/hyperlocal-content-marketing) (secondary). A set that matches the ROPO journey:

| Metric | Source | Cadence |
|---|---|---|
| Profile views, split discovery and brand searches | Profile insights | Monthly |
| Calls from profile and site | Profile insights and call tracking | Weekly |
| Direction requests | Profile insights | Weekly |
| Website clicks and booking-link clicks | Profile insights, GA4 events | Weekly |
| Review count, rating and reply time | Profile and review tool | Weekly |
| Code redemptions and "how did you hear" tally | Till, booking system | Weekly |
| Booked customers and rebooking rate | Booking system | Monthly |

Add modeled store visits only where thresholds are met. Keep a note field for events, since local happenings change patterns; one vendor playbook suggests [increasing TV activity in the 14 days around a neighborhood event](https://adwave.com/resources/hyperlocal-content-marketing) (secondary), and any such change should be logged to explain spikes. For video and streaming channels that feed the same journey, see [how to measure CTV ads for local business](/blog/how-to-measure-ctv-ads-local-business) and [view-through vs click-through conversions](/blog/view-through-vs-click-through-conversions-local-business). ## What this guide cannot tell you

- No source here gives a verified, independent US rate for research-online-buy-offline across all shoppers. The most quoted figures are retold from Google or BrightLocal without links to original studies.
- Several statistics (78%, 88%, 76%) overlap and conflict in definition; the 28% purchase figure suggests that the higher ones describe actions broader than a sale.
- Vendor claims such as 86% of profile views from discovery searches and a 30% to 50% engagement lift from photos come without named studies.
- Industry numbers on lead prices, cost per click and return on ad spend come from pest control and contractor trades, and may not apply to cafés, salons or studios.
- The 2024 NRF and Prosper data covers a holiday weekend and says nothing about ordinary weeks.
- Store-visit modeling needs thresholds the sources do not quantify, so a small business cannot know in advance whether it will see data.
- The worked example uses assumed inputs; it shows method, not expected results.

## FAQ

### Is ROPO the same as webrooming?

Nearly. Both describe researching online and buying in person. ROPO is often used by marketers measuring the journey, while webrooming describes shopper behavior. In services, the "purchase" may be a booking by phone or an appointment kept at the premises.

### How many shoppers research online before buying locally?

The sources give different figures with different bases: 97% search online for local businesses and 59% use Google to research purchases planned in-store or locally, both retold by vendors (secondary). No source here gives one audited rate, so a local check using a checkout question is more reliable.

### Can a small business see store visits?

Only in modeled form. Google Ads reports store visits as estimates extrapolated from a sample at the campaign level, and it requires a linked, verified profile and enough volume to pass privacy and click thresholds (agency guide, secondary). Calls, directions, codes and checkout questions work at any size.

### What is the cheapest way to start measuring?

Complete the Google Business Profile, read its call and direction counts weekly, ask new customers how they found the business and use one unique in-store code. These cost staff time rather than money and cover the main ROPO signals.

### Do reviews really affect offline sales?

The sources show that reviews are read before choosing: 87% read reviews before choosing a local service business and 75% read at least four (secondary). They do not isolate a sales effect, so tracking calls and bookings before and after a review push is the local test.

### How often should the profile be updated?

One vendor suggests profile updates twice weekly and a full product and service review at least quarterly, plus an update whenever a service, price or availability changes. These are recommendations, not proven optimal rates.

Measurement of this kind is easier with a second pair of eyes on the setup. A [free audit](/free-audit) reviews profile, tracking and call data, and the [analytics service](/services/analytics) covers building the full dashboard.
