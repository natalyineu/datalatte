Meta Ads can work for a local business when you run a small, specific test, target the area you actually serve, and measure cost per lead instead of reach. Facebook and Instagram are both widely used: according to a 2026 Pew Research Center survey, 70% of US adults use Facebook and 51% use Instagram ([Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/fact-sheet/social-media/)). Reach is not the goal, though. The goal is a phone call, a booking, or a visit from someone who lives close enough to come in. This guide covers platform choice, setup, a worked budget example, measurement, and how to test without wasting money.

## Which platforms do local customers use?

Pew's survey covers all US adults, not local shoppers, so it tells you where people spend time, not which platform will bring in your customers. Facebook still has the widest reach of the four platforms in the table above. Instagram is growing, and WhatsApp is growing slightly too. TikTok is flat at 37%. Reddit reached 25% in the same survey ([Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/fact-sheet/social-media/)).

Start with Facebook and Instagram because they share one ad manager, so you can run both placements from a single campaign. For a side-by-side look at the two, read [Instagram Ads vs Facebook Ads](/blog/instagram-ads-vs-facebook-ads). If you are weighing TikTok, compare it using the [TikTok vs Facebook ads guide for local businesses](/blog/tiktok-ads-vs-facebook-ads-which-is-better-for-local-business) before adding a third channel.

## How do you set up your first Meta ad campaign?

You need a Meta Business account, a Facebook Page for your business, and an ad account connected to both. Work through the steps below in order. Skipping the tracking step is the most common mistake, because you will spend money without knowing what it produced.

1. Connect your Facebook Page and create an ad account in Meta Ads Manager.
2. Pick one objective that matches a measurable action, such as calls, messages, or leads, rather than awareness.
3. Draw a radius around your service area. Start with the area you can realistically serve in a day, not a regional circle.
4. Write one specific offer, such as a booking discount for first-time customers, so people have a reason to act now.
5. Set up tracking before launch: a call-tracking number, a dedicated landing page, or a unique promo code for each ad.
6. Set one budget for the test and keep it the same for each version you compare.
7. Check the campaign after the first few days, but avoid changing targeting or creative until you have enough results to read.

Meta recommends running tests inside Ads Manager or the Experiments tool rather than switching ad sets on and off by hand. Its help pages state that informal testing "can lead to inefficient ad delivery and unreliable test results" and can create overlapping audiences ([Meta for Business Help Center](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/1738164643098669)).

## How much should a local test budget be?

Meta's help pages do not give a recommended minimum budget, so any figure here is your own planning number. The useful question is what a test has to produce to be worth continuing. The example below uses assumptions you should replace with your own numbers.

Assumptions for a hypothetical plumbing business:

- Monthly test budget: $300 (assumption)
- Average cost per click: $1.50 (assumption)
- Landing page conversion rate to a lead: 3% (assumption)
- Average job value: $400 (assumption)
- Share of leads that become paying customers: 25% (assumption)

Calculation: $300 ÷ $1.50 = 200 clicks. 200 × 3% = 6 leads. Cost per lead = $300 ÷ 6 = $50. Customers = 6 × 25% = 1.5. Revenue = 1.5 × $400 = $600. Return on ad spend = $600 ÷ $300 = 2.0.

If your own numbers come out lower, the test has not failed yet. It tells you which number to improve: the click cost, the landing page, or the close rate. For a broader look at spend levels, see the [Facebook ads budget guide for local businesses](/blog/facebook-ads-budget-local-business-how-much-to-spend).

For context on click costs, WordStream's industry benchmarks report an average Google Ads search cost per click of $2.69 across industries, with Legal at $6.75 and Dating and Personals at $2.78 ([WordStream](https://www.wordstream.com/blog/ws/2016/02/29/google-adwords-industry-benchmarks)). These are Google Search figures from a 2016 update, so treat them only as a rough reference for how click costs vary by industry, not as a Meta benchmark.

## How do you measure whether Meta ads are working?

Track three numbers: cost per lead, cost per customer, and return on ad spend. Cost per lead is your ad spend divided by leads from that ad. Cost per customer divides spend by customers who came from those leads. Return on ad spend divides revenue from those customers by spend. If you cannot tie a lead or sale to a specific ad, the numbers will be guesses.

For offline businesses, use one of three methods. Give each ad its own call-tracking number, use a unique promo code, or ask every new customer how they found you and record the answer in your booking system. Any of these lets you connect a visit or call back to the ad that produced it.

Watch for ad fatigue as well. When the same audience sees the same creative for weeks, cost per result usually rises. Rotate new images or offers when results drop, and keep a log of what changed so you can tell which change caused the result.

## Should you run one ad or several?

Run two versions with one variable changed, such as the image, the headline, or the audience. Keep the budget the same for both so the comparison is fair. Meta's A/B testing documentation says that each version is shown to a separate segment of the audience so no one sees both, and that the test then determines which performs best ([Meta for Business Help Center](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/1738164643098669)). Change one variable at a time. Changing the offer, the audience, and the photo together means you will not know which one caused the difference.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a good first test for a local business on Meta?

A good first test is one offer, one radius, and one landing page or phone number, run at a fixed budget for a set period. Pick a radius you can serve within a day, and measure cost per lead. Keep the setup simple enough that you can explain the results in a single sentence.

### Do I need to post organically for ads to work?

No. Paid ads run through the ad manager and do not depend on your regular posts. Recent, relevant content on your Page can make the business look more established to people who click through, but it is not required for the ads to be delivered.

### Can I target people near a competitor?

Meta's targeting options are based on location and interests, not competitor names. You can target a radius around your own area and interests related to your service. Do not name a competitor in the ad copy, because that can break Meta's advertising rules.

### What should I do if a test shows no results?

Check tracking first, since a broken phone number or landing page can make a working ad look like a failure. Then check the audience size, the offer, and the landing page in that order. Change one thing and run the test again before deciding the channel does not fit.

### How long should a test run before I decide?

Run it long enough to collect a reasonable number of leads, not a fixed number of days. If the test is producing very few clicks, the budget or radius may be too small for a meaningful read. Set the minimum number of leads you need before you start, so the decision does not depend on how the results look on a given day.
