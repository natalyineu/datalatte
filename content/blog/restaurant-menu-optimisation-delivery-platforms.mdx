To optimise your menu on delivery platforms, build a smaller menu made of dishes that travel well, price it for commission rather than dine-in, show every hero item with a clear photo and an honest description, and review your sales data monthly. These four changes usually do more for margin than any promotion.

The advice below applies to Uber Eats, DoorDash, Deliveroo, Just Eat, Grubhub and similar marketplaces. Features and fees differ by country and plan, so check your own dashboard for specifics.

## Know what delivery really costs you

Before touching the menu, understand your margin. Marketplace commissions typically range from the mid-teens to around 30 percent of the order value depending on the plan, with extra charges for marketing features and payment processing. Customers also pay delivery and service fees, which can affect how they perceive your prices.

A quick margin check for each item:

1. Food cost (ingredients and packaging)
2. Platform commission and payment fees
3. Labour for preparation and packing
4. Any promotion you fund

What remains is your real contribution per dish. Items that look profitable in the dining room often are not once packaging and commission are included. Our guide to [delivery platform fees explained](/blog/delivery-platform-fees-explained) breaks down the typical charges.

## Step 1: Cut the menu down to what travels

A delivery menu is not your restaurant menu. Food sits in a bag, then a car or on a bike, then on a doorstep. Review each dish and ask:

- Does it hold heat, or stay crisp, for 20 to 30 minutes?
- Does it survive being tilted, stacked and carried?
- Can it be packed without leaking or steaming itself soggy?
- Is it quick to prepare at peak times?

Remove or modify dishes that fail. Fries in a sealed box, delicate salads dressed in advance and soufflés rarely arrive well. Ordering behaviour supports a tighter list: customers scrolling on a phone tend to choose faster from a shorter menu, though the exact number varies by cuisine. As a starting point, many restaurants do well with roughly 20 to 40 items, grouped sensibly.

## Step 2: Structure categories for scanning

Platforms show your menu as a long scroll. Make it easy:

- Put your best sellers and highest-margin items at the top, in a category such as "Popular" or "Chef's picks".
- Use short, clear category names: Starters, Mains, Sides, Drinks, Desserts.
- Keep one category for bundles or meal deals.
- Avoid dozens of tiny categories that force constant scrolling.

Check how the menu appears on a phone, since most orders come from mobile.

## Step 3: Write item names and descriptions that sell

Each item needs a clear name and a short, honest description:

- Lead with the main ingredient and cooking method: "Slow-roasted lamb shoulder with garlic yoghurt and flatbread".
- Mention allergens and dietary tags (vegetarian, vegan, gluten-free) where the platform lets you, and be accurate. Allergen information is a legal and safety matter in many countries.
- State portion size if it is unclear ("serves 2 to 3").
- Avoid vague hype. Words like "amazing" do not help a customer decide.

Keep descriptions to one or two lines, since long text tends to be skipped.

## Step 4: Photos that work on a small screen

Items with a good photo usually attract more attention than those without, and most platforms encourage photos for every dish. You do not need a studio:

- Use natural light near a window and a plain background.
- Shoot from a slightly raised angle so the food fills the frame.
- Photograph the dish as it is actually packed and served, so expectations match reality.
- Refresh photos when recipes or presentation change.

Start with your top ten sellers, then expand.

## Step 5: Price deliberately

There are three common approaches:

| Approach | How it works | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| Same prices as dine-in | No difference between channels | Simple and fair | Commission eats margin |
| Modest delivery uplift | Slightly higher prices on the app | Protects margin | Customers may compare with your website |
| Platform-only bundles | Bundles designed for delivery | Raises basket size | Needs careful costing |

Some platforms and some local rules restrict price differences between channels, so read your agreement. Whatever you choose, test one change at a time and watch order volume for a few weeks before judging.

Beyond headline prices, think about basket size:

- Offer sensible add-ons at the point of order: drinks, sides, a dessert.
- Create combos that are genuinely good value but protect margin.
- Set a minimum order level if the platform allows it, so tiny orders do not cost you more than they earn.

## Step 6: Use modifiers and upsells without confusing people

Modifiers (extra cheese, choice of sauce, size) are a natural place to earn more per order. Keep them short and logical:

- Required choices first (size, base), optional extras second.
- Keep prices for extras clear.
- Do not hide important options deep in nested menus.

Test the ordering flow yourself on a phone, from opening the app to checkout.

## Step 7: Protect quality and ratings

Delivery ratings and reviews affect visibility in the platform, so operations matter:

- Use packaging suited to each dish, with vented lids for fried foods and separate containers for sauces.
- Seal bags with a tamper-evident sticker and add the order number.
- Set honest preparation times. Over-promising and delivering late is worse than a slightly longer estimate.
- Mark items as unavailable quickly when you run out.
- Reply to complaints politely and use platform refund tools where appropriate.

Track repeated complaints in a simple log, then fix the cause in the kitchen or in the packing process.

## Step 8: Review your data monthly

Platforms provide dashboards with sales by item, peak times, ratings and refund reasons. Set a monthly 30-minute review:

- Which items sell the most? Which sell almost nothing?
- Which items generate refunds or complaints?
- When are the peaks, and is the kitchen coping?
- How does your delivery sales mix compare with dine-in?

Retire or rework the bottom items, double down on the winners and adjust prices one change at a time.

## Do not forget your own channels

Marketplaces are good for discovery but expensive for repeat customers. Consider gently moving regulars to your own ordering link, for example via a website ordering tool, an insert in the delivery bag or a loyalty offer, subject to your platform's terms. ## FAQ

**Q: How many items should a delivery menu have?**
A: There is no universal number, but a focused menu of dishes that travel well, often a few dozen items at most, tends to convert better and is easier to run than the full dine-in menu.

**Q: Should I charge more on delivery apps than in the restaurant?**
A: Many restaurants add a modest uplift to offset commission. Check your platform agreement and local rules, keep the difference reasonable and monitor orders after any change.

**Q: Are professional photos necessary?**
A: Not necessarily. Clear, well-lit phone photos of the dishes as actually served are usually enough, and are better than no photos at all.

**Q: Which metrics matter most?**
A: Item-level sales, contribution margin after commission, refund rate, average rating and average order value.
