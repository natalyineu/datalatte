Take a $16 burger that costs you $5 in ingredients and $2 in kitchen labour. In your dining room it earns $9. Sell the same burger on a 25% commission delivery plan, add $0.60 of packaging, and it earns $4.40. On a 30% plan it earns $3.60. The figures for food cost, labour and packaging are my assumptions, but the commission rates are published: DoorDash lists 15%, 25% and 30% plans and Uber Eats lists 20%, 25% and 30%. That gap is what menu optimisation on delivery platforms is for.

This guide is for independent restaurants, cafes and takeaways in the US, UK, Australia and Canada. Fees and features vary by country and contract. Rates quoted here come from the platforms' own pricing pages; where no official rate card was available, the article says so and points you to a written quote. If you are in continental Europe, our comparison of [Glovo vs Wolt for restaurants](/blog/glovo-vs-wolt-which-is-better) covers those two platforms' contracts in detail.

## Start With the Fees You Actually Pay

Before touching a menu, know your plan.

What the platforms' own pricing pages say:

- **DoorDash** ([merchant pricing](https://merchants.doordash.com/en-us/pricing)): Basic is 15% delivery commission, Plus 25% and Premier 30%, with 6% on pickup in all three. Commissions are a percentage of the order subtotal, with no monthly or activation fees. Online Ordering is commission-free but carries a payment processing fee, and the page does not state the amount.
- **Uber Eats** ([US merchant pricing](https://merchants.ubereats.com/us/en/pricing/)): Lite is 20% (15% for delivery in some cities such as Washington DC, Seattle and Los Angeles), Plus 25% after a 30-day intro, and Premium 30% after the intro. Pickup is 7% only if you can show in-app prices match in-store prices; otherwise it is 10%. A Webshop carries a 2.5% order processing fee plus $0.29 per order. Pricing applies only in the US.
- **Deliveroo and Just Eat (UK) and other markets:** No official published rate card was available, and third-party blog figures disagree with each other. Ask the sales rep for a written quote that states the commission, any marketing fees, and whether VAT is added on top.

The practical point: the plan you pick sets the ceiling on your margin before you write a single menu description. Two plans on the same platform can differ by 15 points of commission, and higher tiers buy you reach and customer fee discounts, not better food economics. ## The Math

Here is the per-dish calculation. Everything except the commission rates is an **assumption** for illustration.

Assumptions: price $16, ingredients $5, kitchen labour $2, delivery packaging $0.60. In-store contribution is $16 minus $7, which is $9. The last two columns show what you earn at the same price, and the price you would need on the app to match your $9.

| Plan example | Commission | Commission on $16 | Earned per dish at $16 | Price to still earn $9 | Markup needed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| DoorDash Basic | 15% | $2.40 | $6.00 | $19.53 | 22% |
| Uber Eats Lite | 20% | $3.20 | $5.20 | $20.75 | 30% |
| DoorDash or Uber Eats Plus | 25% | $4.00 | $4.40 | $22.13 | 38% |
| DoorDash Premier or Uber Eats Premium | 30% | $4.80 | $3.60 | $23.71 | 48% |

The "price to still earn $9" is (contribution + delivery costs) divided by (1 minus commission). It ignores delivery and service fees customers pay, refunds, discounts you fund and card fees.

Read it two ways. First, a 38% markup is not realistic, so you cannot price your way out on a high-commission plan. Second, every point you shave off food cost or labour on a dish counts for more than it does in-store: on the 25% plan, cutting $0.50 of food cost adds the full $0.50 to your contribution, while a $0.50 price increase adds only $0.375 after commission.

## Step 1: Cut the Menu to Dishes That Travel

A delivery menu is not your dining-room menu. Food sits in a bag, then a car or on a bike, then on a doorstep. Test each dish:

- Does it hold heat or crispness for 20 to 30 minutes in a sealed container? (That window is my rule of thumb, not a platform figure.)
- Does it survive being tilted and stacked?
- Can it be packed without leaking or steaming itself soggy?
- Can the kitchen make it quickly at peak?

Remove or redesign anything that fails. Fries in a closed box, dressed salads and soufflés rarely arrive well. Move sauces to separate pots and give fried items a vented lid. There is no verified "ideal number of items". As a starting point, many restaurants find a menu of a few dozen items easier to run and easier to scan than a full dine-in list, but test with your own data (see Step 6).

Start your cull with the bottom 20% of dishes by sales in your platform dashboard, then anything with a high refund rate.

## Step 2: Price for Commission, Within Limits

You have three options, and the platforms are not neutral on them.

- **DoorDash** says it does not require restaurants to match in-store prices on delivery, but recommends keeping prices "as close to in-store as possible", saying its data shows menu markups can negatively impact customer conversion and retention ([DoorDash on menu pricing](https://about.doordash.com/en-us/news/menu-pricing)). It also says customers consider price a key indicator of quality. Its merchant guidance advises matching pickup prices with in-store prices to build trust and repeat orders ([DoorDash menu pricing guide](https://merchants.doordash.com/en-us/blog/how-to-optimize-your-menu-for-profit)).
- **Uber Eats** ties its lower 7% pickup rate to in-app prices matching in-store prices.
- **Your own site** is where nobody can compare you to the app.

So a sensible approach is a modest uplift on delivery only, kept small enough that you pass your own "would I pay this?" test, with pickup priced at in-store. Test one change at a time and watch order volume for three to four weeks before judging; those timings are my advice. Check local rules too: some countries require clear price display or restrict price differences between channels.

Basket size is the other lever. Bundles (a main, a side and a drink) lift average order value without a price increase on the hero item, and an add-on at checkout is nearly pure margin if the ingredient cost is low.

## Step 3: Structure the Menu for Scanning

Platforms show your menu as a long scroll on a phone. Make it fast:

- Put your best sellers and highest-contribution dishes in a "Popular" or "Our picks" category at the top.
- Use plain category names: Mains, Sides, Drinks, Desserts, Meal deals.
- Avoid dozens of tiny categories that force constant scrolling.
- Open your own listing on a phone, logged out of your merchant account, and order something. It takes five minutes and shows you what a customer sees.

## Step 4: Write Names and Descriptions That Sell and Protect

One line, main ingredient, cooking method, portion if unclear: "Slow-roasted lamb shoulder, garlic yoghurt, flatbread. Serves 2." Skip "amazing" and "delicious".

Dietary tags and allergens deserve more care than copywriting. In the UK, Food Standards Agency guidance on distance selling, as summarised in secondary sources, says the mandatory allergen information must be available before a customer completes an online order and again on delivery, and that customers should be clearly told where to find it when you sell through a third-party platform. Treat that as second-hand, because the full FSA text was not available to check, and confirm with the FSA page and your local environmental health officer. In the US, Canada and Australia, check your own food authority's rules. In every country: keep allergen data on the app accurate, and train whoever packs orders.

## Step 5: Photos on a Small Screen

DoorDash requires photos that clearly show menu items in proper lighting, without text or overlays, and its help page says photos should be in focus with a neutral background ([DoorDash photo types help](https://help.doordash.com/en-us/merchants/article/doordash-photos-types)). DoorDash's pricing page also lists a free menu photoshoot on its Basic plan, which is worth asking about before you pay a photographer.

DoorDash says menus with header images and logos often get more monthly sales than those without. It gives no study, sample size or date for that claim, so read it as the platform's own marketing, not proof. The sensible reading: a menu without photos looks unfinished next to one with them.

Practical rules for phone photos:

- Window light, a plain surface, and a slightly raised angle so the dish fills the frame.
- Photograph the dish as it is packed and served, so the bag matches the picture.
- Start with your top ten sellers, then expand.

## Step 6: Use Modifiers Carefully and Read Your Data Monthly

Modifiers (size, extras, sauces) earn margin when they are short and logical: required choices first, optional extras second, extra prices clearly shown. Test the ordering flow on your own phone from open to checkout.

Then book a 30-minute monthly review of what the dashboard shows:

- Sales by item. Which sell most, and which almost nothing?
- Refunds and complaints by item. Which dishes get remade or refunded?
- Peak times, and whether the kitchen keeps up.
- Rating trends, and what low ratings say.

Retire the bottom sellers, fix or drop high-refund dishes, and re-price one thing at a time. If you want help turning platform exports into one view, our [analytics and reporting service](/services/analytics) does exactly that.

## Do This Today

1. Find your plan and commission rate in your merchant dashboard, and write it down next to your bottom-line contribution for your top three dishes (price minus ingredients, labour, packaging and commission).
2. Remove or redesign three dishes that fail the travel test: sogginess, leaks, or slow prep at peak.

## FAQ

**Q: How many items should a delivery menu have?**
A: There is no verified number. A focused list of dishes that travel well is easier to run and scan than the full dine-in menu. Use your dashboard to cut the bottom sellers and high-refund dishes.

**Q: Should I charge more on delivery apps than in the restaurant?**
A: Modestly, if your margin needs it. DoorDash does not require price matching but recommends staying close to in-store prices, saying markups can hurt conversion and retention. Uber Eats links its lower pickup rate to price matching. Test one change at a time.

**Q: Are professional photos necessary?**
A: No. DoorDash asks for clear, well-lit photos without text overlays. A phone and window light can meet that, and DoorDash's Basic plan lists a free menu photoshoot.

**Q: Which plan should I choose?**
A: It depends on volume and your margin. DoorDash lists 15%, 25% and 30% delivery plans and Uber Eats lists 20%, 25% and 30% in the US. Higher tiers add reach and customer fee discounts. Run the math above on your own dishes before upgrading.

**Q: Which metrics matter most?**
A: Contribution per dish after commission, refund rate, average rating, average order value and sales by item.
