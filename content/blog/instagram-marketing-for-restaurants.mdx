Instagram works for restaurants when it makes people hungry and then makes booking or ordering effortless. Post real food and real people consistently, make your profile a clear path to reserve or order, and measure bookings rather than followers.

Instagram is a visual menu that people check before they choose where to eat. You do not need a studio or a big budget. You need a repeatable routine, a few good habits with a phone camera, and a profile that sends hungry people somewhere useful. Here is a practical plan.

## Set up your profile to convert

Your profile is the landing page for everyone who sees a post, so fix it first.

- **Account type**: Switch to a Professional (Business) account so you get insights, contact buttons and ad access.
- **Name field**: Include a searchable phrase, such as "Casa Verde | Italian Restaurant Leeds", not just the brand name.
- **Bio**: One line on what you serve and where, plus hours or a signature dish.
- **Link**: Point it to your booking page, online ordering or menu. A link-in-bio page works if you need several options, but keep it to three or four buttons.
- **Action buttons**: Add Reserve, Order or Call buttons if your booking provider supports them (OpenTable, Resy, SevenRooms, Toast and others integrate in some regions).
- **Highlights**: Create saved Stories for Menu, Location and Parking, Events, Reviews and Private Dining. These answer the questions people ask before they book.
- **Location**: Add your address so you appear in location searches.

## Decide what to post

A good restaurant feed mixes four types of content. Rotating them keeps people interested and avoids a wall of identical dish photos.

| Content type | Examples | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Food | Close-ups, steam, a cheese pull, plating in progress | Create appetite |
| People | Chefs, servers, regulars, the person who bakes at 5am | Build connection |
| Place | Dining room at golden hour, patio, the bar | Set expectations |
| Offers and news | New menu, events, seasonal dishes, holiday hours | Drive action |

A simple weekly rhythm might be two Reels, two feed posts and several Stories. Fewer, better posts are more sustainable than a burst you cannot maintain.

## Shoot good food photos with a phone

You rarely need professional gear. A few habits go a long way:

- Use natural light, near a window if possible, and turn off the flash.
- Wipe the plate rim and check for smudges.
- Shoot from above for flat dishes (pizza, bowls, spreads) and from a low angle for tall ones (burgers, stacks, drinks).
- Show texture: a drizzle, a cut-open dish, steam, a pour.
- Keep the background simple and avoid clutter.
- Edit lightly. Over-saturated food looks fake and sets the wrong expectation.

Keep a small backup of real photos of every menu item in a shared folder so staff can post even on a busy day.

## Use Reels to reach new people

Reels are the main way accounts reach people who do not already follow them. Short, simple videos tend to work well for restaurants:

- A 10 to 20 second prep or plating clip with natural sound
- "Day in the kitchen" or opening routine
- Making the signature dish or drink
- A quick tour of the dining room before service
- A first-bite reaction from a guest who has agreed to be filmed

Hook viewers in the first second with the most appealing visual, add captions because many people watch without sound, and finish with one clear action such as "Book via the link in bio". Our guide to [Instagram Reels for business](/blog/instagram-reels-for-business) goes into formats and editing.

## Use Stories for the daily rhythm

Stories suit what is happening today: the lunch special, a table that just opened up, a live band, a new batch out of the oven. Use stickers sparingly but purposefully: polls for menu votes, question boxes for "what should we cook next", and link stickers to your booking or ordering page. Save the best Stories to Highlights.

Reshare tagged posts from guests (with permission or through the tag-share feature) as it is free social proof.

## Make location work for you

Many people find restaurants by searching nearby. Help them:

- Tag your location on every post and Reel.
- Use a handful of relevant hashtags, such as your neighbourhood, city and cuisine, rather than long generic lists.
- Mention neighbourhood landmarks or events in captions where natural.
- Cross-check that your name, address and hours match your Google Business Profile, since customers often compare the two. See our [Google Business Profile optimization checklist](/blog/google-business-profile-optimization-checklist).

## Encourage user-generated content

Guests already photograph their food. Give them reasons and ways to tag you:

- Display your handle on menus, table cards and takeaway packaging.
- Create one photogenic dish, drink or corner that people want to share.
- Reply to comments and tags quickly and warmly.
- Run occasional low-cost activations, such as a monthly dish for the best tagged photo, and check the platform's current promotion rules before running a giveaway.

## Partner with local creators and businesses

Local food bloggers and micro-influencers (often a few thousand followers) can be more affordable and more relevant than large accounts. Offer a hosted meal in return for honest content, and ask that they disclose the arrangement as required by advertising rules in your country. Cross-promotions with a nearby coffee roaster, brewery or farm supplier also reach people who live close by.

## Consider paid promotion once the basics work

Once your profile and content are in good shape, small paid campaigns can fill quiet days. Typical restaurant uses include promoting a Reel to people within a few miles, advertising a new menu or event, and retargeting people who engaged with your account. Many local restaurants start with a small daily budget of around $5 to $15 (or the local equivalent) and judge it on bookings and orders, not likes. ## Track what matters

Instagram Insights shows reach, saves, shares, profile visits and link taps. Look at those alongside business results:

- Reservations or orders that mention Instagram (add "How did you hear about us?" to your booking flow)
- Clicks on your booking link, tracked with a UTM-tagged URL
- Calls and direction requests from your Google profile

Do a monthly review: which posts drove the most saves, shares and link taps? Make more like them. Saves and shares are usually a better sign of real interest than likes.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Posting only discounts, which trains followers to wait for deals
- Dark, blurry or heavily filtered photos
- Ignoring comments and direct messages
- No clear call to action
- Posting in bursts and then disappearing for weeks
- Buying followers, which inflates numbers and harms engagement

## FAQ

**Q: How often should a restaurant post on Instagram?**
A: Consistency matters more than volume. Many restaurants do well with two to four feed posts or Reels per week plus regular Stories, as long as they can keep it up for months.

**Q: Do I need a professional photographer?**
A: Not to start. A modern phone, natural light and a tidy plate are enough. A professional shoot for your hero dishes and dining room can still be worth doing once or twice a year.

**Q: Are hashtags still useful?**
A: Modestly. A few relevant tags for your cuisine and neighbourhood help categorise your content, but clear captions, location tags and good content matter more.

**Q: How do I turn followers into reservations?**
A: Make the next step obvious: a booking link in your bio, a link sticker in Stories, a call to action in every Reel and quick replies to direct messages asking about tables.
