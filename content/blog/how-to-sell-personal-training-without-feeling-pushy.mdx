To sell personal training without feeling pushy, run the consultation as a set of questions, not a pitch. Ask about the client's goal and past frustrations, repeat their words back, offer two or three packages that fit what they said, and agree a follow-up date if they are not ready. Fitness-industry guides agree on this order.

This article is for studio owners and independent trainers who find the sales part of the job uncomfortable. No public dataset shows close rates for personal training consultations, so there are no benchmark percentages here. The advice comes from trainer-sales guides, labelled secondary in the sources, and the numbers in the example are assumptions.

## Why Does Selling Training Feel Pushy?

It feels pushy when the price comes before the reason. A client who has not said what they want, why now and what stopped them before hears any offer as a sales line. A My PT Hub guide describes the fix as changing the question from "how do I sell more" to "how do I help more", and says consultative selling, where you listen first, gives the best openings.

That means the work happens before the price. The consultation is where the sale is decided, as the Member Solutions guide frames it, so it deserves a structure. My PT Hub's list of upselling ideas includes limited availability to create urgency. Use it only when the limit is real, such as a full class schedule.

## What Does a 6-Step Consultation Look Like?

Member Solutions' guide (April 2025) lays out six steps. The order below follows it, with notes from the canfitpro article.

1. **Build rapport.** Talk casually before business.
2. **Ask discovery questions.** Goals, past frustrations, motivation. Ask who supports the goal and how long they have wanted it.
3. **Reflect back.** Repeat what you heard in their words.
4. **Present two or three options.** Recommend one, using their words, not your opinion.
5. **Handle objections calmly.** Treat each as a problem to solve together.
6. **Guide the decision.** Walk through the pros and cons, then ask which option works better for them.

Canfitpro's author suggests an open question ("Which one of those options works better for you?") over a closed one ("Does that work for you?"). She also advises telling prospects before the visit that you will present package options, so nobody is surprised.

## How Many Packages Should You Offer?

Two or three. Both Member Solutions and My PT Hub recommend this, and My PT Hub lists "presenting more than 2-3 options" as a mistake. Canfitpro says no more than three.

Build the tiers around outcome and need, not session count. Member Solutions says to price on the outcome and experience delivered and to research local competitors without starting a race to the bottom. It also suggests payment plans for higher-priced packages. Check your own market before copying any number; no source here gives a typical price per session.

## How Do You Answer Common Objections?

Member Solutions suggests a pattern: acknowledge the concern, reframe it, then ask a question. Its sample objections and the approach:

- **Price:** acknowledge the cost, then offer a payment plan or show the per-session cost of a larger package.
- **Time:** point to efficient, scheduled sessions.
- **Fear of commitment:** explain the assessment and personal plan.
- **"I need to think about it":** ask what specifically is on their mind, since the hesitation often hides an unspoken concern.

Canfitpro argues for heading objections off earlier. If a spouse might object, ask in the consultation who will support the goal. If the client says they need to think, ask how long they have been thinking about training and what they plan to do to reach the goal. The client's answers then give you something real to refer back to.

## Should You Offer a Guarantee?

It can lower the fear of a wrong choice. The canfitpro article gives an example of a full refund if the client leaves within the first three sessions, and claims the extra sign-ups outweigh refund requests. That claim has no data attached, so treat it as one trainer's view.

Decide whether you can afford refunds before advertising one. State the terms in writing, including which sessions count and how to ask. Check your local consumer rules for gym and training contracts; they differ by state and country, and none of the sources here covers them.

## Worked Example: What Is One Consultation Worth?

All numbers are **assumptions**, not benchmarks. A studio books 10 consultations a month from its website and Google Business Profile. It sells a 12-session package at $600.

| Item | Assumption |
| --- | --- |
| Consultations booked per month | 10 |
| Close rate on the day | 30% |
| Packages sold on the day | 3 |
| Clients who agreed a follow-up date | 4 |
| Follow-up conversion | 25% |
| Extra packages from follow-up | 1 |
| Total packages | 4 |
| Revenue at $600 | $2,400 |

Following up on the 4 undecided clients, at 25%, adds one sale, or $600, which is a 33% rise over the 3 sold on the day. If the follow-up is skipped, that $600 disappears. Change the inputs to your own numbers after a month of tracking.

## FAQ

### When should I bring up packages with a new client?
Present options after the discovery questions in the same consultation. No source here supports a specific session number, so do not wait for a fixed one.

### What if the client says they need to think about it?
Ask what specifically is on their mind and agree a follow-up time before they leave. Then contact them on that date.

### Should I give a discount?
Member Solutions suggests framing a larger package's lower per-session cost as an investment discount rather than cutting your price on request. Decide your rules before the consultation.

### How many options is too many?
More than three. Two or three is the usual advice.

### Is this still pushy if I follow up?
My PT Hub lists pushing after a "no" as a mistake. A follow-up on a date the client agreed to is different from pressing on the spot.
