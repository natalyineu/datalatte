One signed line decides whether your best transformation photo is an asset or a liability: permission to use this person's picture in advertising. In New York, using someone's picture for advertising or trade without their written consent is, under Civil Rights Law section 50, a misdemeanor, and section 51 lets the person sue. In the UK, the Information Commissioner's Office (ICO) says that where you rely on consent it must be withdrawable "easily at any time". An older version of this article told salons that permission becomes permanent after a 30-day window. That is wrong in the UK and a poor policy anywhere, so this version replaces it.

This guide is for salon owners in the US, UK, Australia and Canada. It covers consent, what you can honestly claim in a before and after, who owns the photo, how to shoot consistently, and how to turn the gallery into bookings. **This is general information, not legal advice. Rules differ by country and state, so check your jurisdiction, and ask a local solicitor or attorney to review your release form.** Australia and Canada each have their own privacy and advertising rules that are not covered here.

## Step 1: Get Consent That Holds Up

### What the UK regulator says

The ICO's guidance on [what counts as valid consent](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/lawful-basis/consent/what-is-valid-consent/) is a good template even outside the UK, because it is specific. Applied to a salon:

- **Specific and informed.** The ICO says a request that is "vague, sweeping or difficult to understand" will be invalid. Name the places: your Instagram, your website, paid ads, printed material.
- **Unbundled.** Consent "should be unbundled from other terms and conditions". Do not hide photo permission inside your booking terms, and do not make it a condition of getting the haircut unless the photo is necessary for the service.
- **Withdrawable at any time.** People must be able to withdraw "easily at any time", and you should tell them how. After withdrawal, the ICO says to stop consent-based processing as soon as possible in the circumstances, while earlier processing stays lawful. In practice: take the photo down from your own channels promptly and stop using it in new ads. You cannot recall a print run that already went out, so be honest about that on the form.
- **Recorded.** You "must be able to demonstrate that someone has consented". Keep the signed form or a timestamped digital record.
- **No "evolving" consent.** If your purpose changes, you need to ask again. A yes for an Instagram post is not a yes for a bus-stop poster.
- **Children.** The ICO's under-13 rule is aimed at online services offered to children, but for a salon the simple policy is a parent or guardian signature for every minor.

The ICO notes on its page that this guidance is under review and may change, so check the current version before you finalise your form.

### What the US picture looks like

There is no single US rule. Publicity rights come from state law. In New York: [section 50](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/CVR/50) prohibits using a living person's name, portrait, picture or voice for advertising or trade without first obtaining written consent (a parent or guardian's for a minor) and makes a violation a misdemeanor, and [section 51](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/CVR/51) gives the person a civil action for an injunction and damages, with exemplary damages possible where the use was knowing. Other states' statutes were not reviewed for this guide, so do not assume yours matches. The practical rule is the same everywhere: get written permission for advertising use before you post.

### A six-part release

| Part | What to write |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Who and what | Client name, date, service, and that photos of their hair, and face if shown, may be taken |
| 2. Where | Tick-boxes: Instagram and Facebook, website, paid ads, email, in-salon print |
| 3. Face or no face | Separate option for "hair only, no face" shots |
| 4. How long and how to withdraw | "You can change your mind at any time by telling us at (contact). We will remove it from our channels promptly. We cannot recall material already printed or already delivered." |
| 5. Not a condition | "Saying no does not affect your service" |
| 6. Signature | Client (or parent or guardian for under 18s), date, and where you store it |

No 30-day window and no "permanent" wording. Keep each signed form with the photo file name so you can find and remove an image in minutes.

## Step 2: Keep the Result Honest

Consent covers the person. A separate set of rules covers what the picture claims.

**US: the FTC Endorsement Guides.** The rules at [16 CFR 255.2](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/255.2) say an endorsement is likely to be read as representing what the endorser's experience typically is. If a result is not typical, a vague disclaimer is not enough: the Guides' own example says "Results not typical" is insufficient to prevent an ad from being deceptive. An atypical result can be shown if the ad clearly explains the unusual circumstances. For a salon, a platinum correction after three earlier box-dye jobs is a great photo, but the caption should say it took, for example, two sessions and several hours. That wording is my suggestion; the FTC rule is about clear context.

**Free or discounted services.** The FTC's [Endorsement Guides Q&A](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking) says that if consumers would care that the company gave something away free or at a substantial discount, that connection must be disclosed clearly, not behind a link. If a model had a free service for the shoot, say so in the post itself.

**UK: the advertising regulator.** The ASA's [guidance on production techniques in cosmetics advertising](https://asa.org.uk/advice-online/cosmetics-the-use-of-production-techniques.html) says any "after" image should be representative of what consumers can generally obtain, that minimal retouching is generally acceptable, and that claims "should not misleadingly exaggerate" the effect. It names styling, make-up, eyelash inserts and hair extensions as techniques to watch, and cites hair ads where excessive extensions that added volume were an issue. It also says filters are not inherently problematic, but they can mislead where they affect the thing being sold. For a salon: if the after photo shows extensions, a gloss and a ring light, say so, and never put a filter on a colour result. The colour is what the client is buying.

## Step 3: Know Who Owns the Photo

The [US Copyright Office FAQ](https://www.copyright.gov/help/faq/faq-who.html) describes copyright as the right of the author of the work, or the author's heirs or assignees, and says owning a physical copy does not give you the copyright. So:

- A freelance photographer who shoots for you owns the pictures unless a written agreement says otherwise. Get a licence that covers every channel you plan to use.
- A photo a client took themselves belongs to them. A tag is not a licence to put it in an ad. See [how to get customers to post, and how to ask to repost](/blog/user-generated-content-local-business-get-customers-to-post).
- For photos taken by your own staff, check their contracts. Employment-law rules were not reviewed here, so ask your adviser.

## Step 4: Shoot So the Comparison Is Fair

None of this is legal; it is craft, and it is my recommendation rather than research. The goal is that the only difference between the two photos is your work.

- **Same spot, same light, same distance, same angle.** Mark a spot on the floor and a height on the wall. Use window light or the same lamp every time.
- **Three angles:** front, side, back. Add a close-up for colour and texture.
- **Dry and styled in both**, or wet in both. Do not compare messy "before" hair with a blow-dried "after" if the service was only a cut.
- **No filters, no skin smoothing.** It protects you under the guidance above and builds trust.
- **Shoot vertical** for stories and reels, and keep an uncropped original.
- **Log it.** File name, date, service, stylist, time taken, price range and which consent boxes were ticked.

## Step 5: Post So People Book

A gallery only makes money if the next step is obvious.

1. **Caption with facts:** service, stylist, time it took, and a price range or "from" price. Put the booking link or "book via the link in bio" in every post.
2. **Group the gallery by service** (balayage, curly cuts, colour correction, bridal) in highlights, and pin your three best to the top of your profile.
3. **Reuse on Google.** Add the same approved images to your Business Profile photos, and on your website next to the booking button. Our [trust signals guide](/blog/trust-signals-website) covers where proof belongs on the page.
4. **Match photos to the search.** Someone looking for curly-hair cuts should see curly-hair results, not a platinum blonde.
5. **Answer enquiries fast.** A good photo gets a message; a slow reply loses the booking. See [what to automate and what to keep human](/blog/ai-automation-for-hair-salons) and our Instagram bookings guide.

## Do This Today

1. Write your release using the six-part table, with no 30-day window, and have a local adviser review it.
2. Print or build a digital version that captures a signature and date, and save each one beside the photo.
3. Mark a photo spot with the same light and distance, and shoot your next five clients in three angles.
4. Review your last ten posted transformations: remove any filtered after shots and add context (sessions, time, extensions) to atypical ones.
5. Add a Book link to every post and your bio, and add your best approved photos to your Google Business Profile.

## FAQ

**Q: Do I need a signed release if the client said yes in person?**
A: Get it in writing. Verbal consent is hard to prove, and the ICO says you must be able to demonstrate consent. In New York, section 50 specifically requires written consent for advertising use. This is not legal advice, so check your own jurisdiction.

**Q: Can a client change their mind later?**
A: In the UK, yes. The ICO says withdrawal must be possible easily and at any time. Treat that as your policy everywhere: remove the photo from your channels promptly and stop using it in new ads, while noting you cannot recall material already printed.

**Q: Is it enough to crop out the face?**
A: Not automatically. Someone may still be identifiable from a tattoo, a distinctive background or an outfit, and a face-free photo still involves their hair, which they may not want shared. Offer a "hair only" tick-box and still get a signature.

**Q: Can I say "results may vary" under my photos?**
A: It helps a little, but not for a result that is genuinely unusual. The FTC's example says "Results not typical" is insufficient on its own to prevent an ad from being deceptive, and recommends clearly explaining the unusual circumstances.

**Q: Can I give a client a discount for letting me post their photo?**
A: You can offer a thank-you, but keep it separate from consent and say so on the form. If you give a model a free service, disclose it in the post, as the FTC advises.
