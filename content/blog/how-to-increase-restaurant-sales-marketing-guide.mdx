Restaurant sales rise when more nearby people find the place, choose it, and come back. Marketing supports each step: a complete Google profile so diners find the restaurant, a menu and offer that make ordering easy, regular social video, and ads timed to meal periods. Start with the free fixes, then add paid reach only after the offer is measured.

## What actually increases restaurant sales?

Sales are the product of traffic, conversion, and check size. Marketing touches all three. Local search and ads add traffic. A clear offer and a readable menu improve conversion. Menu design and pricing presentation influence check size.

The sources behind this guide cover four areas: the local search profile, menu and pricing, social and video content, and geo-targeted ads. They do not document email or SMS performance for restaurants, so no benchmark for those channels appears here. They can still be tracked through redemptions, as the table below shows.

Most vendor pages quoted here are written by an ad platform, so their claims are labeled "(secondary)". They describe what a vendor says its product does, not independent results. Where a claim comes from Google's own help documentation, it is treated as a primary description of how Google works.

A useful order of operations is to fix what is free, test one offer, then pay for reach. Skipping straight to ads means paying to send people to an incomplete profile and an unclear menu.

## How does a Google Business Profile bring in more diners?

Google states that local results are mainly based on [relevance, distance, and popularity](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091). Distance cannot be changed, but relevance and popularity can be improved. Google also says there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking, so the profile itself carries the work.

Three profile actions come straight from Google's guidance:

- Verify the business. Google says this tells it the owner is authorized to represent the business, which makes it more likely to show up in search results.
- Complete every field. Google says businesses with complete and accurate info are more likely to show up in local search results.
- Build prominence. Google defines prominence as how well-known a business is, and says it is also based on how many websites link to the business and how many reviews it has.

Reviews are the part a restaurant can influence week to week. A review request process, ideally automated, keeps the count growing. A comparison of [review management software for small businesses](/blog/review-management-software-small-business-compared) covers the tool options, and the [Google Business Profile optimization checklist](/blog/google-business-profile-optimization-checklist) walks through the fields one by one.

One caution: Google notes the in-store products feature is available only for retail businesses, so restaurants should not expect to use it.

## Which menu and pricing changes lift sales before any ad spend?

Menu work costs time rather than money. [Adwave's restaurant sales guide](https://adwave.com/resources/how-to-increase-restaurant-sales) (secondary) describes a profit and popularity matrix that sorts every menu item into four categories: Stars, Plowhorses, Puzzles, and Dogs.

The practical use is to compare what sells with what earns. Items that sell often but earn little, and items that earn well but rarely sell, are the ones worth acting on. The exercise needs the restaurant's own sales counts and food costs, which are not provided by any source here.

The same page says research shows that seeing a price as "21" instead of "$21.00" reduces the "pain of paying." No study size or figure is given, so treat it as a low-cost test, not a guaranteed lift. A simple way to test it is to change the format on one menu section and compare item sales over a few weeks against the unchanged sections.

Menu changes also feed every other tactic. A promo video needs a signature dish to feature, and an offer needs a margin to work from. Settling the menu first gives the rest of the plan firmer ground.

## Does posting on social and making video help a restaurant?

The sources support consistency and format, not a guaranteed sales figure. [Adwave's marketing tips guide](https://adwave.com/resources/restaurant-marketing-tips) (secondary), published January 21, 2026, recommends posting 3-5 times per week on Instagram and Facebook and daily on TikTok to stay relevant in algorithms.

That cadence is a lot for a team already running a kitchen. Video production is the usual bottleneck, and [Adwave's AI promo video page](https://adwave.com/resources/restaurant-promo-videos-with-ai) (secondary) addresses it. The vendor says its Wavemaker videos are typically done in two to five minutes and export in 9:16 vertical format for Instagram and TikTok. For a menu highlights video, it recommends 4-8 photos of signature dishes.

Those are vendor statements about its own tool, not test results. The sensible reading is that a short vertical clip built from existing dish photos is a low-effort way to keep the posting schedule alive.

To connect posts to sales, pair each video with a single call to action, such as a code word to mention at the counter. Views alone do not pay the rent, but code-word redemptions show whether content moves people to visit.

## When and where should restaurant ads run?

Timing is the most concrete paid-media advice in the sources. The Adwave tips guide (secondary) recommends targeting residential zones with geo-targeted ads during dinner hours of 5-8 pm and business districts around lunchtime of 11 am-2 pm.

The logic is that dinner decisions happen near home and lunch decisions happen near work. A restaurant can use this to split budget by meal period, giving each ad set its own radius and message. Search ads follow a different logic, covered in the guide to [Google Ads for restaurants](/blog/google-ads-for-restaurants).

On streaming TV, Adwave claims a restaurant can reach thousands of local viewers for as little as $30 per day, and says it can run a commercial on more than 100 premium networks (secondary). One caution: the vendor's own promo video page states "from $30 a day" in a heading but "from $50" in body text. That conflict is unresolved on the page, so ask for the current rate in writing before budgeting.

No source documents whether ad prices will rise or fall at any date, so any plan should be built on a quote, not an expectation. For background on the format, see [CTV ads for restaurants](/blog/ctv-ads-for-restaurants).

## What does a 4-week restaurant marketing plan look like?

This plan puts free fixes first, then the offer, then paid reach. It assumes one location and a small team.

### Week 1: Fix the foundation

- [ ] Verify the Google profile if it is not already verified
- [ ] Fill every profile field with accurate hours, menu link, and contact details
- [ ] Set up a simple review request after each visit
- [ ] Pull item sales and food costs for the menu matrix

### Week 2: Sort the menu and pick the offer

- [ ] Place each menu item into Stars, Plowhorses, Puzzles, or Dogs
- [ ] Test the price format without dollar signs on one menu section
- [ ] Choose one slow-hours offer and set a redemption code word
- [ ] Calculate break-even covers (see the example below)

### Week 3: Launch content and owned channels

- [ ] Shoot or collect 4-8 photos of signature dishes
- [ ] Publish 3-5 posts on Instagram and Facebook
- [ ] Send the offer to the existing email or SMS list
- [ ] Record redemptions daily

### Week 4: Add paid reach and review

- [ ] Request a written current rate before any streaming TV purchase
- [ ] Run geo-targeted ads for lunch and dinner windows separately
- [ ] Compare redemptions by channel
- [ ] Keep what paid back, cut the rest, and reuse the best clip

A fuller seasonal view is in the [local business marketing calendar](/blog/local-business-marketing-calendar-week-by-week-12-months).

## How can the return on one offer be calculated?

The example uses a coffee shop with a lunch menu and a slow window from 2 to 5 pm. Every input below is an assumption for illustration, not a benchmark.

Assumptions:

- 600 covers per week, average check $14, so weekly revenue is $8,400
- Gross margin on a full-price check is 70 percent, which is $9.80
- Offer: $2 off a lunch combo during the slow window, so the discounted check is $12 and the margin falls to $7.80
- 60 redemptions per week, of which 40 would have come anyway and 20 are new
- Optional ad spend: $100 per week

Without ads, the discount given to the 40 existing customers costs $80 (40 times $2). The 20 new covers earn $156 (20 times $7.80). Net result: +$76 per week.

With the $100 ad spend, the net result is $76 minus $100, or -$24 per week. Break-even needs total costs of $180 ($80 plus $100) covered by new covers at $7.80 each, which is 23.1, so 24 new covers a week.

The lesson is the method. Count how many redemptions are truly new, which the code word and a simple "first visit?" question at the register can help estimate. Then decide whether the ad spend is justified. ## FAQ

### What is the cheapest way to increase restaurant sales?

Completing and verifying the Google Business Profile costs nothing. Google says businesses with complete and accurate info are more likely to show up in local search results, and prominence also depends on reviews. Menu analysis and price formatting are the next free steps.

### Can a restaurant pay Google for a better local ranking?

No. Google states there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking. Ranking is based mainly on relevance, distance, and popularity. Paid ads are a separate product and do not change the local ranking factors.

### How often should a restaurant post on social media?

Adwave's guide (secondary) recommends 3-5 times per week on Instagram and Facebook and daily on TikTok. That is a vendor recommendation, not a tested requirement, so match the cadence to what the team can sustain.

### How much do local streaming TV ads cost for a restaurant?

Adwave advertises streaming TV from $30 per day, but its own page also says "from $50" in body text (secondary). Because the figures conflict, ask for the current rate in writing and confirm what the price includes.
