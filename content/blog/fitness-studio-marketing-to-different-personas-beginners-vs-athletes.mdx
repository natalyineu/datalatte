Market to beginners by making the first visit feel easy and the next class easy to book. Market to experienced athletes with programming, progress tracking and community. ## How Do Beginners and Athletes Differ as Customers?

The two groups ask different questions. A beginner wants to know whether they will fit in, keep up and afford it. An athlete wants to know whether a class will improve their performance, whether the programming is sound and who trains there. Those questions point to different pages, offers and messages.

Treat them as two funnels rather than one. Beginners need reassurance and an easy first step. Athletes need evidence that your coaching is serious. Mixing the two in one ad or one landing page usually makes both messages weaker, because neither group sees a clear reason to act.

## What Should You Tell a Beginner?

Lead with the first visit, not the contract. An intro class, a clear description of who the class suits and a note that coaches will show newcomers the basics all answer the "will I fit in" worry. Keep the ask small: one booking, one class, one visit.

After the first visit, follow up quickly. Mariana Tek's guidance on a 24-hour automated welcome sequence applies here, since interest fades after a pop-up ends. Our [email marketing guide for fitness studios](/blog/email-marketing-fitness-studio) covers how to structure those messages.

## What Do Athletes Respond To?

Athletes respond to programming: weekly structure, progressions and a way to track results. Show these on the page and in short videos. Mariana Tek recommends posting at a steady cadence rather than in bursts, with three to five short-form videos a week as a realistic pace for most studios. Clips of coaching and progressions suit that rhythm; our [social media strategy guide](/blog/fitness-studio-social-media-marketing-strategy) covers the content mix.

Athletes also recommend studios to each other. ClubRight's illustrative example notes that even if only half of your members refer one friend, you would still have doubled your membership base. That is an illustration, not a forecast, but it shows why an event or challenge that members want to share is worth planning.

## How Do You Reach Each Group Without Wasting Spend?

Build separate ad sets for each group. Use a beginner message about the first visit and an athlete message about programming, and point each to a matching page. The [Facebook ads with AI guide](/blog/fitness-studio-marketing-with-facebook-ads-and-ai) walks through setting up that structure.

Contact data can also be thinner than it looks. RevenueBase's Austin group fitness studio list tracks 4,652 professional contacts, and only 302 of them have verified work emails. Check how many of your own contacts are reachable before you plan a campaign around a list.

## How Should You Split a Marketing Budget? (Worked Example)

No source sets the right split between beginners and athletes, so the example below uses labelled assumptions. Monthly revenue of $40,000 and a 60/40 split toward beginners are **assumptions** for illustration only. Replace them with your own figures.

| Scenario (assumption: $40,000 monthly revenue) | Total marketing | Beginners (60%, assumption) | Athletes (40%, assumption) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Floor: 5% of revenue | $2,000 | $1,200 | $800 |
| Ceiling: 10% of revenue | $4,000 | $2,400 | $1,600 |

The 5 to 10 percent range comes from Mariana Tek. The split is the only part you decide. If beginner classes sit half empty while advanced classes are full, move budget toward beginners for a set period, then review.

If 30 pop-up contacts arrive in a month (**assumption**), each needs a follow-up within 24 hours. Automating that sequence is more reliable than relying on staff to send 30 messages on time.

## How Do You Know Which Group Is Working?

Review lead source, trial attendance and joins by group every month. A booking system such as Mindbody can show who attends a trial and who returns for a second class. Our [Mindbody marketing guide](/blog/how-to-use-mindbody-for-marketing-fitness-studio) explains how to pull those numbers.

Track retention at 30, 60 and 90 days for each group. Beginner retention often decides whether a new-member campaign pays off, while athlete retention shows whether advanced classes keep their value. Compare both against your own baseline rather than an industry average.

## FAQ

### How do I find my own beginner dropout rate?

Count how many beginners book a first class, how many return within 30, 60 and 90 days, and how many stop booking. Your booking system can export this. No reliable published rate for boutique studios exists, so your own numbers are the most useful benchmark.

### How much should a boutique studio spend on marketing?

Many boutique studios budget between 5 and 10 percent of monthly revenue for marketing, according to [Mariana Tek](https://www.marianatek.com/blog/group-fitness-studio-marketing-strategies/). Start at the low end, measure joins by source, and increase spend on the channels that bring athletes or beginners who stay.

### Should beginners and athletes take the same classes?

That depends on your format. If you mix levels, label each class clearly and tell newcomers which level to start with, so no one feels lost. If your format separates them, use separate schedules and pages for each group.

### How quickly should I follow up with a new lead?

Within 24 hours, using an automated welcome sequence. Mariana Tek notes that interest fades fast after a pop-up ends, so a delayed reply can cost you the lead.

### How often should a studio post short videos?

Three to five times a week at a steady pace is the cadence Mariana Tek describes as realistic for most studios. Posting in bursts tends to be harder to sustain.
