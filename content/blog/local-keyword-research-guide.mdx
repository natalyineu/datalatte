Local keyword research means finding the exact phrases people type when they need a business like yours nearby, such as "dog grooming near me" or "plumber Denver." Start with your services and service areas, collect the wording customers really use, then rank those terms by intent and cost. A keyword is worth targeting when someone typing it could book you, not just browse. Benchmarks from [WordStream](https://www.wordstream.com/blog/ws/2016/02/29/google-adwords-industry-benchmarks) give a rough yardstick for click rates and costs, though they are all-industry averages rather than local-specific figures.

## What Makes a Keyword Local?

A local keyword combines a service with a place or a proximity signal. "Dog grooming Chicago," "dog groomer near Wicker Park," and "emergency dog grooming open Sunday" all qualify. Generic terms like "best dog groomer" rarely do, because the person may be searching from another city.

Write your list before opening any tool. Include every service you sell, every neighborhood or suburb you serve, and the problems customers describe when they call. A pet groomer might draft three columns: service, location, and problem.

## Which Free Tools Show Real Local Search Language?

Start with tools that cost nothing. Google Trends lets you compare interest in "yoga studio Los Angeles" against "yoga near me" over time, which shows which phrasing people use more. Google Maps shows the language competitors put in their business names, categories, and reviews. Read the first ten results for your service and note repeated phrases.

Google Search Console's query report shows the terms already bringing visitors to your site. If you run Google Ads, the search terms report shows what people typed before clicking your ads. Both are free if you already have the accounts.

Developers and agencies sometimes pull map data programmatically. If that applies to you, the guide on [using the Google Maps API for local businesses](/blog/using-google-maps-api-for-local-businesses) explains the setup. For most owners, manual review of the first page of results is enough.

## How Do You Run Local Keyword Research in Five Steps?

Work through this checklist once, then repeat it every 90 days:

1. Build the three-column list: service, location, and the problem the customer describes.
2. Export the query report from Search Console and the search terms report from Google Ads, and mark any local phrasing you find.
3. Check each candidate in Google Trends against its "near me" version, and keep the variant with steadier interest.
4. Score each surviving term for intent, competition, and what one booking is worth to you.
5. Test the top five terms in a small campaign, then review results after two to four weeks.

Keep the output in one spreadsheet so each term has a single owner page and a recorded result.

## How Much Does a Local Click Cost?

Cost tells you which terms deserve a budget. WordStream's cross-industry averages put the search cost per click at $2.69 and the Display Network cost per click at $0.63, with a 3.17% average search click-through rate. These are all-industry averages, so treat them as a starting range and check your own account data.

Industry averages vary widely. The same source lists consumer services and legal above $6 per search click, while travel and e-commerce sit well below $2. A keyword that costs $6 may still pay off if each booking is worth a lot, so always compare cost with value.

## How Do You Rank Keywords by Conversion Value?

Score each keyword on three criteria: monthly search volume, competition, and intent. Aim for terms with steady demand that a small competitor can realistically win. "Barbershop for men Phoenix" is often easier to win than the broader "barber shop," and "book yoga class Denver" usually signals more purchase intent than "yoga near me."

Here is a worked example using the averages above. Every input marked as an assumption should be replaced with your own data.

1. Assumption: "dog grooming near me" gets 40 monthly searches in your area.
2. Clicks needed for 30 monthly visits at a 3.17% CTR: about 946 impressions (30 divided by 0.0317).
3. Monthly ad spend at the $2.69 average search CPC: 30 clicks times $2.69, about $80.70.
4. Assumption: 10% of clicks book an appointment, so 3 bookings.
5. Assumption: average booking value is $60, so $180 in revenue.
6. Return: $180 divided by $80.70, about 2.2 times spend.

If your bookings are worth $20 instead of $60, the same keyword returns about 0.7 times spend and is not worth paying for. That is why value matters more than volume. ## Should Every Neighborhood Get Its Own Page?

Only when a neighborhood has real customers and a distinct reason to choose you. Creating dozens of thin pages with swapped place names does not help people or search engines. Each page should answer a specific question for that area, such as parking, travel time, or service availability.

If you run a service area business without a storefront, say so clearly on each page. Avoid listing an address you do not serve from. Maps embedded on location pages can also help visitors see where you operate, and the guide on [embedding Google Maps on a local business website](/blog/google-maps-embed-local-business-website) covers the steps.

Test one neighborhood page first. Track whether its searches and calls grow over three months before creating more.

## Where Does Paid Search Fit in Keyword Research?

Paid search is the fastest way to test whether a keyword converts. Run a small search campaign on your top five terms, then review the search terms report weekly. Keep the terms that book customers and add the wasteful ones as negative keywords.

Some local service categories also have their own ad formats. The [Google Local Services Ads complete guide](/blog/google-local-services-ads-complete-guide) explains how those differ from standard search ads, and the [worth-it analysis for local businesses](/blog/google-local-service-ads-worth-it-local-business) helps you decide whether they fit your budget.

Google's help pages point to Performance Max or Search campaigns as alternatives to automated Display setups, so start with search if you want control over individual keywords.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many local keywords should a small business target?

Start with five to ten core terms, each tied to one page or listing. Add more only after the first set shows clicks and bookings. Long lists spread effort thin and make it hard to tell which terms are working.

### How often should I repeat keyword research?

Review your list every 90 days, and sooner if you add a service or a new area. Seasonal demand changes which terms are worth paying for, so compare the same months year over year where you can.

### Should my business name be a target keyword?

Usually not for acquisition. People searching your business name already know you exist. Keep the name in your title tags and homepage, and target service and location terms for new customers.

### Do I need paid tools to do this well?

No. Google Trends, Search Console, Google Maps, and your Google Ads search terms report cover most of the work. Consider a paid keyword tool only after you have a clear list of opportunities to check.
