## How large is the grooming labour market?

The US Bureau of Labor Statistics counted 413,000 animal care and service workers in 2025, and projects 11% employment growth over the decade, which it describes as much faster than average. The same outlook expects about 69,400 openings each year, many of them from replacing workers who move on or retire, according to the [BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/personal-care-and-service/animal-care-and-service-workers.htm). Growth in the workforce signals demand for the trade, but it does not tell you how many pet owners in your postcode are searching for a groomer this month.

These figures are US national data. Readers in the UK, Australia, or Canada should check their own national labour statistics before using them, and the [Canada guide](/blog/local-business-marketing-canada-guide-2026) covers local marketing context for Canadian businesses.

A secondary source, the Gingr blog, argues that pet-care businesses can use social media to increase visibility and share before-and-after photos of grooming work ([Gingr](https://www.gingrapp.com/blog/the-paw-sitive-impact-of-social-media-on-pet-care-businesses), secondary). That supports the creative approach in this guide, but it is a vendor's view rather than measured results.

## How much should a pet groomer spend on Meta Ads?

Start from the number of bookings you want per month, then work backwards to spend. The formula is simple: monthly budget equals target bookings multiplied by cost per booking. If you know your average groom price and your margin, you also know the most you can afford to pay for each booking.

Here is a worked example. Every input is an assumption for illustration, not a sourced figure:

- Average groom price: $75 (assumption)
- Cost per link click: $1.20 (assumption)
- Share of clicks that become bookings: 5% (assumption)
- Test period: 30 days (assumption)

At $400 per month, the campaign buys about 333 clicks ($400 divided by $1.20). At a 5% booking rate, that is about 17 bookings, or roughly $23.50 per booking. Those 17 bookings bring in about $1,275 in revenue, a return of about $3.20 per ad dollar before costs. The table shows how the same inputs scale.

| Monthly budget (assumption) | Clicks at $1.20 | Bookings at 5% | Cost per booking | Revenue at $75 per groom |

| $200 | 167 | 8 | $25.00 | $600 |
| $400 | 333 | 17 | $23.50 | $1,275 |
| $600 | 500 | 25 | $24.00 | $1,875 |

Cost per booking stays close to $24 in this example because the inputs are fixed. Real campaigns vary by season, location, and offer, so treat the table as a template to fill with your own data. ## What steps set up a grooming campaign?

Work through these steps in order before you spend the first dollar:

1. Confirm your booking route works on mobile. Test the booking link on a phone, because most ad clicks will come from one.
2. Write down your target cost per booking, using the formula above.
3. Choose one service and one offer, such as a first-visit groom, so the ad has a clear purpose.
4. Build one audience around your service area and keep it narrow enough to test cleanly.
5. Use before-and-after photos from your own grooming space, with a single call to action such as "Book now".
6. Set a daily budget that matches your monthly plan, then let the campaign run for a fixed 14-day test window (assumption: a planning choice, not a platform rule).
7. Review bookings, cost per booking, and revenue at the end of the window before changing anything.

Changing several variables at once makes it impossible to tell what worked. Change one thing per test, and keep notes.

## How do you know whether the ads are working?

Judge the campaign on bookings, not clicks. A cheap click that never books costs more than an expensive one that converts. Track each booking back to the ad where possible, using a booking form that asks how the client heard about you, or a separate booking link for the campaign.

Compare cost per booking against the target you set in step two. If it is above target after the test window, change one part of the setup, such as the offer or the creative, and run it again. If it stays above target across two tests, reduce spend or stop and move the budget to another channel.

Some groomers also use [Google Local Services Ads](/blog/google-local-service-ads-worth-it-local-business) alongside Meta, since search intent and social browsing bring different clients. Compare each channel on the same cost-per-booking measure so the budget follows the results.

## FAQ

### How much should a small pet groomer spend on Meta Ads each month?

Use the budget formula: target bookings multiplied by your cost per booking. The worked example uses $400 per month as an assumption. Start with a level you can sustain for a full 14-day test, then adjust based on your own results.

### Do I need a professional photographer for ad images?

No source here tests that question, so treat it as a choice to make yourself. Before-and-after photos taken in your own grooming space are the simplest option, and they show the service you actually deliver.

### Is this data valid outside the US?

The labour figures come from the US Bureau of Labor Statistics and only apply to the US. Use your national statistics office for UK, Australia, or Canada, and the cost-per-booking method still applies in any country.

### What if my campaign gets no bookings after the test?

Check the booking route, the offer, and the audience in that order, and change one at a time. If the campaign still books nothing after two careful tests, the channel may not suit your market, and the budget is better spent elsewhere.
