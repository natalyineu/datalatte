Here is the number that matters. If you pay a local creator $100 and comp a meal that costs you $20 in ingredients, you are $120 down before anyone has walked in. At an assumed $25 average spend and 65% gross margin, each new guest earns you about $16, so the deal breaks even at roughly 8 new guests. That is a very beatable target for a creator with a real local audience, and an impossible one for a creator whose followers live in another country.

This guide is for café and restaurant owners in the US, UK, Australia and Canada. It is about the economics and the mechanics: what to expect from influencers (the honest evidence is mixed), who to approach, how to structure the deal, the disclosure rules, and how to count walk-ins. The inputs in the math are assumptions, so swap in your own food cost and ticket size.

## What the Evidence Says (It Is Mixed)

Be sceptical of any blanket claim that influencers fill restaurants. The few restaurant-specific surveys point different ways, and the verifiable ones are older:

- A YouGov survey for SevenRooms, reported by [Restaurant Dive in December 2018](https://www.restaurantdive.com/news/survey-half-of-diners-rely-on-friends-family-not-instagram-for-recomm/544008/), found that over half of Americans turned to friends and family for restaurant suggestions, about a third used review sites such as Yelp, 10% turned to Instagram, and only 7% would order a dish an influencer recommended. The article does not give the sample size.
- A study by the agency MGH, reported by [Restaurant Dive in April 2019](https://www.restaurantdive.com/news/45-of-diners-have-tried-a-restaurant-because-of-social-media/553108), found 45% of US diners had tried a restaurant for the first time because of a post from the restaurant itself. That is about the venue's own posts, not influencers, and the article does not state a sample size either.

Both are several years old and neither is a controlled test of paid creators. The practical reading: friends, reviews and your own social presence matter, and an influencer is one extra route to a first visit, not a replacement for the rest. That is why this article treats a creator deal as a small, measurable experiment. ## Who to Approach

A creator with 500,000 followers across a country can post a lovely photo of your latte and send you nobody. What you want is a local audience that can reach you on foot or by car.

Where to look:

- Search Instagram and TikTok for your city or suburb plus "eats", "brunch", "coffee" or "food".
- Check who already tags your location. Existing fans accept more readily and sound more believable.
- Scan local Facebook food groups and Reddit threads for people who regularly recommend places.
- Ask regulars who post about you.

Screen each one:

1. **Is the audience local?** Ask for a screenshot of their audience location breakdown from Instagram Insights, or check the commenters yourself.
2. **Are the comments real?** Look for specific replies ("is the patio dog friendly?") rather than rows of emoji.
3. **Does their style fit the room?** A moody late-night cocktail account is wrong for a family bakery.
4. **Have they handled sponsored posts properly before?** Clear labels on past posts are a good sign.

Start with two to four creators. Our guides to [local influencer marketing](/blog/local-influencer-marketing) and [micro-influencer marketing for local business](/blog/micro-influencer-marketing-local-business) cover how to build a bigger roster once you know what works.

## What to Offer

| Structure | How it works | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gifted visit | A meal or drinks in exchange for honest content | Smaller local accounts, new venues |
| Fee plus visit | A flat fee and a meal for a defined set of posts | Creators with a proven local audience |
| Event invite | Several creators at a tasting or launch | Openings, new menus |
| Code or phrase | A unique discount or free add-on tied to the creator | Counting real redemptions |

Rates vary enormously by city and by creator, so ask for a rate card rather than guessing. For a cafe, a comped drink and pastry might be plenty for a small account. Never script the review. Ask for an honest post in their own voice, and tell them you will not edit it.

## Put the Deal in Writing

A short email or one-page agreement avoids most disputes. Include:

- The deliverables (for example one Reel and three Stories), the posting window and the handles to tag.
- What you provide (the meal, the fee, the date and a quiet time to film).
- Two or three things worth showing: the signature dish, the room, the pour.
- What to avoid, such as quoting prices that might change.
- Usage rights: can you repost it on your own channels and Google profile, and can you run it as an ad? Paid use usually costs extra, so agree it up front.
- The disclosure requirement (next section).

Book creators at a quiet time, such as a mid-afternoon weekday, and brief the floor staff so the creator is not treated like a confused walk-in.

## Disclosure Rules: Your Responsibility Too

If you give a creator free food or money, the post is generally advertising and has to be labelled. Do not leave that to the creator alone.

**United States.** The FTC's [Disclosures 101 for social media influencers](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/disclosures-101-social-media-influencers) says free products count as a material connection and the disclosure should be hard to miss and placed with the endorsement, not just in a profile. It lists terms like "ad" and "sponsored" as clear and standalone words like "thanks" as vague, and it warns not to assume a platform's disclosure tool is enough on its own. For businesses, the FTC's [Endorsement Guides Q&A](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking) says advertisers need reasonable training and monitoring programmes. For people who only get free products, it says to ask them to clearly disclose the gift and tag the brand, then monitor the tagged posts. It also says a company is ultimately responsible for what others do on its behalf.

**United Kingdom.** The ASA's [guidance on recognising ads](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/recognising-ads-social-media.html) says a payment or other incentive, requested or unsolicited, can bring content into scope, that "Ad" is the preferred label and should come upfront, that "Gifted" is unlikely to be clear enough, and that a label visible only after tapping "See more" is not acceptable.

For Canada and a fuller breakdown, see the disclosure section of our [influencer marketing for fitness studios guide](/blog/influencer-marketing-for-fitness). Australian rules are not covered here, so check your regulator's current guidance. In practice: put "ad" or the platform's paid partnership label at the start of the post, and add the requirement to your written agreement.

## Give People a Reason to Come Now

A beautiful post without a hook is just a nice post. Add one:

- **A unique phrase or code.** "Say 'Maya sent me' for a free cookie with any coffee" is trackable if staff tap a button on the till or tally marks on a sheet.
- **A limited-time item** tied to the creator, with a clear end date.
- **A link in their bio** to your Google Maps listing or booking page.

Make sure the destination is ready. Opening hours, photos and a menu link on your Google profile matter more than a clever caption; our [Google Business Profile guide for coffee shops](/blog/google-business-profile-optimization-for-coffee-shops) is a checklist, and [Google My Business for restaurants](/blog/google-my-business-for-restaurants) covers the restaurant version.

## How to Track It Without Software

You do not need an attribution tool for a $120 experiment.

- **Redemptions** of the creator's phrase or code, logged at the till.
- **Profile visits, follows and direction requests** in the week after the post, from Instagram and Google Business Profile insights. Note the baseline for the same weekday in the three weeks before.
- **A one-line question** at the till for first-time guests: "How did you hear about us?" Record it in a notebook.
- **Sales on the day** compared with the same weekday in earlier weeks.

Judge each creator on cost per new guest, including the food cost of the comp. That decides who gets invited back.

## The Math

Each row below is an **assumption** for illustration. None are industry benchmarks. Replace them with your own food cost, ticket and margin.

| Per creator | Tough | Base | Strong |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Creator fee (assumption) | $100 | $100 | $100 |
| Comped meal, ingredient cost (assumption) | $20 | $20 | $20 |
| Total cash cost | $120 | $120 | $120 |
| New guests who redeem (assumption) | 5 | 15 | 40 |
| Average spend per guest (assumption) | $25 | $25 | $25 |
| Gross margin (assumption) | 65% | 65% | 65% |
| Gross profit from those guests | $81 | $244 | $650 |
| Net after cost | -$39 | +$124 | +$530 |
| Cost per new guest | $24 | $8 | $3 |

Break-even is the total cash cost divided by profit per guest: $120 divided by about $16.25 is roughly 7.4, so you need 8 new guests. This ignores staff time and any repeat visits, which would improve the picture if guests return, and it ignores the value of reusable photos and video, which is real but hard to price.

Two takeaways. First, the downside is capped: in the Tough case you lose $39 per creator. Second, the spread between Tough and Strong is 8 to 1, which is why choosing local creators with a real audience matters more than negotiating the fee.

## Do This Today

1. Work out your numbers: ingredient cost of a comped meal, average spend and gross margin. Calculate your break-even number of new guests.
2. Shortlist ten local creators, ask three for a rate card and an audience location screenshot, and narrow to two or three.
3. Write a one-page brief and agreement covering deliverables, the offer, usage rights and the "ad" label requirement.
4. Create a trackable hook (a phrase or code) and a tally sheet at the till, and brief your staff.
5. Book the visits for quiet slots, then review redemptions, profile visits and sales a week after each post and invite back only the ones that beat break-even.

## FAQ

**Q: How much should I pay a local influencer?**
A: There is no standard. Rates vary by city, platform and audience, so ask each creator for a rate card. Many smaller local accounts accept a comped meal, and the Math table above shows how to check whether a fee pays back.

**Q: Do I have to label gifted meals as ads?**
A: Generally yes. The FTC treats free products as a material connection that should be disclosed, and the UK's ASA says incentives can bring content into scope and that "Gifted" alone is unlikely to be clear enough. Check your own country's regulator for the exact wording.

**Q: Instagram or TikTok for a cafe?**
A: Use the platform where your local customers already look for places to eat. Ask your regulars and look at where local creators get comments from nearby people.

**Q: How long until I see results?**
A: Redemptions usually show within days of a post, but a single post is a small sample. Judge a creator on one post, and judge the channel after three or four partnerships.
