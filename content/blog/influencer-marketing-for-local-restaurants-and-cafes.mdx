Influencer marketing can work for a local restaurant or cafe if you choose creators who live in your area, offer a clear reason to visit, and track who actually walks in. For most independents that means micro-influencers (a few thousand local followers) paid in food, drinks or modest fees, not big-name accounts with audiences scattered around the world.

Here is a practical playbook: who to work with, what to offer, how to brief them, how to stay within advertising rules, and how to tell whether it paid off.

## Why local beats big

A creator with 500,000 followers across the country might get you a lot of likes and very few customers. A local food blogger, student, or neighbourhood account with 3,000 to 30,000 followers mostly reaches people who can drive or walk to you. Their audience also tends to trust their recommendations more because the account feels like a neighbour, not an advertiser.

Typical tiers, roughly:

- **Nano** (under about 10,000 followers): often happy with a free meal or coffee.
- **Micro** (about 10,000 to 100,000): may charge from tens to a few hundred dollars per post, varying by market.
- **Larger creators**: rates rise quickly and are rarely worth it for a single-location business.

Rates vary widely by city and platform, so treat these as rough guidance and ask creators for their rate card.

## Step 1: Set one clear goal

Pick one outcome: opening week traffic, a new menu item, weekday lunch, or a weekend brunch push. Vague goals such as "awareness" are hard to judge. A good goal reads like "get 50 new faces in on weekday mornings this month."

## Step 2: Find the right creators

Where to look:

- Search Instagram and TikTok for your city plus terms like "food", "eats", "coffee", "brunch" or your suburb name.
- Check who is already tagging your location or hashtag. Existing fans make the best partners.
- Look at local Facebook food groups and Reddit threads for people who regularly recommend places.
- Ask regular customers who post about you.

Then screen each candidate:

- Is the audience local? Instagram insights or a quick scroll through followers and comments will show it.
- Is engagement genuine, with real comments rather than generic emoji spam?
- Does their content style match your venue?
- Have they posted sponsored content clearly and professionally before?

## Step 3: Make an offer they can say yes to

Common deal structures:

| Structure | How it works | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| Gifted visit | Free meal or drinks in exchange for honest content | Nano creators, new venues |
| Fee plus visit | Flat fee and a meal for a defined set of posts | Micro creators |
| Affiliate code | Creator gets a small reward per redemption of a unique code | Tracking and ongoing partnerships |
| Event invite | Creators attend a tasting or launch | New menus, openings |

Never demand a glowing review. Ask for an honest post and let them keep their voice. Overly scripted content tends to look like an ad and performs worse.

## Step 4: Brief them properly

A short written brief avoids awkward back-and-forth. Include:

- What you want posted (for example one Reel and three Stories) and when.
- Your handle, location tag and any hashtag to use.
- Two or three things worth showing: the signature dish, the room, the espresso pour.
- What to avoid, such as mentioning pricing that may change.
- A reminder to disclose the partnership.

Offer a quiet slot for the visit, so they can film without a busy rush.

## Step 5: Follow the disclosure rules

Paid or gifted content must be clearly labelled. In the US, the FTC expects creators to disclose material connections; in the UK, the ASA and CMA have similar expectations, with labels like "#ad" or the platform's paid-partnership tool. Rules vary by country, so check your local guidance and ask creators to use the built-in branded content tools. This protects both of you.

## Step 6: Give people a reason to act now

Awareness alone rarely fills tables. Add a hook:

- A unique code or phrase ("say you saw us on the Reel" is simple but trackable only if staff record it).
- A limited-time item or a free add-on with the code.
- A link in the creator's bio that goes to your booking page or Google Maps listing.

Make sure your own profile is ready for the traffic. Check that your Google Business Profile has current hours, photos and a menu link. See our [coffee shop Google Business Profile guide](/blog/coffee-shop-google-business-profile-optimization) for a checklist.

## Step 7: Measure what matters

You do not need fancy software. Track:

- **Redemptions** of codes or phrases at the till.
- **Profile visits and follower growth** on your account during the week of the post.
- **Direction requests and website clicks** in Google Business Profile insights.
- **New versus returning guests**, if your POS or booking tool reports it.
- **Sales on the day** compared with the same weekday in earlier weeks.

Judge the partnership against what you spent, including the cost of the food. If it did not move the needle, ask whether the creator, the offer or the timing was the problem before dropping the channel.

## Reuse the content

With permission, repost creators' content on your own channels, in your Google Business Profile posts, and on your website. A short clip from a local voice often works better than your own polished photo. Written agreement on usage rights is wise, since reuse in paid ads usually costs extra.

## Common mistakes

- Choosing by follower count instead of local relevance.
- Giving no brief, then being surprised by the result.
- Skipping disclosure.
- Expecting one post to transform sales rather than building a steady rhythm.
- Failing to prepare staff, so the creator's followers arrive to a confused welcome.

## A simple first-month plan

- **Week 1:** Shortlist ten local creators, check audience and content quality, and narrow to three.
- **Week 2:** Send a friendly invitation with your offer and a one-page brief.
- **Week 3:** Host the visits at quiet times, and make sure staff know who is coming.
- **Week 4:** Review redemptions, profile visits and sales, then decide whom to invite back.

Starting with two or three creators lets you learn what your audience responds to without a large commitment.

## FAQ

**Q: How much should I pay a local influencer?**
A: It varies by city, platform and audience. Many nano creators accept a free meal, while micro creators may ask for a fee in the tens to low hundreds of dollars per post. Always ask for their rate card.

**Q: Is it better to use Instagram or TikTok?**
A: Pick the platform where your local customers already look for places to eat. Instagram suits cafes and brunch spots; TikTok can work well for visual or novelty items. Many creators post on both.

**Q: Do I have to give free food?**
A: Not necessarily, but gifted visits are the most common starting point. The key is that the exchange is clear and fair to both sides.

**Q: How long until I see results?**
A: Some effect often shows within days of posting, but consistent results usually come from several partnerships over a few months.
