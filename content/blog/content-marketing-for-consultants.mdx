Ten discovery calls a year. That is roughly how many a consultant needs from content to cover the cost of their time, if they invest 10 hours a month, value those hours at $100 each, close 30% of calls and earn $4,000 per new client. Every one of those inputs is my assumption, not a benchmark, and the table later in this article lets you swap in your own. The point: content marketing is not a vague "brand" activity. It is a small funnel with numbers you can check, and most consultants never check them.

This guide is for independent consultants and small advisory firms in the US, UK, Australia and Canada, including people who advise local businesses such as salons, studios and cafés. It covers how to pick a focus, what to publish, where to share it, and how to measure whether it works. Dollar figures are illustrative; use your own currency.

## What the Research Says (and Does Not)

Most public content marketing research covers B2B marketing teams in larger companies, not solo consultants. Treat it as direction, not a forecast. The Content Marketing Institute's [B2B Content and Marketing Trends: Insights for 2026](https://contentmarketinginstitute.com/b2b-research/b2b-content-marketing-trends-research) surveyed 1,015 B2B marketers, mostly in North America, between June and August 2025. Among its findings:

Three useful reads. First, nearly everyone publishes, so "having content" is not an edge; a clear point of view is. Second, in the same report email newsletters (54%) and speaking events or webinars (52%) follow LinkedIn as effective thought-leadership channels. Third, only 18% of respondents report substantial employee participation in thought leadership, which hints that most output is thin. That last point is my reading, not CMI's conclusion.

Google's guidance on [helpful, people-first content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) points the same way. It asks whether your content offers original information, reporting, research or analysis, and whether it shows first-hand expertise. It also warns that summarising other people's work without adding much value is a red flag. For a consultant, "original" means your clients' questions, your process and your numbers.

## Step 1: Narrow Your Focus

Broad claims like "business strategy for growing companies" are easy to ignore. Define three things:

1. **Who you help.** A size, industry or role: "owners of 5 to 30-person home services firms".
2. **The problem you solve.** "Fixing cash-flow forecasting" or "setting up hiring".
3. **The outcome.** What changes for the client afterwards.

Write one sentence: "I help [who] to [outcome] by [method]". Every article, post and email should connect to it. If you advise local businesses, see how [local marketing consultants](/blog/local-business-marketing-consultant-worth-it) are judged by their buyers.

## Step 2: Build Topics From Real Questions

List 15 questions prospects ask you on discovery calls, in emails and in meetings. Those are your topics, and they already have demand because buyers ask them. Group them:

- **Costs and timelines:** "How much does it cost to hire someone like you?" These attract buyers, not casual readers.
- **Mistakes:** the three errors you keep fixing.
- **Process:** how you run an engagement, step by step.
- **Comparisons:** in-house vs outsourced, option A vs option B.

## Step 3: Pick One Home Base and One Channel

| Format | Best for | Effort |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Articles on your site | Search visibility, a lasting asset | Medium to high |
| LinkedIn posts | Reaching professional buyers, starting conversations | Low to medium |
| Email newsletter | Staying in touch with prospects and past clients | Medium |
| Case studies | Proof | Medium (needs client permission) |
| Webinars and talks | Warm leads, live questions | High |
| Checklists and templates | Collecting email addresses | Medium |

Choose a home base (your website or newsletter) and a distribution channel (for most B2B consultants, LinkedIn), and stay with them for several months. Do not try all six.

## Step 4: Publish Proof, Not Fiction

Authority comes from evidence you really have:

- **Client case studies,** with permission and agreed details. If a client needs confidentiality, anonymise and say so.
- **Your process,** laid out step by step.
- **Frameworks you use,** with real examples and the numbers behind them.
- **Honest lessons,** including what did not work.

Never invent client stories, statistics or testimonials. Buyers spot them and trust drops fast. If you have no case studies yet, write about your method and reasoning, and add proof as you collect it. Google's helpful-content page also notes that readers should be able to tell who created content, so put your name, a short bio and your photo on every article.

## Step 5: A Routine You Can Keep

Consistency beats volume. A schedule for a busy consultant (my suggestion, not a research-backed benchmark):

- **One substantial article per month** on your site.
- **Two or three LinkedIn posts per week,** drawn from the article and client questions.
- **One email per month** with the article and a short personal note.
- **One repurposed asset per article:** a checklist, slide or short video.

Block two hours a week for writing and treat it as a client meeting.

## Step 6: Use LinkedIn Like a Conversation

The CMI survey above found LinkedIn rated most effective by 76% of respondents for thought leadership, though those are marketers rating their own programmes. For a consultant, the practical moves are:

- **Profile:** a headline that says who you help, a plain-language summary and your best content in the Featured section.
- **Posts:** one idea each, with a clear takeaway and a real example. Skip vague motivation.
- **Comments:** thoughtful replies on prospects' and peers' posts often start more conversations than posting alone (my experience, not a statistic).
- **Direct messages:** reply to people who engage; do not pitch cold.

## Step 7: Use Email to Turn Interest Into Calls

Send a monthly or fortnightly note with one useful idea, one link and a gentle invitation to reply. Replies are the strongest signal you will get. Mind the rules: the FTC's [CAN-SPAM guide](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business) says the US law applies to business-to-business email, with no exception, and requires accurate sender details, a valid postal address and a working opt-out honoured within 10 business days. Penalties can reach $53,088 per violating email. UK, Australian and Canadian rules differ and are generally stricter on consent, so check your local law. ## The Math

How many discovery calls does content need to produce before it pays for your time? **Every input below is an assumption** except the structure; replace them with your own.

| | Tough | Base | Strong |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hours per month on content (assumption) | 10 | 10 | 10 |
| Value of your hour (assumption) | $100 | $100 | $100 |
| Annual time cost | $12,000 | $12,000 | $12,000 |
| Discovery calls from content per month (assumption) | 1 | 2 | 4 |
| Call to client rate (assumption) | 20% | 30% | 40% |
| New clients per year | 2.4 | 7.2 | 19.2 |
| Revenue per new client (assumption) | $4,000 | $4,000 | $4,000 |
| Annual revenue from content | $9,600 | $28,800 | $76,800 |
| Revenue minus time cost | -$2,400 | +$16,800 | +$64,800 |

The break-even formula: calls needed per year = annual time cost / (revenue per client x close rate). With $12,000, $4,000 and 30%: 12,000 / 1,200 = **10 calls a year**, under one a month. At a 20% close rate you need 15.

Two cautions. This ignores the lag: articles and an email list often take months before calls arrive, so expect a loss in the first quarter or two. And revenue is not profit; subtract delivery cost if you hire help. The lever that moves most is usually your close rate and the value of a client, not the number of posts.

## Measure What Matters

Track four numbers, monthly:

- **Discovery calls booked** and where each came from (ask every time).
- **Enquiries that mention your content.**
- **Email subscribers and reply rate.**
- **Content forwarded or referenced** in conversations.

Review quarterly. Do more of what leads to calls and drop what only collects likes.

## Common Mistakes

- Writing for other consultants instead of buyers.
- Publishing for a month, then stopping.
- Sharing opinions without examples or process.
- Publishing generic AI text with no real insight. AI can help with outlines and editing, but your experience must lead.
- No call to action or follow-up.

## FAQ

**Q: How long until content marketing brings leads?**
A: It varies, and no benchmark applies to every consultant. LinkedIn activity can start conversations within weeks; search traffic from articles often takes months. Plan for at least three to six months of consistent effort, which is my rule of thumb.

**Q: Do I need a blog?**
A: It helps. A site with in-depth articles is a lasting asset and a place to send prospects. A newsletter or LinkedIn alone can work as a start, but your own site gives you control.

**Q: What if I cannot share client details?**
A: Anonymise, share your process and lessons, or ask for a short permission-based quote. Never invent examples.

**Q: Should I use AI to write content?**
A: Use it for brainstorming, outlines and editing. Google's guidance asks whether content offers original information and first-hand expertise, which is your job, not the tool's.

**Q: Which channel should I start with?**
A: For most B2B consultants, LinkedIn plus a monthly email. In CMI's survey, LinkedIn, email newsletters and speaking or webinars were the top-rated thought-leadership channels.
