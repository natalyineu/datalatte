Google Ads has five access levels, and a remote consultant needs only one of them: Standard. Standard can edit your campaigns. Only Admin can give or take away access to the account, and Admin should stay with you. That one setting answers most of the nervous questions owners ask about remote marketing help: can they wreck my account, can they lock me out, and who owns the data? If you set access up correctly, the answers are no, no, and you.

This article is for owners of coffee shops, salons, groomers, studios and other local businesses in the US, UK, Australia and Canada who are weighing a remote consultant against a local agency or doing it themselves. It covers the access you grant, what the first month looks like, what reports to insist on, which red flags matter, and a payback model with labelled assumptions.

## Why Distance Barely Matters

The tools behind local marketing are all cloud accounts: Google Ads, Google Business Profile, Google Analytics and Meta's ad tools. Auditing a campaign, rebuilding a keyword list or fixing a profile category happens in a browser. Your consultant does not need to be in your postcode. What does need to be local is the knowledge: your market's competitors, your customers' search words, your opening hours and your busy seasons. You supply the local context; they supply the method. A good remote consultant asks for that context before touching anything.

That is a judgment, not a statistic. Nobody publishes reliable data that remote consultants outperform local agencies, or the reverse, and you should be suspicious of anyone who claims it. The better test is whether the person can show you, in your own account data, what changed and why.

## The Access You Should Grant (and Not Grant)

Never share a password. Every major platform lets you invite someone with a limited role, and you can remove that access later.

**Google Ads.** Google's [account access levels page](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/9978556) lists Email-only, Billing, Read-only, Standard and Admin. Standard covers editing campaigns and creating conversions from Google Analytics. Admin adds the ability to manage user access, accept or reject manager account link requests and unlink manager accounts. So: give the consultant Standard, keep Admin yourself, and keep billing in your own name so you control the card. Some consultants prefer to link their manager account to yours. Google's [manager account linking page](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/7459601) says the request is sent by customer ID and must be accepted by someone with admin access to your account, so you stay in control of that step.

**Google Business Profile.** Google's [user roles page](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3403100) says an owner can add or remove users and edit or delete the profile, while a manager has almost the same access except that they cannot add or remove users or remove the profile. Only owners can remove other owners and managers. Add your consultant as a manager and keep ownership yourself.

**Google Analytics 4.** Google's [GA4 roles page](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/9305587) describes Administrator (full control, can manage users), Editor (property settings but cannot manage users), Analyst and Viewer. Editor is enough for most consultants; Viewer is enough if they only report.

**Meta ads.** Meta has its own business-portfolio settings for giving an agency partner access. Menu labels change often, so follow Meta's current Business Help Center instructions, give access to specific assets, and make sure the Page and ad account are owned by your business.

**One rule for all of them.** Whoever pays for the account and owns the profile should be you. If a consultant creates the ad account under their own name and you cannot see the billing, you do not own your history. Ask in the first call, in writing: who is the owner of each account?

## What the First Month Should Look Like

There is no official standard for this, so treat the following as a reasonable pattern rather than a rule.

1. **Week 1: access and audit.** You grant the access above. The consultant reviews your Business Profile, website, ad accounts and tracking, and sends a written audit with the top priorities.
2. **Week 2: tracking before spend.** They confirm calls, forms and bookings are being counted. Our conversion tracking setup guide shows what correct looks like. If tracking is broken, fix it before changing any bids.
3. **Week 3: first changes.** Profile corrections, negative keywords, ad copy tests, a landing-page fix. Each change is logged with a date.
4. **Week 4: first report and call.** A 30 to 60 minute call goes through what changed, what moved and what comes next month. Those durations are common practice, not a requirement.

## What You Provide

- **Context:** busiest days, average spend per customer, services you want more of, upcoming promotions.
- **Fast approvals:** if the work includes ads or emails, you review before anything goes live. Agree a turnaround, such as one working day.
- **Outcome data:** which enquiries became customers. The consultant can see clicks and forms; only you know who actually bought.
- **History:** old campaign reports, seasonal patterns and any previous audits.

You do not need to be technical, but you do need to answer questions promptly. Slow approvals are the most common reason remote projects stall.

## What to Ask For in Reports

A monthly report should be readable in five minutes and should show:

- Spend, clicks, calls, form fills and bookings.
- Cost per lead and, once you are logging it, cost per customer.
- What was changed, on which date, and why.
- What will be tested next month.

If a report is a screenshot of impressions and clicks with no cost per lead, that is a red flag. If you cannot see the work reflected in the numbers within three months (a rule of thumb, not an industry standard), have an honest conversation or end the engagement. ## Common Worries, Answered

**"Will they understand my local market?"** Ask for experience in your country and niche, and ask how they would research your town. A good answer includes your top competitors on Google Maps, local search phrases and your review count versus theirs. A weak answer is a generic template.

**"What if something goes wrong at 4 pm on a Friday?"** Agree a response time in writing for urgent issues such as a disapproved ad, a budget overspend or a billing problem. Same business day is a reasonable ask.

**"What about time zones?"** Async work (campaign changes, reporting) does not care. For calls you need an overlap; many US/UK and UK/Australia pairs have a workable window but not always during traditional office hours, so check before you sign.

**"Can I leave?"** You can if you own the accounts. With the access setup above, you remove the user and your campaigns, history and conversion data stay where they were.

## The Math

Does a retainer pay for itself? This example uses a small ad budget, which is where many local businesses sit. **Every row is an assumption**; replace it with your numbers.

| | No change | Consultant improves cost per lead 20% | Consultant improves it 35% |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Monthly ad budget (assumption) | $1,500 | $1,500 | $1,500 |
| Cost per lead (assumption) | $60 | $48 | $39 |
| Leads | 25 | 31.3 | 38.5 |
| Lead to customer rate (assumption) | 25% | 25% | 25% |
| New customers | 6.3 | 7.8 | 9.6 |
| Value per customer, first year (assumption) | $150 | $150 | $150 |
| Revenue from ads | $938 | $1,172 | $1,442 |
| Extra revenue vs no change | $0 | $234 | $505 |
| Consultant fee (assumption) | $500 | $500 | $500 |
| Extra revenue minus fee | -$500 | -$266 | +$5 |

How to read this: at a $1,500 budget and a $500 monthly fee, the consultant has to cut your cost per lead by about 35% just to break even on these numbers. The 20% and 35% improvements are illustrations, not benchmarks, and no source publishes an average. The lesson is not that consultants are bad. It is that fee size should match the money being managed.

The break-even rule is: minimum extra revenue = monthly fee. If your ad budget is small, a one-off audit plus a training session often beats a retainer. If your budget is large, if customers are worth much more than $150 over their lifetime, or if the consultant also fixes your Business Profile, tracking and landing page, the maths improves quickly. ## Do This Today

1. List every account your marketing touches (Google Ads, Business Profile, Analytics, Meta, website, domain) and write down who owns each one.
2. Check each one has you as Admin or Owner. If an agency or ex-employee owns anything, ask for ownership to be transferred to you.
3. Work out your numbers: monthly ad budget, current cost per lead if you know it, value of a new customer and the most you would pay in fees.
4. Write a three-line brief: what you sell, who you want, and what a good month looks like in leads or bookings.
5. Request a free audit or a first call, and ask five questions before agreeing to anything: who owns each account, what access level they need, what the monthly report contains, how fast they respond to urgent issues, and what the notice period is.

## FAQ

**Q: What access does a remote marketing consultant need?**
A: For Google Ads, Standard access lets them edit campaigns, while Admin lets someone manage who has access. Keep Admin. For Business Profile, a manager role lets them edit but not add or remove users. For GA4, Editor is typically enough.

**Q: Should I give out my passwords?**
A: No. Use each platform's invite system so access is tied to the consultant's own login and can be removed by you.

**Q: How long before I see results?**
A: There is no official timeline. Tracking and profile fixes can show up within weeks; ad optimisation needs enough clicks and conversions to read. Agree a review date, such as three months, in advance.

**Q: Remote consultant or local agency?**
A: Decide on evidence: relevant niche experience, clear reporting, ownership of accounts and a fee that matches your budget. Location alone is not evidence.

**Q: What does it cost?**
A: Fees vary widely and there is no reliable published average. Use the break-even rule above: the extra revenue the work can realistically produce has to exceed the fee.
