LocaliQ's June 2026 search benchmarks list an average click in Health & Fitness at $6.17. Now work backwards for a yoga studio. If you will pay $200 to win one new member (about two months of a $100 membership), and 8 in 100 clicks become intro-offer sign-ups and 35% of those join, you can afford $5.60 a click. Drop the intro offer and bid on a generic "yoga classes near me" search, where 5 in 100 clicks enquire and 25% join, and the ceiling falls to $2.50. The $200, 8%, 35%, 5% and 25% are my assumptions, not benchmarks. The lesson is real, though: in yoga, the offer attached to the keyword matters as much as the keyword.

This guide is for studio owners in the US, UK, Australia and Canada. It covers the yoga-specific keyword groups (class styles, life stage, intro offers, teacher training), match types, a negative list that knows when to flip, and a campaign structure. It does not print search volumes or click prices per keyword, because they vary by city and season and we could not open Google Keyword Planner for this guide. You will pull your own in the steps below. ## Who Searches for Yoga, and Why

The latest US study we could open is dated: the 2016 Yoga in America Study, run by Yoga Journal and Yoga Alliance with Ipsos Public Affairs, counted 36.7 million US practitioners and reported that 72% were women. Its top reasons for starting were flexibility (61%), stress relief (56%), general fitness (49%) and improving overall health (49%). Those numbers are a decade old, so use them only as a prompt for keyword ideas, not as today's market size.

What they suggest: yoga searchers often arrive with a goal ("yoga for flexibility", "yoga for stress"), a life stage ("prenatal yoga") or a style ("hot yoga"), not just the word "yoga". Group your keywords that way.

## Keyword Groups by Intent

Give each group its own ad group and landing page. Replace [city] and [neighborhood] with the names people actually use.

| Intent | Example keywords | Match type |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ready to visit | "yoga studio [city]", "yoga classes [neighborhood]", "yoga classes near me" | Phrase + exact |
| Style | "hot yoga [city]", "vinyasa yoga [city]", "yin yoga classes [city]", "power yoga [neighborhood]", "restorative yoga near me" | Phrase + exact |
| Life stage | "prenatal yoga [city]", "postnatal yoga classes [city]", "yoga for seniors [city]", "chair yoga near me" | Phrase + exact |
| Goal | "yoga for back pain [city]", "yoga for stress [city]", "yoga for flexibility classes" | Phrase |
| Beginner and offer | "beginner yoga classes [city]", "yoga for beginners near me", "yoga intro offer [city]", "first yoga class free [city]" | Phrase |
| Membership and price | "yoga membership [city]", "unlimited yoga [city]", "yoga class pass [city]" | Phrase |
| Private and group | "private yoga lessons [city]", "corporate yoga [city]", "kids yoga classes [city]" | Phrase |
| Teacher training | "200 hour yoga teacher training [city]", "yoga teacher training [region]" | Phrase + exact, own campaign |
| Brand | [your studio name], [your studio name] schedule | Exact |

Notes on the table:

- **Style and life-stage terms** are specific, so the ad can repeat the words typed. Only list what is on your timetable this month. A "prenatal yoga" ad that lands on a page with no prenatal class is a paid bounce.
- **Goal terms** need careful wording. Google's [unreliable claims policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/15936857) bars ads that promise an improbable result, even if that result is possible, and lists unproven cures as an example. "Gentle classes for tight backs" is safer than "Cure back pain". That is my reading of the policy; check it before you write copy.
- **Membership and price terms** sit later in the decision. Our [ClassPass versus your own membership guide](/blog/yoga-studio-class-pass-vs-own-membership-marketing) covers how those searchers compare options.
- **"Online yoga" is a different product.** If you stream classes, run it as a separate campaign with a wider location and its own page. Mixing it into a local campaign drags local ad groups toward people who will never visit.

## Match Types: What Google Says

Google's [match type guide](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/2497836) describes three:

- **Broad match** can show on related searches, including ones that do not share the keyword's literal meaning. Google calls it critical to use Smart Bidding with broad match.
- **Phrase match** (quotes) can show on searches that include the meaning of your keyword, including more specific versions.
- **Exact match** (square brackets) can show on searches with the same meaning or intent, and gives the most control but the fewest searches.

For a studio with a few sign-ups a month, start with phrase and exact. Smart Bidding needs conversion data to learn from, and a small studio gives it little. Use phrase to see how people really word it, and exact for terms you already know fill classes plus your brand. Because phrase and exact now match on meaning, you do not need every spelling ("yoga classes", "yoga class", "yoga lessons") as a separate keyword. Check the search terms report to see what actually matched.

## Negative Keywords That Know When to Flip

Yoga attracts shoppers, students, teachers and at-home practitioners. Google's [negative keywords help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/2453972) says a broad-match negative blocks a search containing all the negative terms in any order, that negatives do not cover close variants (blocking "flowers" does not block "flower"), and that too many negatives can reduce your reach. So add singular and plural forms yourself, and be selective.

| Group | Negatives for your class campaigns |
| --- | --- |
| Free and DIY | `free`, `diy`, `tutorial`, `how to`, `youtube`, `video`, `videos`, `app`, `at home` |
| Wrong format | `online`, `virtual`, `zoom`, `livestream` |
| Training and jobs | `teacher training`, `certification`, `200 hour`, `500 hour`, `instructor`, `jobs`, `job`, `salary`, `hiring` |
| Travel | `retreat`, `retreats`, `holiday`, `bali`, `costa rica` |
| Products | `mat`, `mats`, `pants`, `leggings`, `blocks`, `straps`, `bolster`, `clothing`, `equipment` |
| Poses and theory | `poses`, `pose`, `sequence`, `benefits`, `history`, `philosophy`, `quotes` |
| Other | `for dogs`, `goat yoga` (drop any you offer) |

Three rules for yoga specifically:

1. **Flip the training terms by campaign.** `teacher training` is a negative in your class campaigns and a keyword in your teacher training campaign. A keyword and an identical negative in the same campaign cancel each other out, so keep them in separate campaigns.
2. **Handle "free" with care.** If you offer a free first class, keep "first yoga class free" as a keyword and add exact-match negatives for plain "free yoga" instead of blocking the word.
3. **Drop what you sell.** If you run online classes, retreats or a mat shop, remove those negatives from the relevant campaign.

Then read the [search terms report](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/2472708). Google says it shows the actual searches that triggered your ads and advises adding irrelevant ones as negatives. Do it weekly. Our negative keywords guide shows how to apply lists.

## Campaign Structure for a Studio

Keep it small enough that each ad group gets data.

1. **Campaign 1: Classes.** Ready-to-visit, style and life-stage ad groups. Most of the budget starts here.
2. **Campaign 2: Intro offer and membership.** Beginner, offer and price terms, landing on a page that repeats the offer. Separating it lets you cap spend and judge it by cost per new member.
3. **Campaign 3: Teacher training and workshops.** Different geography (people travel for training), different budget, different ceiling (see The Math), and a page with dates, price and curriculum.
4. **Campaign 4: Brand.** Your studio name in exact match, so a competitor cannot sit above you when someone searches your name.

Google's [budget help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/6385083) says daily spend can reach two times your average daily budget for most campaigns, with a monthly limit of 30.4 times the daily budget. Divide a monthly figure by 30.4 when you set it.

For ad text, responsive search ads take 3 to 15 headlines of up to 30 characters and 2 to 4 descriptions of up to 90, per Google's [ad requirements](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/7684791). Put the style, the town and the offer into headlines: `Hot Yoga in [Town]`, `First Class Free`, `Beginner Classes Weekly`, `Book Online in 30 Seconds`. Pair the ads with a complete [Google Business Profile](/services/google-business-profile), since people check reviews and photos before they book.

## The Math

The same click is worth different amounts depending on what it sells. Every input below is an **assumption** for illustration; replace them with your own after 30 days of data. The table shows the ceiling click price if you hold your target cost per sale fixed.

| | Generic near me | Style class | Intro offer | Teacher training | Brand |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Max ad cost per sale (assumption) | $200 | $200 | $200 | $450 | $200 |
| Click to lead rate (assumption) | 5% | 7% | 8% | 5% | 15% |
| Lead to sale rate (assumption) | 25% | 30% | 35% | 10% | 50% |
| Ceiling click price | $2.50 | $4.20 | $5.60 | $2.25 | $15.00 |

Formula: ceiling CPC = max cost per sale x lead rate x sale rate. For the intro offer: $200 x 0.08 x 0.35 = $5.60. The member rows assume a $100 monthly membership, so $200 is about two months of fees (revenue, not profit). The teacher training row assumes a $3,000 course, with 15% of it ($450) as the most you would spend to enrol one trainee.

What follows:

- **Compare the ceiling with a real estimate.** Open [Keyword Planner](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/7337243), set your city and read the average cost for each group. Google says you must complete account setup, including billing, to use it, and that very low-volume keywords may return nothing.
- **If the estimate is above the ceiling, change an input, not just the bid.** Add an offer, tighten the page, answer enquiries within minutes, or ask more per sale. Cutting the bid alone removes you from the auction.
- **Teacher training is a low-ceiling, high-ticket game.** A 10% enquiry-to-enrolment rate is a guess; most enquirers compare several programmes. Measure it before scaling.
- **Check retention.** A $200 limit is two months of a $100 fee, revenue not profit. If your members leave after two or three months, you break even at best, so lower the limit.

## FAQ

**Q: What are the best Google Ads keywords for a yoga studio?**
A: Local phrases that name your style or offer and your place, such as "hot yoga [city]", "prenatal yoga [neighborhood]" or "beginner yoga classes [city]". Which is best depends on your market, so test in Keyword Planner and judge by cost per new member.

**Q: Should I bid on "yoga classes near me"?**
A: Test it in its own ad group. Under the assumptions above its ceiling is the lowest of the class groups, so check the cost per member before you scale it.

**Q: Should I bid on "yoga teacher training"?**
A: Only if you run one, and in a separate campaign, because the same words are a negative in your class campaigns. Expect a different budget and geography.

**Q: Should I use broad match?**
A: Not at the start. Google ties broad match to Smart Bidding, which needs conversion data. Get tracking and a mature negative list first.
