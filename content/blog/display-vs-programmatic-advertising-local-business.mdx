Display advertising is the buying of banner and image ads on websites and apps, while programmatic advertising is the automated way those ads (and video, audio and other formats) are bought across many sites at once. Google Display Network is one type of display buying, and it is also technically programmatic, so the real choice for a local business is between a simple self-serve tool like Google Ads and a more powerful, more expensive platform called a DSP.

Our honest recommendation: most coffee shops, salons, groomers and studios should start with Google Ads search campaigns and a well-optimized Google Business Profile before touching either. This article explains the difference in plain English, when each makes sense, and what budgets to expect.

## What is display advertising?

Display advertising means showing visual ads, usually banners, images or short animations, on websites, apps and video pages that people are already visiting. Nobody typed a search; your ad simply appears next to the content they are reading.

For a local business, display is usually about awareness and reminders. A person reads a recipe blog, sees your cafe's ad, and maybe remembers your name next time they are hungry for a flat white. It rarely produces the instant, ready-to-buy clicks that search does.

The most common way small businesses buy display is the **Google Display Network (GDN)**, which you run inside your normal Google Ads account. Google says the network reaches a very large share of internet users through partner sites, apps and Gmail and YouTube placements, but check Google's current documentation for the exact claims. For a deeper walkthrough, see our [Google Display Network guide](/blog/google-display-network-guide).

## What is programmatic advertising?

Programmatic advertising is the use of software to buy ad space automatically, in real time, instead of a person negotiating with each website. When a page loads, an auction happens in a fraction of a second, and the highest relevant bidder gets the ad slot.

The software that buys the ads on your behalf is called a **DSP (demand-side platform)**. Examples include The Trade Desk, StackAdapt, and Google's own DV360. A DSP can buy banners, online video, connected TV, audio and more, and it can target using data sources that go beyond Google's own.

That last point matters: Google Display Network buys through Google's own system, while a DSP can reach inventory on many exchanges, including streaming TV and audio, and often offers more targeting and reporting options. If you want the full picture, our guide on [display advertising vs programmatic for local business](/blog/display-advertising-vs-programmatic-local-business) goes deeper on the mechanics.

## The simple difference

Think of it like shopping for coffee beans.

- **Display (GDN)** is like ordering from one big, well-organized supplier with an easy website. You pick what you want, set a budget and go. Fast to start, limited to what that supplier carries.
- **Programmatic (DSP)** is like using a broker who can source beans from many suppliers, with more choice, more detail on where each batch came from, and more cost and complexity.

Neither is automatically better. The right one depends on your budget, your goals and how much time you have to manage it.

## Quick comparison

| Factor | Google Display Network | Programmatic via a DSP |
|---|---|---|
| Setup | Inside Google Ads, self-serve | Separate platform, often managed |
| Typical minimums | Usually none; you set a daily budget | Often higher; check current pricing and platform policies |
| Formats | Banners, responsive display, some video | Banners, video, connected TV, audio, native and more |
| Local targeting | Radius and location targeting available | Location and sometimes ZIP-level targeting, varies by platform |
| Best for | Retargeting and low-cost awareness | Broader campaigns, streaming and audio reach |
| Skill required | Beginner to intermediate | Intermediate to advanced |
| Management effort | Low to moderate | Moderate to high |

Prices and minimums change often. Always confirm current numbers with the platform or a vendor before committing.

## When a local business should use Google Display Network

GDN is the more practical starting point of the two for small local businesses. It makes the most sense in these situations:

- **Retargeting.** You show ads to people who already visited your website, so they are reminded to book or visit. This is often the strongest display use for small budgets.
- **You already run search ads.** If your search campaigns are working and you have a steady flow of website visitors, display can add reminders on top.
- **You have a visual offer.** A new menu item, a seasonal package or a grand opening looks good as an image ad.
- **You can set tight location targeting.** Reaching people within a few miles of a single location keeps waste lower.

Be careful with defaults. Display campaigns can spend money on poorly matched sites and apps, so use placement exclusions, set frequency caps, and review where your ads actually appeared. Many first-time advertisers find a share of their budget going to low-quality placements if they never check.

## When a local business should consider programmatic and a DSP

Programmatic becomes worth a look when:

- **You have more than a few locations**, or a regional brand with a larger media budget.
- **You want connected TV or streaming audio**, which are generally bought through DSPs rather than Google Ads. Our [audio ads vs display ads](/blog/audio-ads-vs-display-ads-local-business) article covers how audio compares.
- **You need specific audience or location data** that GDN does not offer.
- **You have someone to run it**, either in house or an agency or managed service that works with small advertisers.

Most major DSPs were built for agencies and big brands, and many have minimum spend requirements or work only through managed service. Some platforms and resellers are friendlier to small advertisers than others, but if a platform asks for a monthly commitment that dwarfs your whole marketing budget, that is a sign it is not built for you yet. ## Budgets and minimums: what to expect

We will not invent exact figures here, because they vary by country, season, audience and platform. Here is how to think about it instead.

**Google Display Network**
- There is typically no required minimum spend; you choose a daily budget.
- Costs per click and per thousand views are generally lower than on search, but conversions are also typically less direct. Check current pricing and benchmarks in your own account.
- A small test over several weeks with retargeting only is a reasonable way to learn without big risk.

**Programmatic with a DSP**
- Many DSPs and managed services set monthly minimums, and those typically assume a budget larger than a single small shop would spend on all marketing. Always ask for current minimums in writing.
- On top of media costs, there can be platform fees, data fees and management fees. Ask for a full breakdown.
- Reporting is richer, but richer reporting only helps if someone has time to read and act on it.

## Why search and Google Business Profile usually come first

Here is the honest part. For most local businesses, both display and programmatic are second-step channels. The reason is intent.

When someone searches "coffee shop near me," "dog groomer open Saturday" or "yoga studio for beginners," they are looking for exactly what you sell, right now. Search ads and your Google Business Profile (GBP) put you in front of those people at that moment. Display and programmatic interrupt people who were not necessarily looking for you.

A sensible order for most local businesses looks like this:

1. **Fix your Google Business Profile.** It is free, shows up in map results, and drives calls, directions and website visits. Our [Google Business Profile optimization checklist](/blog/google-business-profile-optimization-checklist) walks through it.
2. **Run a small, tightly local Google Ads search campaign.** Target the high-intent searches in your radius. See our [Google Ads service page](/services/google-ads) for how we approach this.
3. **Track results.** Make sure calls, form submissions or bookings are measured, so you know what is working.
4. **Add retargeting display** once your website gets steady visits.
5. **Consider programmatic** only when you have budget, multiple locations or a specific goal (like streaming audio) that search cannot meet.

Skipping steps one and two to jump into programmatic is one of the most common ways small businesses burn budget. Awareness ads are only useful if the places people land (your profile, website and booking flow) are ready to convert them.

## Examples by business type

These are general patterns, not case studies.

- **Coffee shop:** Start with GBP and local search ads for terms like "coffee near me." Add display retargeting for a loyalty offer or a new location opening. Programmatic is rarely needed for a single cafe.
- **Hair salon:** Search ads for "balayage near me" or similar service searches, plus strong photos and reviews on GBP. Display can promote a seasonal offer to people who visited your booking page.
- **Pet groomer:** Search captures urgent, high-intent needs. Display can remind recent website visitors about booking a regular appointment.
- **Fitness studio:** Search ads for "yoga classes near me," then retargeting for people who viewed trial offers. A multi-location studio chain may eventually test programmatic or streaming audio.

## A simple test plan if you want to try display

If you decide to experiment, keep it small and measurable.

1. Make sure your website has conversion tracking for calls, forms or bookings.
2. Build a retargeting audience of website visitors.
3. Create a few simple image ads that include your location, offer and a clear call to action.
4. Restrict location tightly around your business.
5. Set a modest daily budget and a frequency cap.
6. Run for several weeks, then check placements, visits and conversions before deciding whether to continue.

If you want to test programmatic, ask any vendor these questions: What is the monthly minimum? What are all the fees on top of media? Can I see where my ads ran? How do you measure local visits or bookings? What happens if I stop after one month?

## FAQ

**Q: Is Google Display Network programmatic advertising?**
A: In a technical sense, yes, because ads are bought through automated auctions. In everyday marketing language, though, people usually mean programmatic as buying through a separate DSP that can reach more exchanges and formats than Google's own network.

**Q: Which is cheaper for a small local business, display or programmatic?**
A: Google Display Network is typically easier to start with a small budget because it usually has no required minimum. Programmatic through a DSP often carries higher minimums and fees, so check current pricing before you commit.

**Q: Do display ads work for local businesses?**
A: They can, mainly for retargeting and reminders, especially when paired with search ads and a strong Google Business Profile. As a standalone first channel, they tend to be less direct than search because people are not actively looking for your service.

**Q: Should I choose display or programmatic if I only have one location?**
A: Probably neither at first. A single-location business usually gets more from a Google Business Profile and local search ads, and can add display retargeting later if the basics are working.

**Q: Can a DSP target people near my shop?**
A: Many can target by location, but precision and methods vary by platform. Ask the vendor how they define local targeting and how they verify it.
