Start with one number. LocaliQ's 2025 home services benchmark puts the median cost per lead for Construction & Contractors at $165.67. Suppose half of those leads are real, quotable jobs and you win 3 in 10 quotes. Then a booked job costs about $1,100 in ads. Whether that is a bargain or a disaster depends on one thing most concrete crews never write down: what a job actually leaves in your pocket.

This guide is for concrete contractors in the US, UK, Australia and Canada: driveways, patios, slabs, stamped work and repairs. It does not repeat the setup steps in our [Google Ads for contractors](/blog/google-ads-for-contractors) guide. Instead it answers a different question: given how seasonal and quote-driven concrete is, where should the next hour and the next dollar go?

## Why Concrete Marketing Is Different

Three things set concrete apart from, say, a plumbing call-out.

- **Quote-driven, not emergency-driven.** A cracked driveway is annoying, not an emergency, so homeowners collect two or three quotes. The winner is often the one who replies first and looks most credible.
- **Weather decides when you can work, not when people search.** People plan patios in autumn and winter for spring. If you only market when you can pour, you miss the booking window.
- **Few categories exist for you in the ad platforms.** Concrete is not a named line in the LocaliQ report, and we could not confirm that Google's Local Services Ads cover flatwork or driveway pours (Google lists categories such as Foundations pro and General contractor). Check your area with Google's eligibility tool before planning around LSAs, and see our [Local Services Ads guide](/blog/google-local-services-ads-complete-guide).

## What a Job Is Worth

HomeAdvisor's cost guide (page dated June 2026, though its HTML title says 2025 data) reports an average concrete driveway cost of $6,400, with a typical range of $2,700 to $14,500. It lists plain finishes at $6 to $8 per square foot, stamped concrete at $12 to $18, and says labour is about half the bill. These are US national figures from a lead marketplace, so check them against your own last ten invoices before using them.

The number that matters for ad decisions is not revenue, it is gross profit per job after materials, labour, equipment and delivery. Every figure in the next section hinges on it.

## Step 1: Win the Map Before You Pay for Clicks

Free and cheap channels come first because they compound, and because paid leads at $166 each are expensive when the profile that converts them is weak.

**Set up the profile for how you actually work.** Google's [Business Profile guidelines for service-area businesses](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177) say that businesses without a staffed storefront should hide their address, and that the service area should not extend farther than about 2 hours of driving from where you are based. Google also says to use as few categories as possible and to pick categories that are specific but representative of your main business. In practice: one primary category that says what you are (look for a concrete contractor option in the dropdown for your country), and secondary ones only for work you genuinely sell. Use our [profile checklist](/blog/google-business-profile-optimization-checklist) to work through photos, services and descriptions, and [local SEO for contractors](/blog/local-seo-for-contractors) for the wider picture.

**Photos beat paragraphs.** Concrete is a visual trade. Upload finished jobs, the forms before the pour, and the crew on site. Name the town in the caption when the customer has agreed.

**Ask for reviews the legal way.** Google's [review policy](https://support.google.com/business/answer/2622994) lets you ask customers for reviews that reflect a genuine experience, but prohibits offering incentives such as discounts or free services, selectively asking only happy customers, and discouraging negative reviews. A clean routine: text the review link the same day the job finishes to every customer, not just the ones who thanked you. More in [our review guide](/blog/google-business-profile-reviews).

## Step 2: Make Winter Your Booking Season

Cold weather limits when you can place concrete, which is exactly why quoting and deposits should happen earlier. The American Concrete Institute defines cold weather concreting as conditions where the air temperature has fallen to, or is expected to fall below, 40°F (4°C) during the protection period. A technical bulletin from the US National Institutes of Health Office of Research Facilities, quoting ACI 306R-16, adds that below 40°F the hydration reaction slows and concrete stops gaining strength, and that concrete can lose up to 50% of its ultimate strength if it freezes at an early age. It says concrete should be protected from freezing until it reaches 500 psi, which takes about 48 hours for most concrete kept at 50°F. It also advises keeping surfaces in contact with the concrete free of snow and ice and above freezing.

The marketing takeaway is not "never pour in winter". The bulletin says good results are possible in the coldest weather with precautions. It is that many weeks you cannot (or should not) pour, and those are the weeks to:

- Quote spring jobs now and take a deposit to hold a start window.
- Email past customers about sealing and repairs (see [email marketing for small business](/blog/email-marketing-for-small-business)).
- Photograph and caption last season's work, and ask for the reviews you forgot.
- Post "booking spring" updates on your profile.

## Step 3: Speed and Follow-Up Win Quotes

With quote-driven work, the second most valuable minute is the one after the enquiry arrives. We could not find a verified concrete-specific response-time study, so we will not quote one. The logic is simple, though: the homeowner who filled in three forms talks to whoever answers first. Set a rule you can keep, such as a call back within the hour during working hours, and a text-back for calls you miss. Then follow up on every quote at day 3 and day 10. Many quotes are lost to silence, not price.

Track the source of every enquiry. Our [call tracking guide](/blog/call-tracking-for-local-business) shows how, and a notebook with columns for date, source, job type, quote and outcome is enough to start.

## Step 4: Referrals From the Trades

Builders, landscapers, fencing crews, plumbers and estate agents see jobs before the homeowner has googled anything. Offer them something real: reliable start dates, a text when you are on the way, and photos for their own portfolios. Do not pay cash for referrals without checking local rules. ## The Math

All rows are **assumptions** except the $165.67 benchmark cost per lead (a general-contractor median from LocaliQ) and the $6,400 average job (HomeAdvisor, national average). Replace everything with your own numbers.

| | Tough | Base | Strong |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cost per lead (Base = LocaliQ median; others assumed) | $250 | $166 | $100 |
| Leads that are real, quotable jobs (assumption) | 40% | 50% | 60% |
| Quotes won (assumption) | 20% | 30% | 40% |
| Leads needed per job | 12.5 | 6.7 | 4.2 |
| Ad cost per job | $3,125 | $1,105 | $417 |
| Job value (HomeAdvisor average) | $6,400 | $6,400 | $6,400 |
| Gross profit at 25% (assumption) | $1,600 | $1,600 | $1,600 |
| Profit after ad cost | -$1,525 | +$495 | +$1,183 |

How to read it: the same job loses $1,525 or earns $1,183 depending on lead quality and close rate. Your break-even cost per lead is gross profit per job multiplied by the share of leads that become jobs. In the Base column that is $1,600 x 15% = $240. Pay more than that per lead and you lose money on average.

Notice what moves the answer most. Going from a 20% to a 40% win rate halves your cost per job without touching your ad account. That is why quote follow-up sits above paid search in this guide. If your margin is nearer 15% than 25%, the Base column drops to about -$145 per job, so know your real margin.

## Do This Today

1. Pull your last ten invoices and work out the average gross profit per job. Use it to calculate your break-even cost per lead with the formula above.
2. Open your Google Business Profile. Hide the address if you have no staffed yard, check the primary category, and upload ten recent job photos.
3. Text your review link to every customer from the last 60 days, with no incentive.
4. Write a one-page spring pour plan: three quote follow-up messages (day 1, day 3, day 10) and a deposit offer for booking start dates.
5. Start a lead log with date, source, job type, quote and outcome. After 30 days, decide whether paid search or a marketplace deserves a test.

## FAQ

**Q: What is the best marketing for a concrete company?**
A: Start with a complete Google Business Profile, steady reviews and fast quote follow-up, then add paid search or referrals. Which channel pays is a matter of your own cost per job, so measure it.

**Q: How much do concrete leads cost?**
A: No concrete-specific public benchmark was found. LocaliQ's median for Construction & Contractors is $165.67 per lead, and one agency suggests staying under $100 without citing evidence. Use your own tracked numbers.

**Q: Are lead marketplaces worth it?**
A: Possibly, but leads can be shared with competitors and charged per lead. Compare cost per won job against your other channels with the math above.

**Q: Can I market in winter if I cannot pour?**
A: Yes. Winter is when homeowners plan spring projects, so quoting, deposits, reviews and email to past customers all work while the ground is cold.

**Q: Do I need a page for every town?**
A: Only where you genuinely work and can add real local detail, such as photos of jobs there. Near-duplicate pages help nobody.
