Accountants should use social media to stay visible to people who already know them, not to chase strangers. Referrals are usually the main source of new clients, so the job of a post is to remind past clients, colleagues and local business owners that you are the person to recommend.

Start with one professional platform (LinkedIn), keep a simple posting rhythm, and follow the advertising rules that apply to tax practitioners.

This guide covers which platforms matter, what to post, the IRS rules on advertising, and a worked referral example with every number labelled as an assumption.

## Which Social Platforms Should an Accountant Use?

Pew Research's [social media fact sheet](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/fact-sheet/social-media/) shows how many US adults use each platform: Facebook 71% in 2025, Instagram 50% in 2025 and LinkedIn 32% in 2024, up from 28% in 2021. LinkedIn reaches fewer people but it is where professional contacts, other advisers and business owners check credentials.

Xero's [guide for accountants](https://www.xero.com/us/accountant-bookkeeper-guides/business-networking) says referrals consistently rank as the top source of new clients and calls LinkedIn the most effective social platform for professional services. That is a vendor's opinion with no statistics, so treat it as a view, not a measurement.

A practical split: LinkedIn for your professional network, a Facebook business page for local discovery and reviews, and nothing else until those two run smoothly.

## What Should an Accountant Post?

Aim for posts that answer questions clients already ask. Three types cover most needs:

- **A question from this week**, with the identifying details removed. "A client asked whether a home office is deductible. Here is how I think about it."
- **A deadline reminder** with a link to the official source.
- **A plain-language explanation** of one term, such as estimated payments or sales tax registration.

Keep each post short, one idea each, and avoid advice that depends on someone's personal situation. End with a prompt to message you, not a hard sell. ## What Do the IRS Rules Say About Advertising?

If you practise before the IRS, [31 CFR 10.30](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/10.30) governs how you advertise. Read the full text, but the key points are:

- No false, fraudulent, coercive, misleading or deceptive statements or claims.
- Enrolled agents and registered tax return preparers may not call themselves "certified" or suggest an employment relationship with the IRS.
- Lawful solicitations must be clearly identified as solicitations.
- You may publish fixed fees, hourly rates, fee ranges and the fee for an initial consultation, and must charge no more than the published rates for at least 30 calendar days after publication.
- For direct mail and e-commerce communications, keep a copy and a list or description of recipients for at least 36 months.
- You must stop contacting a prospect who says they do not want to be solicited.

State boards and professional bodies have their own rules. This is not legal advice, so check with your licensing body before you publish pricing or testimonials.

## How Do Posts Turn Into Referrals?

A post rarely produces a client directly. It keeps you in mind. When a client mentions a friend with a tax problem, your recent post is what comes to mind. Three habits help:

1. Ask happy clients for a LinkedIn recommendation or Google review.
2. Comment on posts by local business owners, lawyers and bankers, who all refer accountants.
3. Reply to every comment and message within a working day.

## What Is a Referral Worth? A Worked Example

All figures below are **assumptions** for illustration, not benchmarks.

| Item | Assumption |
| --- | --- |
| Posts per week | 2 |
| Time per post | 20 minutes |
| Time per month | About 3 hours 20 minutes |
| Referral conversations started per month | 1 |
| Share of conversations that become clients | 40% |
| Annual fee per new client | $1,500 |

At these numbers you win about 0.4 clients a month, or roughly 5 a year, worth around $7,200 in annual fees. Your cost is the time plus any scheduling tool. If you get one conversation every three months instead, the yearly total falls to about 1.6 clients, around $2,400. Track how each new client heard of you, and ask on the intake form.

## What Mistakes Do Accountants Make on Social Media?

- Posting tax law updates nobody asked for.
- Trying to run five platforms at once.
- Disappearing for two months, then posting daily.
- Using social media as a billboard and ignoring replies.
- Sharing client details, even in disguised form, without permission. Confidentiality comes first.

## FAQ

**Q: Is social media worth it for a solo accountant?**
A: It can be, mainly for referrals and credibility. Keep the effort small, at around an hour or two a week.

**Q: Which platform is best?**
A: LinkedIn for professional contacts, plus a Facebook page if you serve local consumers. Do not add more until these work.

**Q: Can I post client testimonials?**
A: Check your licensing body's rules and get written permission first. Never imply guaranteed outcomes.

**Q: Can I publish my fees?**
A: IRS rules allow published fixed fees, hourly rates and ranges, with the 30-day rule above. Check state rules too.

**Q: How do I know it is working?**
A: Ask every new client how they found you and record the answer.
