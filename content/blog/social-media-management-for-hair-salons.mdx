Social media management for a small business is commonly quoted at $500 to $2,000 a month (eClincher's 2026 estimate, which cites no survey). Suppose a new regular is worth about $225 in first-year profit to your salon. Then that retainer has to bring in somewhere between 2 and 9 new regulars every month before it earns its keep. The $225 is my assumption, and the sums are below. The point: managing social media is a hiring decision, so judge it like one.

This guide is for salon owners in the US, UK, Australia and Canada who are deciding whether to run social media themselves, hand it to a stylist, hire a freelancer or sign with an agency. It covers what the job actually involves, what each route costs, what to put in a brief, and how to tell whether it is working. For the platform-specific plans, see our guides to [salon TikTok](/blog/tiktok-marketing-for-hair-salons) and [Meta ads for salons](/blog/using-meta-ads-for-hair-salon-marketing); this one is about the management layer around them.

## What Your Clients Actually Do on Social

Start with where your clients are. Pew Research Center's 2025 survey of 5,022 US adults (fielded February to June 2025) found that 78% of women use Facebook, 55% use Instagram and 42% use TikTok ([Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/fact-sheet/social-media/)). For men the figures are 63%, 44% and 30%.

Use is not the same as discovery, though. A Phorest Salon Software survey of Americans, reported by Salon Today in 2022, found that 72% of people looking for a new hair salon searched on Google and 25% searched on social media. It also found 49% said online reviews and reputation influenced their choice of current salon ([Salon Today](https://www.salontoday.com/articles/49-of-americans-influenced-by-online-reviews-when-choosing-a-salon)). The article gives no sample size and the survey is a few years old, so treat it as direction, not gospel.

BrightLocal's 2026 Local Consumer Review Survey (1,002 US adults) adds a useful piece: 24% of consumers visit a business's social media channels after reading positive reviews ([BrightLocal](https://www.brightlocal.com/research/local-consumer-review-survey/)). So for most salons, social media does two jobs. It is the profile people check before they book, and it is the channel that keeps existing clients thinking of you. Planning your management around those two jobs stops you from posting into the void.

## What "Managing" Social Media Actually Involves

When someone quotes you for management, they are pricing five separate tasks. Know which ones you are buying:

1. **Planning.** Deciding what to post this month, tied to your quiet days and the services you want to sell.
2. **Creating.** Filming, photographing, editing and writing. This is the slowest part, and the one an outsider cannot do without access to your chairs.
3. **Publishing.** Scheduling posts and stories across platforms.
4. **Community.** Answering comments and direct messages, usually where bookings start.
5. **Reporting.** Counting what came of it: profile visits, link clicks, booking enquiries and new clients.

The trap is that an outside manager can easily take over tasks 1, 3 and 5 but struggles with task 2, because the best content comes from inside the salon. And task 4 needs someone who knows your diary and prices. Decide who owns each task before you decide who to hire.

## Four Ways to Run It, and What They Cost

| Option | Typical cost | Best for | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| You, the owner | Your time | Tight budgets and small teams | Falls off when the diary fills |
| A stylist or front-desk lead | A few paid hours a week | Salons with someone who already posts well | Needs time off the floor, written guidelines and a backup |
| A freelancer | See ranges below | Owners who want content planning and publishing handled | Needs a regular salon shoot day |
| An agency | See ranges below | Multi-chair or multi-site salons | Generic content if they never visit |

On price, published ranges vary and are not independent surveys, so use them to sense-check quotes, not to set a budget:

- **US:** eClincher estimates $500 to $2,000 a month for basic small-business management, typically one to two platforms, around 8 to 12 posts, light engagement and simple reports. It gives freelancer retainers of about $300 to $800 for beginners and $1,500 to $3,000 or more for seasoned professionals, and agency retainers from about $2,000 ([eClincher](https://eclincher.com/articles/social-media-management-pricing-rates-and-costs-for-2026)).
- **UK:** Whito's roundup, which combines published agency and freelancer pricing and does not state a sample size, puts most UK businesses at £500 to £1,500 a month. It lists experienced freelancers at £500 to £1,200 and small agencies at £800 to £2,000, and says paid social management is usually billed separately from the figures above ([Whito](https://whito.co.uk/research/uk-social-media-management-costs/)). For Australia and Canada, no comparable public source was reviewed, so get three local quotes.

Ad spend is on top of any management fee. If you plan to run ads, read our [organic vs paid social media test](/blog/organic-vs-paid-social-media) before you commit to a retainer.

## Write a Brief Before You Hire Anyone

Most disappointing management deals come from a vague brief. Whether it is a freelancer, a stylist or an agency, put these in writing:

- **The goal.** For example, "12 new-client bookings a month from Instagram and Facebook", not "grow our brand".
- **The scope.** Platforms, posts and stories per week, who films, who replies to messages, and what happens out of hours.
- **Access.** You should stay the owner of every account and the admin on your pages. Never hand over your only login. That is my advice, not a platform rule, but it saves a painful fight if you part ways.
- **Client consent.** Photos and video of clients need permission. Add a tick box to your booking form, and offer a hair-only option. Our [salon TikTok guide](/blog/tiktok-marketing-for-hair-salons) walks through the consent wording.
- **Reporting.** A monthly one-page summary of reach, link clicks, enquiries and bookings that mention social. Likes are not a report.
- **Notice period.** A rolling 30-day contract for the first three months is a reasonable thing to ask for.

Questions to ask any provider: Who will film? Who will see our messages, and how fast? What did the last salon client get, and can I speak to them? If an agency only shows engagement figures and cannot tell you how many bookings came from its work, keep looking. ## A Weekly Rhythm That Fits a Busy Salon

Whoever runs it, the schedule can be small:

- **Once a month (45 minutes):** pick four to eight things worth showing, such as a transformation, a stylist introduction, a colour tip, a quiet-day offer.
- **Once a week (45 minutes):** film or photograph two to four items on a quiet morning. Capture the result from the front and back, in the same light.
- **Two or three times a week:** publish.
- **Daily (10 minutes):** answer every comment and message. A salon that replies in an hour wins bookings from the salon that replies in two days.
- **Once a month (30 minutes):** count new clients by source and compare with the cost.

Mix content types. A transformation shows the work, a short answer to a client question shows expertise, and a behind-the-scenes clip shows the people. Balance promotion with useful or fun posts.
## What to Measure

Reach is the first number owners look at, and it is easy to misread. Socialinsider's 2026 reach study (872,075 posts from brand pages, January 2025 to August 2026) found that Instagram accounts with 1,000 to 5,000 followers reached 6.65% of followers per post, and Facebook accounts of the same size 4.35% ([Socialinsider](https://www.socialinsider.io/blog/social-media-reach/)). The same page puts Instagram reach by format at 4.50% for carousels, 4.10% for Reels and 4.00% for images, though it is internally inconsistent on some headline averages. So a salon with 1,500 Instagram followers might expect to reach roughly 100 of them per post (1,500 x 6.65%). That is why consistent posting to an engaged local audience matters more than a large following.

The numbers that matter more:

- **Booking-link clicks and profile visits** from your platform insights.
- **Enquiries and bookings that mention social media.** Ask every new client "How did you find us?" and write it down. This is the only way to see clients who watched a Reel three weeks ago and then walked in.
- **Your reply time** in the inbox.
- **Rebookings.** Social is as much about keeping existing clients as finding new ones. Our guide on [why salon clients ghost you](/blog/why-salon-clients-ghost-you-and-how-to-stop-it) covers what to do when they drift.

## The Math

Every figure below is an **assumption** for illustration, apart from the price ranges quoted above. Replace them with your own.

| | Owner does it | Freelancer | Agency |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Monthly cash cost (assumption, inside the quoted ranges) | $0 | $800 | $2,000 |
| Your time (hours per month, assumption) | 20 | 4 | 3 |
| Value of your time at $40/hour (assumption) | $800 | $160 | $120 |
| Total monthly cost | $800 | $960 | $2,120 |
| Average ticket (assumption) | $90 | $90 | $90 |
| Visits in first year (assumption) | 5 | 5 | 5 |
| Profit margin (assumption) | 50% | 50% | 50% |
| First-year profit per new regular | $225 | $225 | $225 |
| New regulars needed per month to break even | 3.6 | 4.3 | 9.4 |

How to read it: break-even is total monthly cost divided by first-year profit per regular, $960 / $225 = 4.3 for the freelancer. If your own log shows only one or two social clients a month, the lesson is not "social media does not work". It is that you are overpaying for the current result, so improve the offer and booking path first, or cut the scope. Two things could change the picture: a higher average ticket (colour clients are worth more than $90) and rebooking, because a regular who comes back eight times a year has a much larger first-year profit. Salons with a full book should also remember that new clients only help if you have chair time for them.

## Do This Today

1. Write down who owns each of the five tasks: planning, creating, publishing, replying and reporting.
2. Add a social media consent tick box (with a hair-only option) and a "How did you find us?" question to your booking form.
3. Check that you are the admin of your Facebook page and Instagram account, and that your login details are not held only by one stylist or supplier.
4. Pull the last 10 posts' reach from your insights and compare it with the 6.65% and 4.35% small-account figures above.
5. If you are hiring, request three quotes and ask each one for scope, reporting and a 30-day notice period in writing.

## FAQ

**Q: How much does social media management cost for a hair salon?**
A: Published estimates for US small businesses run from about $500 to $2,000 a month (eClincher), and in the UK mostly £500 to £1,500 (Whito). These are roundups, not audited surveys, and ad spend is extra. Get three local quotes.

**Q: How often should a salon post?**
A: Choose a pace you can keep for 90 days. Two to three posts a week is a workable starting point (my suggestion, not a platform rule). Replies matter as much as posting.

**Q: Should I let a stylist run the account?**
A: It can work well because they are in the room, but protect their time, give them written guidelines and make sure the account is administered by you. See the brief section above.

**Q: Which platform should a salon prioritise?**
A: The one your clients use. Pew's US data shows 78% of women use Facebook and 55% use Instagram; check your own client mix and ask new clients where they found you.

**Q: Is social media or Google more important for new clients?**
A: In the Phorest survey, far more people searched Google (72%) than social media (25%) for a new salon. That suggests doing both, and keeping your [Google Business Profile](/blog/google-business-profile-optimization-for-hair-salons) as complete as your social profiles.
