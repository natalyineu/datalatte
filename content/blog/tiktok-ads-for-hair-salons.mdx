A TikTok ad test for a hair salon is worth running only if two things are true: a meaningful share of the salon's clients use the app, and the salon can measure whether a click becomes a booking. Pew Research's 2026 survey puts TikTok use among US adults at 37%, the same level as 2025 and up from 21% in 2021. This guide shows how to check the client mix, price a 14-day test with assumption-labelled inputs, and decide on cost per booking rather than views.

## Are your clients on TikTok at all?

The national figure is only a starting point. Pew reports that 37% of US adults use TikTok, while Instagram sits at 51% and Facebook at 70%. Those numbers show that a large share of potential clients can be reached on TikTok, but not all of them. Nextdoor, which is built around neighbourhoods, reaches 18% of adults and has grown since 2021.

Before spending anything, pull the age profile and postcodes of the last 100 bookings from the booking software. If most revenue comes from clients who live close to the salon and find it through Facebook or Google, TikTok may be a secondary channel. If newer clients are under 40 and discovered the salon through short videos, a test is worth running. Pew's figures used here do not break down TikTok use by age, so the salon should not assume its own clients match the national average.

## What budget should a salon set for a TikTok test?

Platform budget minimums change over time, and this article does not quote them. Check the current minimum for campaigns and ad groups in Ads Manager before planning. Then build the test budget around an amount the salon can afford to lose if the test produces no bookings.

The worked example below uses assumptions to show the method. Replace every input with real numbers.

The inputs are:

- Average ticket: $85 for a cut and style (assumption)
- Profit kept per first visit after product, chair rent share and payment fees: $40 (assumption)
- Test spend: $300 over 14 days (assumption)
- Cost per click: $1.00 (assumption)
- Click-to-booking rate: 3% (assumption)

Under these inputs, the test produces about 300 clicks and 9 bookings. Cost per booking is $300 divided by 9, or about $33. Booked revenue is 9 x $85, or $765. Because profit kept per first visit is $40, the 9 bookings contribute $360, which is $60 more than the $300 spent. That figure is a first-visit result and holds only if the bookings are not cancelled.

## How do you measure a booking from a TikTok ad?

If the salon cannot tell which bookings came from TikTok, the test cannot be judged. Use a tracking link for the ad destination, or ask every new client how they heard about the salon at booking. Record the answer in the booking system so the count is available at day 14.

Send ad clicks directly to the booking page, not the homepage or social profile. Every extra step between the ad and the calendar loses people. If the booking tool does not allow tracking parameters, the question at booking becomes the only measurement, so keep a simple sheet with the date and the ad that was running.

Meta's guidance on testing applies the same logic to any paid channel. Its help page recommends using the same budget for both versions of a test so the comparison is fair. It also warns against informal testing, such as switching ads on and off by hand, because that can create overlapping audiences and unreliable results ([Meta, About A/B testing for Meta Ads](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/1738164643098669)). The same discipline applies here: one ad group, equal budget per creative, and a fixed end date.

## Which salon services suit TikTok ads?

Short vertical video works best for visible transformations. Colour work, balayage and extensions show a clear before and after within fifteen seconds. Cuts with a dramatic change also work. Services that are hard to show on screen, such as a standard trim or a relaxing treatment, usually perform poorly as paid content.

Pick one offer for the test rather than the whole menu. A consultation or a first-visit price gives a clear reason to book now and makes the result easier to read. Mixing several services in one test makes it hard to tell which one drove the bookings. Organic content can support the offer, and [the influencer approach to free salon bookings](/blog/influencer-marketing-for-hair-salons-how-to-get-free-bookings-from-local-creator) shows another way to get video in front of local people without ad spend.

## How does break-even math change the result?

The ceiling for cost per booking is the profit kept per first visit, not the ticket value. The table below applies the same $300 test spend and $40 profit kept per visit to three scenarios. All inputs are assumptions.

| Scenario (assumptions) | Cost per click | Click-to-booking rate | Clicks | Bookings | Cost per booking | Profit from bookings minus spend |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Base case | $1.00 | 3.0% | 300 | 9 | $33.33 | +$60 |
| Expensive clicks | $2.00 | 3.0% | 150 | 4.5 | $66.67 | -$120 |
| Weak offer or page | $1.00 | 1.5% | 300 | 4.5 | $66.67 | -$120 |

The base case clears break-even only because cost per booking stays below the $40 ceiling. The other two rows fail for different reasons: the first has an ad problem, and the second has an offer or booking page problem. Reading the table this way tells the salon where to act before spending more. If clicks are cheap but bookings are low, the offer or page needs work. If clicks are expensive, the video or targeting needs work.

If rebooking is common, the test may still pay off over time. Without rebooking data, judge the test on first-visit profit only.

## How do you run the 14-day test?

The steps below keep the test simple enough to read at the end.

1. Days 1 to 2: confirm the booking link works on a phone and that a click lands on the booking page.
2. Days 1 to 3: film three short vertical videos from real work: a before-and-after, a fifteen-second colour process, and one offer clip.
3. Day 3: set one ad group with the service area as the location, an age range matched to the real client profile, and all three videos as separate ads at equal budget.
4. Days 4 to 13: check the numbers every few days, but do not switch ads off by hand or change the budget mid-test.
5. Day 14: pause any ad with clicks and no bookings, keep the one with the lowest cost per booking, and compare it with the break-even figure.

If the cheapest ad still costs more per booking than the profit kept per first visit, stop the test. The salon will have learned something useful without spending more.

## When should a salon skip TikTok ads?

Skip TikTok ads if clients are mostly older and rarely use short video, if the full test budget cannot be spent with no return, or if the booking link does not work on a phone. Those three problems hurt any paid channel, not only TikTok.

## FAQ

### Is TikTok worth it for a hair salon?

It depends on the clients. Pew reports 37% of US adults use TikTok, so the audience is large, but the salon's own booking records show whether its clients are on the app. Test only if that audience overlaps with the people who already book.

### How long should a TikTok ad test run?

Plan for 14 days, then judge the test on cost per booking rather than views or likes. Shorter tests rarely produce enough bookings to read.

### Do I need organic videos before running ads?

No platform requirement applies. Many people who see an ad will visit the profile, and an account with a few real videos looks more credible than an empty one. Keep organic posts simple and show real work.

### How do I target my city on TikTok?

Set the location in the ad group to the salon's service area. Keep the radius close to where clients actually live, which the booking records can confirm.

### What should a salon do if the test loses money?

Check whether the problem is the ad, the offer or the booking page. Cheap clicks with no bookings point to the offer or page. Expensive clicks point to the video or targeting. Fix one variable at a time before the next test.
