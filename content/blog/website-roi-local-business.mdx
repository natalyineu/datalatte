To measure your local business website's ROI, subtract what the website costs you (build, hosting, tools, ads and your time) from the profit it brings in, then divide by that cost. The hard part is not the maths. It is tracking which bookings, calls and sales actually came from the website, which takes a few simple tools and some consistent habits.

Here is a practical way to do it, even if you are not technical.

## The Basic ROI Formula

**ROI = (Profit from website - Cost of website) / Cost of website**

Use profit (or gross margin), not revenue. A website that brings in $10,000 of sales at a 40 percent margin has produced $4,000 of profit, and that is the number to compare with costs.

Example with round numbers: if the website brought in $4,000 of profit over a quarter and cost $1,000 to run in that quarter, the ROI is ($4,000 - $1,000) / $1,000 = 3, or 300 percent. These figures are only an illustration of the method, not a benchmark.

## Step 1: Add Up the Real Costs

Include everything, otherwise your ROI will look better than it is.

| Cost | Examples |
|---|---|
| One-off | Design and build, branding, photography, copywriting |
| Recurring | Domain (often around $10 to $20 a year), hosting (from a few dollars to $50 or more a month), website platform fees, booking software |
| Marketing | Google Ads or Meta ad spend, SEO help, email software |
| Time | Your hours or staff hours updating the site, at a fair hourly rate |
| Maintenance | Updates, security, small fixes, developer help |

Spread one-off costs across a sensible lifetime, such as 24 to 36 months, so each month carries a fair share.

## Step 2: Decide What a "Win" Is

A website has different jobs for different businesses. Choose the actions that matter:

- **Bookings or appointments** made online
- **Phone calls** from the site (tap-to-call)
- **Form submissions** or quote requests
- **Direction requests** to your location
- **Online orders or gift card sales**
- **Email sign-ups** that lead to later purchases

Give each action a rough value. Your average sale, or the lifetime value of a customer, is a useful anchor. If you do not know it, work it out from your records: average spend per visit multiplied by typical visits per year.

## Step 3: Set Up Tracking

You cannot measure ROI without data. The essentials:

1. **Google Analytics 4 (free):** install it and mark key actions as conversions, such as booking confirmations and form submissions. See our [GA4 guide for small businesses](/blog/google-analytics-4-for-small-business).
2. **Google Search Console (free):** shows what people searched before clicking to your site.
3. **Call tracking:** services like CallRail or a Google forwarding number (typically from about $40 a month for third-party tools) show which calls came from the site or ads.
4. **Booking platform reports:** Square, Fresha, Mindbody, Calendly and others often show how many bookings came via your website link.
5. **Your Google Business Profile insights:** website clicks, calls and direction requests.
6. **A simple question:** ask every new customer "How did you hear about us?" and log the answer.

## Step 4: Attribute Results Honestly

Attribution is imperfect. Some customers see your Instagram, search your name, visit the site, and then call. Different tools will give different answers. Use more than one source and look for patterns.

- **Direct attribution:** online bookings and form fills tracked in GA4 and your booking system.
- **Phone and walk-in attribution:** call tracking plus customer surveys.
- **Assisted value:** the website helped, even if the final step happened by phone or in person. Count a reasonable share, and be conservative.

If you need one rule: when unsure, under-count. A conservative ROI you can trust is more useful than a flattering guess.

## Step 5: Calculate a Month at a Time

Create a simple spreadsheet with one row per month:

| Column | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| Website sessions | From GA4 |
| Conversions | Bookings, calls, forms |
| Conversion rate | Conversions divided by sessions |
| Estimated customers won | Conversions that became paying customers |
| Average profit per customer | From your own records |
| Website profit | Customers won multiplied by profit per customer |
| Total website cost | From step 1 |
| ROI | (Profit - cost) / cost |

Your own history matters far more than any industry average. Watch how the figures move over several months rather than overreacting to one slow or busy period.

## Step 6: Look Beyond the Headline Number

ROI is a summary. These supporting metrics tell you where to improve:

- **Conversion rate:** the share of visitors who take a key action. Typical rates vary widely by business and traffic source, so compare against your own past performance.
- **Traffic source quality:** do Google search visitors convert better than social visitors?
- **Mobile experience:** if most visitors are on phones, test your booking flow on one.
- **Page speed and clarity:** slow, confusing pages leak customers.
- **Return visitors and repeat customers:** a site that helps retention has value beyond the first booking.

## Step 7: Improve the Return

You can raise ROI by increasing results or lowering costs. Common high-impact improvements:

- **Make the booking button impossible to miss,** on every page and on mobile.
- **Speed up the site** by compressing images and removing unused plugins.
- **Add trust signals:** real reviews, photos and clear prices. See our [trust signals guide](/blog/trust-signals-website).
- **Create service pages** for each core service and location, so search engines understand what you offer.
- **Capture emails** with a useful offer and follow up. See [email marketing for small business](/blog/email-marketing-for-small-business).
- **Fix leaks:** broken forms, missing confirmation messages and slow replies.

## When a Website Is Not the Right Place to Look

Some businesses get most of their customers from walk-ins, referrals or Google Maps. That does not make the website worthless. It often supports those channels by confirming you are legitimate, showing hours and prices, and giving people a booking path. In that case, measure the website's role in assists and enquiries, and consider whether improving your Google Business Profile first would deliver more.

## FAQ

**Q: What is a good ROI for a local business website?**
A: There is no single benchmark. A good result is one where the profit it generates clearly exceeds what you spend on it, and trends upward over time. Track your own numbers month by month.

**Q: How do I track phone calls from my website?**
A: Use call tracking tools such as CallRail, or Google forwarding numbers if you run ads. Add a "How did you hear about us?" question for callers who do not use trackable numbers.

**Q: Should I include my time in the cost?**
A: Yes, at a fair hourly rate. Otherwise a site that eats your evenings may look profitable when it is not.

**Q: How long before a new website shows a return?**
A: It depends on whether you have traffic. A site with ads or existing audiences can show results in weeks, while an SEO-driven site often takes several months to build momentum.

## A Note on Patience and Review Cadence

Review your numbers monthly and do a deeper check each quarter. Short windows can mislead: a rainy fortnight, a school holiday or a one-off event can swing bookings without telling you anything about the website. Keep a short log of changes you make (new photos, a revised booking page, a new ad campaign) next to your monthly figures, so that when results move you have a good chance of understanding why.
