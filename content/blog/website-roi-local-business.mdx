To measure a local business website's ROI, take the profit the site brings in, subtract what it costs you, and divide by that cost: ROI = (profit minus cost) / cost. Cost covers the build or monthly plan, hosting, maintenance, any ads that send traffic to the site, and your own time. The hard part is attribution, meaning which calls, bookings and directions really came from the website. This guide covers the formula, the actions worth counting, the tracking steps, and a worked salon example where every input is an assumption.

## What is the website ROI formula?

Use this formula: ROI = (profit attributed to the website minus website cost) divided by website cost. Use profit, not revenue. Revenue is the money coming in, while profit is what remains after the cost of delivering the job. If a booking brings $90 and costs $40 to deliver, the profit is $50 (assumption for illustration).

Adwave draws a useful line between two metrics. Return on ad spend measures gross revenue against only the ad spend, while ROI accounts for total cost ([Adwave, how to measure marketing ROI](https://adwave.com/resources/how-to-measure-marketing-roi)). A website carries costs beyond any ad budget, so ROI fits the whole site better.

Website cost includes the build or plan, domain and hosting, maintenance, paid traffic and your time at a fair hourly rate. Ad costs are easier to size with a [local ad spend forecast](/blog/local-ad-spend-forecast-2026-small-business), and SEO costs follow the lines in the [local SEO pricing guide](/blog/how-much-does-local-seo-cost-for-a-small-business-honest-pricing-guide).

## Which website actions should you count?

Count actions that lead to money, not visits. For most local businesses that means calls, booking-form submissions, direction requests and orders. A page view tells you interest, but it does not show a sale.

Google describes Google Analytics as the go-to platform for millions of website and app owners, although it gives no exact count ([Google Analytics](https://developers.google.com/analytics)). Create an event for each action you want to measure and mark the important ones as key events. Menu names change over time, so check Google's current Analytics help pages for the exact steps.

Business Profile insights are a separate data source. Check the performance section of your dashboard for calls, direction requests and website clicks, and keep those numbers apart from events recorded on your own site.

## How do you know where visitors came from?

Tag every link you control. Use the same three campaign fields on each link: source, medium and campaign. Put tagged links in email signatures, social bios, ads, and the website link on your Business Profile. Consistent tags matter more than clever names, because a mismatched label splits one channel into several rows.

If you run Local Service Ads, Google provides a dashboard that shows leads generated and cost per lead, along with return on investment ([Accrisoft, secondary](https://www.accrisoft.com/blog/2024/06/03/service-industries/do-google-local-service-ads-work/)). Keep those paid leads separate from organic website results so the website figure is not inflated.

For more on finding the search traffic that feeds the site, see the [local SEO steps for 2026](/blog/how-to-improve-local-seo-for-your-small-business-15-steps-that-work-in-2026).

## What about calls and walk-ins?

Calls from the website are easy to count when you track taps on the phone number as an event. Walk-ins are harder. Ask "How did you hear about us?" at booking or checkout and record the answer in a simple sheet. The answers will not be perfect, but a consistent question beats guessing.

A tap on a call button shows demand, not a completed sale. Treat call counts as leads and only count a booking as revenue once it is paid or confirmed.

## Does mobile performance affect website ROI?

It can. Mozello's website launch checklist, cited by Adwave, says users are 61% more likely to return to sites that provide a good mobile experience ([Adwave, mobile website checklist, secondary](https://adwave.com/resources/mobile-website-design-checklist-for-local-businesses)). Returning visitors are cheaper to convert, so a weak mobile layout raises your cost per booking.

Check the basics on a phone. Adwave's mobile-first guidance sets buttons at a minimum of 9 mm by 9 mm ([Adwave, mobile-first design](https://adwave.com/resources/mobile-first-website-design)). Google also offers a free tool that tests whether a page is mobile-friendly ([Impact BC, secondary](https://www.impactbc.com.sg/ultimate-guide-digital-marketing-success/)).

Write clear booking and call copy too. The [website copy examples for local businesses](/blog/how-to-write-website-copy-local-business-examples) show how to make the next step obvious.

## Worked example: a salon website

Every input below is an **assumption** for illustration. Replace them with your own figures.

| Input (assumption) | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Monthly website cost (plan, hosting, maintenance) | $60 |
| Your time on updates, 2 hours at $25 | $50 |
| Total monthly cost | $110 |
| New bookings a month from website forms and taps | 14 |
| Profit per booking after costs | $38 |

Profit from the website is 14 x $38 = $532. ROI = ($532 - $110) / $110 = 3.84, or 384%.

Now test a stricter case. If only 5 of those bookings can be traced to the site, profit falls to 5 x $38 = $190 and ROI = ($190 - $110) / $110 = 0.73, or 73%. The site still pays back, but the gap shows how much attribution matters. Label each booking as confirmed or estimated before you report the result.

## Which mistakes distort website ROI?

- Counting all revenue instead of profit.
- Crediting the website with customers who would have booked anyway, such as regulars on a standing appointment.
- Leaving your own time out of the cost.
- Judging after one month for a service with a long decision cycle.
- Mixing paid-ad results into website results without separating the ad cost.
- Treating a call tap or a page view as a confirmed sale.

## FAQ

### What is a good ROI for a small business website?

No benchmark for local websites is cited here. Adwave describes a 3:1 to 5:1 return as often considered strong for response-focused TV campaigns ([Adwave, TV advertising ROI](https://adwave.com/resources/tv-advertising-roi-calculation)), but that is a TV figure, not a website one. Any ROI above zero means the site covers its cost, so start with your own break-even point.

### Should I count Google Business Profile calls?

Count them as demand from your online presence, not as tracked website results. Keep them in a separate column so your website figure only includes events recorded on the site.

### Do I need Google Analytics?

It is the platform Google describes as the go-to choice for millions of owners ([Google Analytics](https://developers.google.com/analytics)). Any tool that records events and traffic sources works. The measurement method matters more than the specific tool.

### How long until a website pays back?

Divide the total upfront cost by the monthly profit the site generates. For example, a $1,200 build (assumption) that produces $300 of monthly profit (assumption) pays back in 4 months.

### Can an ad platform guarantee a return?

No. Adwave states that it does not guarantee sales or a return on spend, because results depend on the offer, audience, budget, creative and website ([Adwave](https://adwave.com/resources/cord-cutting-statistics)). Build your forecast on your own tracked data.
