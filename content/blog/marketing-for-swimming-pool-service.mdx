The cheapest way to fill pool routes is to win the customers already searching on Google Maps, keep every weekly customer longer, and use paid ads only to top up open slots. Pool service is recurring work, so marketing pays back over months, not on the first visit. This guide ranks five levers by effort and cost, shows the retention math with clearly labelled assumptions, and links the paid side to a separate ads guide.

## What are the five marketing levers for a pool service company?

| Lever | Cost | Time to first leads | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Google Business Profile and reviews | Free | Weeks to months | Homeowners searching "pool service near me" |
| Referral offer to current customers | One discount per referral | Immediate | Filling a route in a tight area |
| Property managers, HOAs, agents | Your time | Slow, then steady | Bulk and commercial accounts |
| Seasonal offers (openings, green pool rescues) | Free to low | Seasonal | One-off jobs that convert to weekly |
| Paid search | Per click | Days | Open slots you need filled now |

Start at the top. Each lever builds on the one above it.

## How do you rank higher on Google Maps?

Google says local results are based mainly on relevance, distance and prominence, and that complete, accurate profile information makes a business more likely to appear. It also says more reviews and positive ratings can help local ranking and that you cannot pay for a better local ranking. All of this is in [Google's local ranking guidance](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091).

Practical steps:

1. Choose a primary category that describes the work, and fill in hours, service area and website.
2. Add real photos of your trucks, chemical logs and finished pools.
3. Use the Offer and Event post types for seasonal specials. Google lets you schedule posts and set them to repeat, per its [posts help page](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7662907).

Google's page does not list posts as a ranking factor, so treat posts as conversion tools, not a ranking trick. Our [profile checklist](/blog/google-business-profile-optimization-checklist) covers the full setup.

## How many reviews do you need, and how should you reply?

BrightLocal's [2026 Local Consumer Review Survey](https://www.brightlocal.com/research/local-consumer-review-survey/) (1,002 US adults, published February 2026) found:

- 41% always read reviews, up from 29% in 2025.
- 68% only use a business rated 4 stars or higher; 31% want 4.5 or higher.
- 74% care about reviews from the last three months, and 32% look for reviews from the last two weeks.
- 89% expect owners to respond; 50% are unlikely to choose a business that uses templated or generic replies.

For a route business, recency matters: ask for a review after a good visit or a green-to-clear rescue, not once a year. Reply to each one with a specific detail from the job. Automating the ask saves time; see [how to automate review requests](/blog/how-to-automate-google-review-requests).

## What is a weekly customer worth, and how much can you spend to win one?

This worked example uses assumptions, not industry data. Replace them with your own figures.

- Assumed price: $160 per month per customer.
- Assumed gross margin after chemicals, labour and fuel: 40%, so $64 a month.
- Assumed churn: 2% of customers leave each month, so the average customer stays about 50 months.
- Lifetime gross profit: $64 x 50 = $3,200.

If you assume you can spend no more than a quarter of that to win a customer, your ceiling is about $800. Even a customer who stays only 12 months yields $768 of gross profit. That is why keeping customers (on-time visits, a text after each service, fixing issues fast) is as valuable as finding new ones.

Check the same formula against paid ads below before deciding how much to spend.

## When do paid ads make sense?

LocaliQ's [2026 search advertising benchmarks](https://localiq.com/blog/search-advertising-benchmarks/) list Home and Home Improvement at an average $8.33 cost per click, 8.05% conversion rate and $90.92 cost per lead (all-industry averages in the same report are $5.42 per click and a $66.69 cost per lead). The page has no separate pool row, so these are a broad home-services guide, not a pool-specific figure.

At $90.92 a lead, and assuming you close one in three, a new customer costs roughly $273 in ads, well under the $800 ceiling above. Your own numbers decide; our [Google Ads for pool service guide](/blog/google-ads-for-pool-service) walks through keywords, negatives and call tracking.

Google Local Services Ads charge per valid lead, and prices vary by location and job type, according to [Google's help page](https://support.google.com/localservices/answer/7195435). Check that your category and country are supported before planning around them.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: How much should a pool service company spend on marketing?**
A: There is no official benchmark. Start from the customer-value math above, set a ceiling per new customer, and track leads by source for 90 days.

**Q: Is a website necessary if I have a Google Business Profile?**
A: A profile gets you found; a simple site with services, service area, pricing guidance and a click-to-call button helps people decide. Treat them as a pair.

**Q: How do I get commercial pool accounts?**
A: Contact property managers, HOAs and agents directly with a one-page offer. Results depend on your area, so track every contact in a spreadsheet.

**Q: Should I offer a referral discount?**
A: It is cheap to test. Offer a fixed credit to both sides, cap it, and compare the cost per customer with your ad cost per customer.

**Q: How do I handle a bad review?**
A: Reply specifically and promptly. BrightLocal found 89% of consumers expect owners to respond.
