LocaliQ's 2026 Facebook benchmarks put the average cost of a Health & Fitness lead at $27.11. If Los Angeles costs twice that (an assumption, because no published benchmark isolates the city), a $1,200 monthly budget buys about 22 leads and, with realistic show-up and join rates, roughly three new members. Whether that is a good result depends on a number only you know: what a member is worth to you.

This guide is for studio and gym owners in the City of Los Angeles who are considering Facebook and Instagram ads. It covers what the public data says about who lives where, how many fitness businesses you compete with, the California rules that touch a free-trial offer, what Meta's policies allow, and the arithmetic from budget to members. Figures are sourced and attributed. Anything that is an assumption is labelled as one.

## Rules That Touch Your Offer

### California auto-renewal and health club rules

Most studios sell a free trial that converts into a paid membership. In California, that sits under two sets of rules. The notes below are general information from law-firm and legislative summaries, not legal advice, so read the current statute text or ask a lawyer.

- **Automatic renewal law.** Amendments (AB 2863) took effect July 1, 2025. According to Barnes & Thornburg's summary, businesses must obtain the consumer's express affirmative consent to automatic renewal or continuous service terms, and the law now reaches free-to-paid conversions. Cancellation must be offered through the same channel used to sign up (online, by phone or in store). Consumers must receive an annual reminder stating the renewal frequency, the amount charged and how to cancel. Proof of consent must be kept for at least three years or one year after the contract ends, whichever is longer.
- **Health studio contracts.** California's Civil Code has a separate health studio services contract law (section 1812.80 onward). According to the 2016 Assembly floor analysis of AB 2810, every health studio contract must include a statement that the buyer may cancel before midnight of the fifth business day after the agreement. That bill added cancellation in person, by email from an address on file or by first-class mail, and required contracts to disclose the initial or minimum term. The law may have been amended since 2016, so check the current text.

What this means for your ad: describe the offer accurately, state what happens when a trial ends, and make sure the sign-up page shows renewal terms before the person pays.

### Meta's advertising policies

Meta's Health and wellness advertising policy, as shown on its transparency pages, says that ads for weight loss products must be targeted to people aged 18 or older, but it treats fitness services and health clubs as marketed for general well-being, exempt from that age restriction. In a weight loss context it bars promises of specific outcomes within a set time frame without disclaimers, and it bars statements that attack an individual's appearance. So "Lose 10 pounds in 30 days" is a policy risk, and "First class free, beginners welcome" is not. Check the live policy before you run any claim.

## Set Up the Campaign

These steps are recommendations, not Meta rules.

1. **Offer.** One clear, low-commitment offer: a free class, a first-week pass or an intro price. Put the end-of-trial terms in writing on the form and landing page.
2. **Objective.** Start with a lead campaign using an instant form, or send traffic to a landing page with a short form. Our [landing page best practices](/blog/landing-page-best-practices) cover the layout.
3. **Location.** Pick two or three neighborhoods near the studio where your members already live, using the postcodes of your last 50 sign-ups. In a driving city, think in drive times from your door, then check your Meta location options for the radius controls available to you.
4. **Creative.** Use real photos or short video of your coaches, your room and a class in progress. Avoid before-and-after claims and appearance-based messaging.
5. **Follow-up.** Call or text new leads within minutes. Fast follow-up is a recommendation here, not a measured figure; see Facebook ads for fitness studios for more on the funnel.
6. **Tracking.** Set up conversion tracking for the lead form and log which leads become members. Our conversion tracking guide covers the principles.

## The Math

A $1,200 monthly lead campaign. Rows marked assumption are illustrations, not benchmarks.

| | LocaliQ benchmark | 2x (assumption) | 3x (assumption) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Monthly budget | $1,200 | $1,200 | $1,200 |
| Cost per lead | $27.11 | $54 | $81 |
| Leads | 44 | 22 | 15 |
| Lead to attended trial (assumption) | 50% | 50% | 50% |
| Attended trial to member (assumption) | 30% | 30% | 30% |
| New members | 6.6 | 3.3 | 2.2 |
| Ad cost per new member | $181 | $361 | $542 |
| Monthly fee (assumption) | $140 | $140 | $140 |
| Months of fees to pay back the ad cost | 1.3 | 2.6 | 3.9 |

How to read it: the last row divides ad cost per member by the monthly fee. It uses revenue, not profit, so real payback is slower. If your 50% and 30% rates are lower (for example 40% and 20%), the cost per member in each column rises by about 1.9 times. The point is not these exact figures. It is that you can compute your own break-even: the most you can pay per lead equals your allowable cost per member multiplied by your show-up rate and your join rate.

Check how long members stay before you scale spend. If an average member leaves after three months, the 2x and 3x columns lose money.

Run two offers (for example a free class and a one-week pass) to the same neighborhoods for two weeks before testing new audiences. The offer is usually the biggest lever, and it is the cheapest to change.

## Do This Today

1. Pull your last 50 sign-ups and map their ZIP codes. Choose two or three neighborhoods for your first campaign.
2. Calculate your allowable cost per member: monthly fee times the number of months a typical member stays, times the share of that revenue you are willing to spend on acquiring them.
3. Write your free-trial terms in plain language: what is free, for how long, what it costs after, and how to cancel. Put them on the form and the landing page.
4. Build one campaign, one offer and one form, with real photos and no weight-loss or appearance claims.
5. Decide who answers new leads within 15 minutes, and log every lead's outcome (attended, joined, not interested) for four weeks before judging results.

## FAQ

**Q: How much do Meta ads cost for a fitness studio in Los Angeles?**
A: There is no published Los Angeles benchmark. LocaliQ's 2026 national figures for Health & Fitness are $0.64 per click on traffic campaigns and $27.11 per lead on lead campaigns. Treat Los Angeles as likely to cost more and measure your own.

**Q: Do I need to follow California's auto-renewal rules for a free trial?**
A: If your trial converts into a paid membership automatically, the California automatic renewal law, as amended effective July 1, 2025, appears to apply, including express consent and a way to cancel through the channel used to sign up. Confirm with the current statute or a lawyer.

**Q: Can I use before-and-after photos in a gym ad?**
A: Meta's Health and wellness policy page allows before-and-after depictions for general cosmetic products and procedures but does not address them for fitness services, and it restricts time-framed outcome claims and appearance-based negativity. Safer creative shows your space, coaches and classes.

**Q: Facebook ads or Google Ads for a studio?**
A: They reach different moments. Google reaches people searching now; Meta can reach people who might join after seeing an offer. Many studios start with search and add Meta once their follow-up process works. See our Google Ads for fitness studios in Los Angeles guide.

**Q: How long before I know if it works?**
A: Leads can arrive within days, but plan on four to six weeks of lead outcomes before judging, so you can see attended trials and sign-ups. That is a rule of thumb, not a Meta figure.
