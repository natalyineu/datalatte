Here is the number to hold onto. If a click costs you $3, 6 in 100 clicks become a booking request, and 7 in 10 requests turn up, a new massage client costs about $71 in ads. That is just under the $72 you keep from a first $90 session at an assumed 80% contribution margin. Move all three inputs to a tough or strong case and the answer swings between roughly $31 and $250. All three are my assumptions, not benchmarks, and the math section below shows how to replace them with your own.

This guide is for solo therapists and small clinics in the US, UK, Australia and Canada. It covers the part most generic guides skip, which is what Google's advertising policies say about health claims, then how to set up a Search campaign, whether Local Services Ads are worth checking, how to track bookings, and the arithmetic of whether it pays. Dollar figures are illustrative; run the same sums in your own currency.

## Know the Health-Claim Rules Before You Write an Ad

Massage sits next to healthcare, and ad copy tends to drift toward "relief" and "cure" language. Here is what Google's policy pages say, and where they are silent.

**Unreliable claims.** Google's [unreliable claims policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/15936857) says ads may not make inaccurate claims or claims that entice the user with an improbable result, even if the result is possible. It prohibits unproven claims of cures for incurable medical ailments, with miracle cures for conditions such as arthritis, diabetes, Alzheimer's disease or cancer as examples, and it prohibits "cure-all" products claiming to treat several diseases. The page also says Google may defer to local regulatory guidelines for health-related claims, including testimonials. If you guarantee results you must offer a clear refund policy, and testimonials claiming specific results need a visible disclaimer that results are not guaranteed and may vary. Google says violations do not lead to immediate suspension: it issues a warning at least 7 days before any account suspension.

**Healthcare and medicines.** Google's [healthcare and medicines policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/176031) covers things like unapproved substances, prescription drug services and speculative medical treatments. The portion we reviewed does not mention massage, and it restricts promoting unapproved products as safe or effective for preventing, curing or treating a disease. Whether a massage ad counts as "health" for every restriction is a judgement for Google's reviewers, not something this page settles.

**Targeting.** Google's [personalized advertising policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/143465) lists Health among the categories with targeting restrictions, and says advertisers promoting in these sensitive categories are unable to use advertiser-curated audiences, which includes custom audiences. If Google treats your ads as health-related, remarketing lists may not be available to you. Check in your own account rather than assuming.

What this means in practice (my reading, not Google's wording):

| Risky | Safer |
| --- | --- |
| "Cure your sciatica in one session" | "Deep tissue massage for lower back tension" |
| "Guaranteed pain-free in 7 days" | "Same-week appointments. Book online." |
| "Fixes migraines for good" | "Neck and shoulder massage by a licensed therapist" |
| A review saying "my herniated disc disappeared" | A review about the room, the therapist and how they felt afterwards |

Describe the service, the location and the booking, and leave diagnosis and cure to clinicians. For the wider picture of how Google treats health advertisers, see our guides on [Google Ads for mental health professionals](/blog/google-ads-for-mental-health-professionals) and [Google Ads for urgent care](/blog/google-ads-for-urgent-care), which go deeper on health-ad policy and its practical limits.

## Local Services Ads: Check Your Account

Google's Local Services Ads (the pay-per-lead "Google Guaranteed" style listings) are separate from Search campaigns. Google's [US category list](https://support.google.com/localservices/answer/6224841) shows "Massage therapist" and "Massage school" among the categories. That page is for the US. Eligibility in the UK, Australia and Canada, and in your state or city, is not something this article could confirm, so check your account or Google's sign-up flow.

If you qualify, expect screening. Google's [page on how providers qualify](https://support.google.com/localservices/answer/6230381) says it verifies applicable state, provincial or country-level licences for the business and its owners or managers, to the extent practical, and confirms that workers are appropriately licensed. Depending on the service and country, it may also check insurance and run background checks. It also says customer ratings and reviews can lower a provider's ranking or hide it entirely if feedback is serious or repeatedly negative.

In short: have your licence number, insurance details and a few real reviews ready, then see whether the option appears in your account.

## Set Up a Search Campaign

Search ads catch people typing "massage near me" or "deep tissue massage [town]". For most solo practices this is the right start.

- **One campaign, one location.** Draw a radius around your treatment room based on where your last 50 clients live or work. Most people book close to home or work.
- **Location setting.** Google notes that location targeting relies on signals and is "Google's best effort," and that 100% accuracy is not guaranteed ([location targeting help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/6317)). Open the location options in your campaign and prefer people physically in or regularly in your area over people who only searched for your town. That is my recommendation; check what your campaign defaults to.
- **Schedule.** Run ads when you can answer the phone or when online booking is open, then read results by day and hour.

### Keywords

Group them by what the person wants. Start with phrase and exact match at a small budget.

| Ad group | Example keywords |
| --- | --- |
| Generic | "massage near me", "massage therapist [town]" |
| Technique | "deep tissue massage [town]", "sports massage [town]", "prenatal massage near me" |
| Problem-led | "massage for neck and shoulder tension", "massage for lower back tension" |
| Offer-led | "same day massage", "evening massage appointments" |
| Your brand | [your practice name] |

Problem-led keywords are fine as search terms. The care is in your ad text, as the table above shows.

### Negative keywords

Add these on day one and check the search terms report weekly: `free`, `jobs`, `salary`, `school`, `course`, `certification`, `chair`, `gun` (massage guns), `oil`, `table`, `happy ending`, `erotic`, `sensual`, `adult`. The last four protect your brand and ad account from the wrong kind of enquiry. Our negative keywords guide explains campaign-level lists.

### Ads

Responsive search ads take multiple headlines and descriptions. Use plain, specific text: "Deep Tissue Massage in [Town]", "Licensed Therapist. Online Booking.", "Evening and Weekend Slots", "First-Visit Offer". Add a call asset, a location asset linked to your Business Profile and sitelinks (Prices, Book Online, About Me). Send clicks to a page for one service with price, duration, a booking button, your qualifications, a map and a tap-to-call number. ## Track Bookings, Not Clicks

Set up three things before turning ads on. Our conversion tracking setup guide has the steps.

1. **Online booking confirmation.** Track the thank-you page or the booking event.
2. **Calls from the ad.** Count only calls over a minimum length, which filters out misdials.
3. **Calls from your website.** Google's [website call conversions help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/6095883) explains that your number on the page is swapped for a Google forwarding number, you set a minimum call duration, and the default tag tracks only one number per page.

Then close the loop by hand. Ask every new client how they found you and note who attended. The platform counts requests; only you know who turned up.

## The Math

Take a $600 monthly budget. Every row marked assumption is mine, not a benchmark: use Keyword Planner for your own click prices, and replace the rest with your calendar and booking data after a month.

| | Tough | Base | Strong |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Monthly budget (assumption) | $600 | $600 | $600 |
| Cost per click (assumption) | $4.50 | $3.00 | $2.25 |
| Clicks | 133 | 200 | 267 |
| Click to booking request rate (assumption) | 3% | 6% | 9% |
| Booking requests | 4 | 12 | 24 |
| Show-up rate (assumption) | 60% | 70% | 80% |
| New clients who attend | 2.4 | 8.4 | 19.2 |
| Ad cost per attending client | $250 | $71 | $31 |
| Session price (assumption) | $90 | $90 | $90 |
| Contribution margin after supplies and fees (assumption) | 80% | 80% | 80% |
| Visits to repay ad cost | 3.5 | 1.0 | 0.4 |

How to read it: contribution per visit is $72, so a $250 acquisition cost needs 3.5 visits to repay, and a $71 one is covered by the first session. The figure ignores your own time, rent and insurance. The Strong column also needs capacity: 19 new clients is a lot of hours for a solo therapist, and you may run out of slots before you run out of budget.

Two things follow:

- **Your break-even click price.** Maximum CPC = the most you will pay for an attending client x request rate x show-up rate. At $70 per client, 6% and 70%: $70 x 0.06 x 0.70 = $2.94. Above that, you lose money on average.
- **No-shows are a cost you control.** Moving show-up from 60% to 80% cuts cost per attending client by a quarter without touching the ad account. Text reminders and a deposit policy cost nothing in ad spend.

## Optimise Weekly

Clicks are noisy at this budget, so avoid daily tinkering. Each week, read the search terms report and add negatives, pause keywords that spent about twice your target cost per client with no requests, test one new headline, and raise budget only on campaigns that hit your target. Pair ads with a complete [Google Business Profile](/services/google-business-profile) because people compare reviews and photos before they book. If your town has little search demand, put effort into reviews and local search first and add ads later.

## Do This Today

1. Pull your last 50 new clients and note their town or postcode. Draw your ad radius from that map.
2. Work out your numbers: session price, average visits per client in six months, and the most you will pay for one attending new client.
3. Rewrite your draft ad copy against the risky versus safer table above, with no cures, guarantees or diagnosed conditions.
4. Check your account for Local Services Ads availability, and have your licence and insurance documents ready.
5. Set up booking, ad-call and website-call conversions before turning on one Search campaign with five tight ad groups and the negative list.

## FAQ

**Q: Can I mention back pain or sciatica in a massage ad?**
A: Google does not ban naming a body area or a symptom as a search term. Its policy bans claims that entice with improbable results and unproven cures for incurable ailments. "Massage for lower back tension" is safer than "cure your sciatica". Google may also defer to local health regulators, so check your own rules.

**Q: Are Local Services Ads available for massage therapists?**
A: Google's US category list includes "Massage therapist". Availability elsewhere, and in your location, is something to check in your account. Expect licence and insurance verification.

**Q: How much should I budget per month?**
A: There is no official figure. At an assumed $3 click, $600 buys about 200 clicks. Use the break-even formula to see what your area supports, and give it at least a month.

**Q: Can I use client testimonials in my ads?**
A: Google's unreliable claims policy says testimonials claiming specific results need a visible disclaimer that results are not guaranteed and may vary. Keep testimonials about the experience rather than medical outcomes.

**Q: Will I lose remarketing because I am in health?**
A: Possibly. Google says advertisers promoting in sensitive categories, including Health, cannot use advertiser-curated audiences. Whether your ads fall in that group is something to confirm in your account.
