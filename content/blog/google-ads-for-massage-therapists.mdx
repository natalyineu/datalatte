**Do Google Ads work for massage therapists?** Yes, if there is enough local search demand and you track bookings. Google Ads shows your practice to people who are actively searching for a massage, often with pain or stress they want to fix soon. Start with a small test budget, a tight local radius, specific keywords and a clear booking page, then scale only when you can see what each new client costs you.

Costs and results vary widely by city and by the services you offer, so the numbers in this guide are planning guidance, not promises. Below is a step-by-step way to set up and run a first campaign.

## Setting up your Google Ads campaign

You can set up a Google Ads account yourself, but the choices you make at the start (campaign type, location settings and conversion tracking) determine how efficiently your budget is used.

- **Campaign type:** For most massage practices, a Search campaign is the right starting point because it captures people already looking for a massage.
- **Location:** Target the area your clients realistically travel from. Use "presence" targeting where possible so you do not pay for people who are just interested in your city from elsewhere.
- **Schedule:** Consider running ads during your booking hours or when people tend to search, and check results by day and time.
- **Conversion tracking:** Set it up before you spend anything (see below).

## Choosing the right keywords

Keywords decide who sees your ad. Use Google's Keyword Planner to see what people search for in your area, then group them by service.

For a Swedish massage practice in San Francisco, relevant keywords might be:

- "Swedish massage San Francisco"
- "relaxation massage near me"
- "massage for stress relief"

For a deep tissue or sports massage therapist, think about pain-based searches such as "deep tissue massage for back pain" alongside the service name.

<Callout type="tip">Use specific, long-tail keywords like "deep tissue massage in Los Angeles" or "prenatal massage near me" rather than only the single word "massage." They are more specific, and usually closer to booking intent.</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">Add negative keywords from day one to avoid irrelevant clicks, such as "free massage," "massage school," "massage jobs," "massage chair" and any service you do not offer.</Callout>

## Writing ads that get booked

Lead with the problem or result, then the proof and a reason to act.

- **Headlines:** name the service and location, such as "Deep Tissue Massage in Austin," plus a differentiator like "Same-Week Appointments."
- **Descriptions:** focus on relief and outcomes, and mention anything that reduces friction, such as online booking, evening hours or a first-visit offer.
- **Extensions:** add call, location and sitelink extensions so people can phone you or jump straight to booking.

For example, "Lower back pain? Book a deep tissue massage this week. Online booking available." speaks to what the client wants better than a list of credentials.

Make sure your ad lands on a page built for that service, not just your homepage. Our guide to [website design for massage therapists](/blog/website-design-for-massage-therapists) covers what a booking-focused page needs.

## Budgeting and bidding

Google Ads for local services is generally pay-per-click, so you pay when someone clicks your ad. Cost per click varies by city and keyword, and massage-related searches can be competitive in larger markets, so check Keyword Planner for estimates in your area.

A sensible approach:

1. Pick a test budget you can afford to run for at least a month.
2. Start with a manual max CPC cap or "Maximize clicks" with a cap, then switch to conversion-based bidding once you have enough tracked bookings.
3. Judge results by **cost per booked client**, not cost per click.

An illustration with made-up numbers: if you spend $500 a month and your average click costs $2.50, you would get about 200 clicks. If 5% of clicks become bookings, that is 10 new clients at about $50 each. Whether that works depends on what a client is worth to you over time, including repeat visits.

## Measuring success

Without tracking you are guessing. Set up:

- **Call tracking** for phone calls from your ads
- **Conversion tracking** on your booking confirmation or "thank you" page
- **Google Analytics 4** linked to your ads account

Then review search terms weekly, add negatives and pause keywords that spend without booking. See also our [analytics service](/services/analytics) for help connecting the data.

## FAQ

**Q: How much should I budget for Google Ads as a massage therapist?**
A: Start with an amount you can afford to run for a month or two, enough to collect real data. Many practices begin in the hundreds of dollars per month and scale once the cost per booked client is clear. Competitive cities cost more.

**Q: Can I run Google Ads myself, or do I need to hire someone?**
A: You can learn it yourself, but plan on regular weekly attention for setup, search-term review and optimization. Be cautious of anyone promising "set it and forget it," since keywords and competition change.

**Q: What if I'm in a small town with low search volume?**
A: If few people search for massage in your town each month, ads may not deliver much. In that case focus first on local SEO and your [Google Business Profile](/services/google-business-profile), reviews and referrals, then add ads when there is enough demand.

**Q: How do I know if someone booked because of my ad?**
A: Use Google Ads call reporting and conversion tracking on your booking confirmation page. Without it you cannot tell which keywords actually produce clients.

**Q: Is Google Ads better than Yelp or a booking app for getting clients?**
A: They do different jobs. Google Ads reaches people searching right now, Yelp and Google reviews help comparison shoppers, and a booking tool handles scheduling. Most practices use a mix.
