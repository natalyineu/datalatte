Canada's anti-spam law says a past purchase can create an "existing business relationship" only if it happened "within the two-year period immediately before the day on which the message was sent." That one phrase is why a reactivation campaign is a permissions problem before it is a marketing problem. The customers you most want to bring back are the ones who last bought long ago, and they are exactly the ones whose permission may have quietly expired.

This guide is for owners of cafes, salons, studios, groomers and similar businesses in the US, UK, Australia and Canada. It does not repeat the basics of subject lines and offers; for the email side, see our [AI-assisted email marketing for coffee shops](/blog/ai-assisted-email-marketing-for-coffee-shops) and [AI automation for hair salons](/blog/ai-automation-for-hair-salons) guides. Here the angle is different: how to sort a lapsed list by what you are actually allowed to send, then run a short sequence and work out whether it pays. This is general information, not legal advice. Rules change, so check with an adviser before a large send.

## Why Bother With Lapsed Customers?

Harvard Business Review's Amy Gallo wrote that "acquiring a new customer is anywhere from five to 25 times more expensive than retaining an existing one," and added that the range depends on "which study you believe" and the industry. The same article attributes to Bain's Frederick Reichheld the claim that "increasing customer retention rates by 5% increases profits by 25% to 95%." Those are broad figures, not local-business benchmarks, and they are about retention rather than winning back people who left. Use them as a reason to test, then replace them with your own cost per reactivated customer, which we calculate below.

## Step 1: Define "Lapsed" From Your Own Data

Export customers from your POS, booking system or CRM. Take the usual gap between visits for your active customers and multiply it by about 1.5 (my rule of thumb, not a published standard). A client who books every six weeks looks lapsed at about nine weeks; a coffee shop's window is far shorter than a dentist's.

Then add a second clock: **the date of each person's last purchase or enquiry, and how they gave you their contact details.** You need both for the next step.

## Step 2: Sort Into Permission Buckets Before You Write a Word

The rules differ by country and by channel. This table summarises what we could confirm from official pages; open them for the exact text.

| Where | Email or text rule | Opt-out deadline | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| US email (CAN-SPAM) | The FTC says all commercial email must comply, including "a message to former customers announcing a new product line." Accurate header, honest subject line, clear ad disclosure, valid postal address, opt-out explanation | Honor within 10 business days | FTC guide |
| US texts (TCPA) | Consent rules apply to autodialed marketing texts; check current FCC rules and ask an adviser | The FCC's January 2024 fact sheet says revocation must be honored no later than 10 business days from receipt; some pieces of that order may have changed since | FCC fact sheet |
| UK (PECR) | ICO: "You must not send marketing emails or texts to individuals without specific consent," unless the soft opt-in applies | "Act on this promptly"; offer an opt-out in every message | ICO guide |
| Canada (CASL) | Express consent, or implied consent from an existing business relationship (a purchase within two years, or an enquiry within six months) | No later than 10 business days | CASL text |
| Australia (Spam Act) | Express or inferred consent (the person gave you their address and would reasonably expect marketing from you) | No later than 5 working days | business.gov.au |

A few points from those pages worth spelling out:

- **US email has no exemption for ex-customers.** The FTC guide says the law makes no exception for business-to-business email either, and that the penalty is "up to $53,088" for each separate non-compliant email. Hiring someone to send for you does not transfer the duty: "you can't contract away your legal responsibility."
- **The FTC's relationship exemption is narrow.** Messages that are only transactional or relationship content are exempt from most provisions, but the FTC says the law "views these categories narrowly," and a message that reads as an advertisement to a reasonable recipient is commercial. A "we miss you, here is 10% off" email is marketing.
- **UK soft opt-in has conditions.** The ICO says it covers someone who "bought (or negotiated to buy) a similar product or service from you in the past" and applies only if you "gave them a simple way to opt out both when you first collected their details and in every message you have sent." It excludes new contacts such as bought-in lists. The ICO page does not say how long a soft opt-in lasts, so ask your adviser rather than assume it never expires. The ICO also notes that sole traders and some partnerships are treated as individuals, not as companies.
- **Canada's clock is explicit.** The Act's s.10(10) lists a purchase or lease "within the two-year period" and an inquiry or application "within the six-month period" before the day the message was sent. Section 11(3) requires unsubscribe requests to take effect "without delay, and in any event no later than 10 business days." This reflects the Act's own text, so also read the regulator's current guidance.
- **Australia asks you to keep records for express consent.** Business.gov.au says to keep a record of who gave permission, when and how. It does not mention records for inferred consent.

Three buckets follow from this:

| Bucket | Who | What you do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Green | Opted in clearly, recent purchase, nothing on your suppression list | Run the full sequence |
| Amber | Old purchase, unclear source, or contact details collected without an opt-out | Do not send marketing yet. Use a non-marketing channel such as in-person, or take advice on a permission-refresh approach |
| Red | Unsubscribed, bounced, complained, or bought-in list | Never email or text. Keep them on a suppression list so they are not re-imported |

The last point matters more than people expect. If you move to a new email tool, import your suppression list too. Opt-outs belong to the person, not to the software.

## Step 3: Segment the Green Bucket by Value

Inside the green bucket, split by past spend and visit frequency.

- **High value:** your best past customers. A personal note or a call from the owner (a call is subject to different rules than email, so check yours).
- **Medium value:** the three-touch sequence below.
- **Low value:** one automated message.

Add a "likely reason" tag where you know it: moved, price, a bad visit, or simply drifted. Someone who had a bad experience needs an apology, not a coupon.

## Step 4: Send a Short Three-Touch Sequence

- **Touch 1, day 1:** a warm note from a named person. Say who you are, why you are writing ("we noticed it has been a while"), what is new, and one link. No offer yet.
- **Touch 2, about day 7:** one specific reason to come back, with a date.
- **Touch 3, about day 14:** a final reminder. After this, stop and move them to your normal low-frequency list.

Every message needs the legal basics. For email in the US, the FTC lists accurate header information, a subject line that "accurately reflect[s] the content," a clear disclosure that it is an advertisement, and your valid physical postal address. In the UK, the ICO says you "must not disguise or conceal your identity" and must give "a valid contact address" for opt-outs. Make unsubscribing a single click, and make sure the unsubscribe actually suppresses the person across every list.

If you use texts as well, they need their own consent trail; see our [SMS marketing best practices](/blog/sms-marketing-best-practices) and, for UK salons, [email and SMS marketing for UK salons](/blog/email-sms-marketing-uk-salons). The wider UK data protection picture is in our [GDPR-compliant email marketing guide](/blog/gdpr-compliant-email-marketing).

For people who booked and did not turn up, a different message is better: see [win back no-shows with abandoned appointment emails](/blog/abandoned-appointment-email-win-back-no-shows).

## Step 5: Pick an Offer You Can Afford

A deep discount trains customers to wait for sales. Cheaper options: a small add-on that costs you little, bonus loyalty points, early access to something new, or a personal note. Work out the cost per redemption before you send. In the math below we assume $4.

## Step 6: Ask Why They Left

Put one question in the first or second message with four or five choices (moved, found another place, price, a bad experience, just busy) and an "other" box. The answers can tell you whether you have a marketing problem or a service problem. Reply to any complaint personally and the same day.

## The Math

Take a lapsed list of 800 customers. Every figure here is an **assumption** for illustration, except the legal buckets, which come from your own records.

| | Tough | Base | Strong |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lapsed customers on file (assumption) | 800 | 800 | 800 |
| Share in the green bucket (assumption) | 50% | 65% | 80% |
| Customers messaged | 400 | 520 | 640 |
| Reactivation rate (assumption) | 5% | 8% | 12% |
| Customers who return | 20 | 42 | 77 |
| First-visit spend (assumption) | $45 | $45 | $45 |
| Revenue | $900 | $1,890 | $3,465 |
| Gross margin (assumption) | 60% | 60% | 60% |
| Offer cost at $4 per return | $80 | $168 | $308 |
| Tool and staff time (assumption) | $110 | $110 | $110 |
| Profit from the campaign | $350 | $856 | $1,661 |
| Campaign cost per returning customer | $9.50 | $6.62 | $5.43 |

How to read it: profit is revenue times gross margin, minus offer cost and tool and time. It counts only the first return visit, so it understates the value of customers who stay, and it ignores those who lapse again (track your re-lapse rate). Compare the cost per returning customer with what you pay to win a new customer from ads. If a new customer costs you $40 (also an assumption), even the Tough column wins on cost per customer. The real risk in this table is the second row. If your green bucket is 30% instead of 65%, you simply have a smaller campaign, and no clever subject line fixes that. Fixing it means collecting clear permission from the start. Our guide to [Google Ads for gyms](/blog/google-ads-for-gyms) shows the same kind of break-even thinking for paid acquisition.

## Measure What Matters

Open rates are unreliable, so use outcomes:

- **Reactivation rate:** share of messaged customers who buy again within about 30 days
- **Reactivated revenue:** their total spend
- **Cost per reactivation:** offers plus tools plus staff time, divided by customers won back
- **Re-lapse rate:** how many lapse again within a couple of months
- **Unsubscribe and complaint rate:** a rising number means your list or your message is off

Use your sales or booking records, not just email reports.

## Do This Today

1. Export your lapsed customers and add two columns: date of last purchase and how you collected the contact details.
2. Sort them into green, amber and red buckets using the table above, and put every red contact on a suppression list that travels with you between tools.
3. Pick your offer and cost it per redemption.
4. Write the three messages, with sender name, postal address and a one-click unsubscribe in each, and schedule them for days 1, 7 and 14.
5. Four weeks later, calculate reactivation rate, revenue, cost per returning customer and unsubscribes, and decide whether to repeat the campaign next quarter.

## FAQ

**Q: How long should I wait before calling a customer lapsed?**
A: Use your own buying cycle. About one and a half times the usual gap between visits is our rule of thumb. Coffee shops use weeks; salons and dentists use months.

**Q: Can I email everyone who ever bought from me?**
A: Not automatically. In the US, CAN-SPAM covers emails to former customers and requires an opt-out. In the UK, you need specific consent unless the soft opt-in applies. In Canada, implied consent from a purchase is limited to two years. In Australia, you need express or inferred consent. Check each against your records.

**Q: Do the rules apply to text messages?**
A: Yes. The ICO page says electronic mail includes messages sent by SMS, and US texts fall under separate TCPA consent rules. Each channel needs its own consent trail.

**Q: Should I offer a discount?**
A: Not first. Try a personal note or a small add-on and check what each redemption costs you.

**Q: What if a customer replies "stop"?**
A: Treat it as an unsubscribe and suppress them everywhere, within the deadlines in the table or sooner. Do not argue with the message.
