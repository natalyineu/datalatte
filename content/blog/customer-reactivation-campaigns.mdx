A customer reactivation campaign is a short, targeted sequence of emails, texts or mailers sent to customers who used to buy from you and have stopped. To run one: define what "lapsed" means for your business, segment the list by past value, send a three-touch sequence over about two weeks with a genuine reason to return, and track the revenue from customers who come back. It is usually cheaper than finding new customers, because these people already know you.

Businesses often assume that winning back an old customer costs less than finding a new one. That is generally true, because you do not pay to introduce yourself, but the actual numbers depend on your business, so measure your own cost per reactivated customer.

## Step 1: Identify Lapsed Customers

Export your customer list from your POS, booking system or CRM and find people whose last purchase is older than your normal buying cycle. A useful rule of thumb: take the usual gap between visits for your active customers, then multiply by about 1.5. A client who normally books every six weeks starts to look lapsed at around nine weeks. A coffee shop's window will be much shorter than a hair salon's or a dental practice's.

Clean the list first: remove duplicates, invalid addresses and anyone who unsubscribed.

## Step 2: Segment by Value and by Reason

Not all lapsed customers are equal, and not all lapsed for the same reason.

**By value (past spend and frequency):**

- **High value:** your best past customers. Consider a personal message, call or handwritten note from the owner.
- **Medium value:** a personalized email or SMS sequence with a value-added offer.
- **Low value:** a single automated message with a small incentive; do not spend extra effort here.

**By likely reason:** price-sensitive customers, customers who may have had a bad experience, and customers whose life circumstances changed (they moved, changed jobs). Each group responds to a different message. A friendly "we have not seen you in a while" with no discount can work better than a coupon for people who simply drifted away.

## Step 3: Build a Simple Three-Touch Sequence

A short sequence spaced over about two weeks avoids pestering people:

- **Touch 1 (day 1):** a warm, short email: "It has been a while, [Name]. Here is what is new." No hard sell. Link to your menu, services or booking page.
- **Touch 2 (about day 7):** a specific, time-limited reason to come back, such as a free add-on or a bonus.
- **Touch 3 (about day 14):** a final reminder with a clear deadline. After this, stop and move them to a long-term list you contact occasionally.

If you use SMS, make sure the person has opted in and keep it to one well-timed message. Rules differ by country (for example TCPA in the US, PECR and GDPR in the UK and EU), so check the consent requirements that apply to you. ## Step 4: Choose an Offer That Does Not Hurt Your Margins

A big percentage discount can attract deal-seekers and train customers to wait for sales. Alternatives:

- **A free add-on** that costs you little but feels valuable (a conditioning treatment, a nail trim, a pastry with a drink)
- **Bonus loyalty points** on their next visits
- **Early or exclusive access** to a new service or event
- **A small, personal gesture**, such as a note from the owner

Work out the cost of each offer so you know what you give up when someone redeems it.

## Step 5: Match the Channel to the Customer

Use the channel the customer actually engaged with: email if they opened your emails, SMS if they opted in and replied, a postcard or handwritten note if you only have a mailing address, or an Instagram or Facebook retargeting ad if they follow you there. ## Step 6: Ask Why They Left

Add a one-question survey to your first or second message: "What is the main reason you have not been in lately?" Offer a few choices (moved, found another place, prices, a bad experience last time, just busy) plus an "other" field. The answers show you whether the problem is your marketing or your business, and they can tell you what to fix, such as prices or service quality.

## Step 7: Measure What Matters

Open rates are less reliable than they used to be, so focus on outcomes:

- **Reactivation rate:** the share of lapsed customers who buy again within about 30 days
- **Reactivated revenue:** total spend from customers who came back
- **Cost per reactivation:** campaign cost (tools, offers, staff time) divided by customers won back
- **Re-lapse rate:** how many of them lapse again within a couple of months

Compare these with your cost of acquiring new customers from ads. If you run [Google Ads](/services/google-ads), the cost per new customer is a useful benchmark.

<Callout type="warning">Do not stop at email metrics. Use your sales or booking records to see whether the campaign actually brought customers back and how much they spent.</Callout>

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Sending one generic "we miss you" email to everyone.** Segment by value and reason.
- **Sending too many messages too fast.** A short, spaced sequence builds goodwill; a flood looks desperate.
- **Leaning on deep discounts.** Use value-added offers where you can.
- **Ignoring the preferred channel.** Use the channel each customer actually responds to.
- **Never asking why they left.** One question can reveal a fixable problem.

## A Simple 30-Day Plan

**Week 1:** clean and segment your list and define your lapse window.
**Week 2:** write your three messages and set up the sequence in your email tool.
**Week 3:** send, and check replies and bookings daily.
**Week 4:** calculate reactivation rate, revenue and cost; keep what worked and repeat the process each quarter.

If you use a POS or booking system that supports automation, set the sequence to trigger automatically when a customer passes your lapse threshold. ## FAQ

**Q: How long should I wait before treating a customer as lapsed?**
A: Use your own buying cycle. A rough rule is about one and a half times the usual gap between visits for an active customer. Coffee shops will use days or weeks; salons and dentists will use months.

**Q: Should I offer a discount to win back lapsed customers?**
A: Not always. Try a value-added offer or a simple personal message first, and use discounts selectively. Always work out what an offer costs you.

**Q: How many messages should I send?**
A: Three touches over about two weeks is a sensible starting point. After that, move people to an occasional list rather than continuing to push.

**Q: Which channel works best, email or SMS?**
A: It depends on the customer. Text messages tend to be read quickly but require opt-in and care; email is cheaper and good for more detail. Use whichever the customer has actually engaged with.

**Q: How do I measure whether a reactivation campaign worked?**
A: Count the customers from your lapsed list who purchased within a set window after the campaign, add up their spend and compare it with the campaign's cost.
