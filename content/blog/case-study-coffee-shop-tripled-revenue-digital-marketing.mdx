## What did the Bottega Coffee store change online?

Bottega Coffee sells premium coffee machines, grinders and accessories in Australia. As a new business, it needed a website that signalled trust before a shopper paid, since expensive equipment is a hard online purchase. [1] The agency built a Shopify store with detailed product pages, comparison features, a fully responsive design and multiple payment options, including buy-now-pay-later. [1]

The reported results are a 3.8x increase in monthly online sales within six months, a 49% increase in mobile and tablet conversions, and a 32% rise in repeat purchase rate. [1] The campaign ran for more than 12 months on a $25K budget, using five resources across organic, paid social, email and direct traffic. [1]

A café selling beans rather than machines would have different products, but the logic carries over. Clear product pages and a fast mobile checkout reduce drop-off, and the mobile gain at Bottega suggests that friction is costly. ## How did a subscription brand grow without overspending?

Brick Marketing worked with a gourmet coffee subscription brand with 23 employees. The brand had strong customer satisfaction, but growth relied on word-of-mouth, its website had poor search visibility, and paid social was expensive with minimal return. [2]

The agency built a keyword strategy around phrases such as "best coffee subscription" and "single-origin beans," published blog content on brewing methods and taste profiles, launched a retargeting ad funnel, and added reviews and certifications to landing pages. [2]

The reported results were a 175% increase in organic traffic, twice as many subscriptions, a 40% drop in cost per subscriber, first-page rankings for more than 20 keywords, and 4x higher social media engagement. [2] The case study does not state the time period or a baseline date, so the timeline is unknown. The clearest lesson is that organic search and trust signals reduced dependence on paid acquisition. Subscription businesses also need follow-up messages, and the [Klaviyo email guide for coffee shops](/blog/using-klaviyo-for-coffee-shop-email-marketing) covers that setup.

## What does a 4.2x ROAS mean for a small shop?

Blackout Coffee Co. is an online US specialty roaster that delivers freshly roasted coffee in two days. According to the agency, it grew from $1.2M per year when it began working with TAS Digital in 2022 to $1.5M in monthly revenue, with a 4.2x blended ROAS and a 39.2% conversion rate. [3] The agency reports spend above $500K per month across Meta, Twitter, Snapchat, Google and Rumble. [3]

Blended ROAS (assumption: standard definition) means total revenue divided by total ad spend across all channels. Blackout's scale is far beyond a single shop, and the figures are the agency's own. What transfers is the habit of measuring return across all channels rather than judging one platform in isolation.

A small shop should first confirm which ad clicks lead to sales. Whether an outside team is worth the cost is a separate question, covered in [can you afford a marketing agency for a coffee shop](/blog/marketing-agency-for-a-coffee-shop-can-you-afford-one).

## What break-even ROAS would a small shop need?

The sources do not set a universal ROAS target, because the right number depends on margin. The example below uses assumptions, labelled as such.

Assumptions: a gross margin of 65% of revenue (assumption), a single campaign with a $1,000 monthly budget (assumption), and Blackout's 4.2x used only as a reference point (source figure applied to an assumed budget).

Break-even ROAS = 1 ÷ 0.65, which is about 1.54x. Below that, ads lose money even before overhead.

| Scenario | Ad spend | ROAS | Revenue | Gross profit after ads |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Weak campaign (assumption) | $1,000 | 1.2x | $1,200 | -$220 |
| Break-even region (assumption) | $1,000 | 2.0x | $2,000 | $300 |
| Reference from Blackout (assumption) | $1,000 | 4.2x | $4,200 | $1,730 |

Gross profit after ads equals revenue multiplied by 0.65, minus the $1,000 spend. The table shows why a campaign reporting 2.0x can still be profitable while one at 1.2x is not, even though both look like returns at first glance. A shop that reports 4.2x on paper but has a thin margin may still lose money once staff and rent are counted.

## Which steps can a coffee shop copy first?

The cases point to a short sequence of steps. Each one draws on a specific result above.

1. Build a keyword list of buying phrases for your products, then publish content on brewing methods or taste profiles, as Brick Marketing did for its subscription client. [2]
2. Add reviews and certifications to the pages where people order or sign up. [2]
3. Start retargeting past website visitors before spending on cold audiences, since Brick's retargeting funnel reaches people who already know the brand. [2]
4. Track cost per new customer or subscriber every month, the same metric Brick used to show a 40% reduction. [2] Tools in the [AI marketing automation guide for coffee shops](/blog/ai-tools-for-coffee-shop-marketing-automation) can help log that data.
5. Test your own checkout on a phone. Bottega's 49% mobile and tablet conversion gain suggests mobile friction is worth fixing first. [1]

## FAQ

### Are these case studies independently verified?

No. Each one is published by the agency that ran the campaign, so the results are vendor-reported. The sources do not provide audited baselines or full methodology.

### Do these results apply to a single coffee shop?

Only partly. The three businesses sold online and shipped products, while a café sells in person. The measurement habits transfer more easily than the percentages.

### What ROAS should a small business aim for?

The sources do not set a universal target. Start with break-even ROAS, which depends on your margin. At an assumed 65% margin, the break-even point in the worked example is about 1.54x.

### Which metric should a shop track first?

Track cost per new customer, tied to the ad spend that produced it. Brick Marketing reported its results using cost per subscriber, which is the same logic applied to a different sales model. [2]
