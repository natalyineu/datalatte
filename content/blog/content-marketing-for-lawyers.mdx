Content marketing for a law firm means publishing useful, accurate pages that answer what a prospective client is searching for, then making it easy to call. It matters because paid clicks are expensive: LocaliQ's 2026 benchmarks put Attorneys and Legal Services at $9.87 per click and $131.63 per lead on Google Ads. Content takes longer, but it keeps working after the writing is paid for.

This guide is for solo lawyers and small firms in the US. It is marketing information, not legal advice. Advertising rules differ by state, so check your own bar's rules. Examples are labelled as assumptions, and no firm results are claimed.

## Why Does Content Matter When Ads Cost This Much?

At $9.87 a click and a 5.55% conversion rate, the LocaliQ average cost per lead is $131.63, against $66.69 across all industries. A lead is an inquiry, not a retained client. Many inquiries never become cases, so the cost per signed client is higher.

A page that ranks for a question such as "what to do after a car accident in Texas" can keep bringing inquiries without a per-click fee. Ranking is not guaranteed and takes time. No source here gives a typical ranking timeline, so plan for months, not weeks.

## What Does Google Expect From Legal Content?

Google's guidance on helpful content asks for people-first content that is created primarily to help people, not to manipulate search rankings. It says trust is the most important part of experience, expertise, authoritativeness and trust (E-E-A-T), and that visitors should be able to tell who wrote a page.

Google also treats topics that affect health, finances, safety or well-being as "Your Money or Your Life," which need higher accuracy and consistency with expert consensus. Legal advice fits that description. So put a real lawyer's name and bar admissions on each page, cite statutes and court sites, and date every article. Do not use invented author profiles, which Google calls a form of deception.

## What Do Advertising Rules Require?

Lawyer advertising is regulated state by state. California's Rule 7.1 says a lawyer shall not make a false or misleading communication about the lawyer or the lawyer's services, including by omitting a fact needed to avoid misleading. A comment to the rule says an express guarantee of a particular result is misleading.

California's Rule 7.2(c) requires a communication to include the name and address of at least one lawyer or law firm responsible for its content. Rule 7.2(b) generally bars paying someone to recommend your services, with exceptions such as paying reasonable advertising costs. Rule 7.3(c) requires "Advertisement" labels on certain direct solicitations. Check your own state's version before publishing, particularly on testimonials and past results.

## Which Content Types Win Local Clients?

Match content to a decision a client is making:

| Content | Question it answers | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Practice area pages | "Do you handle my type of case here?" | One page per service and city you serve |
| Process guides | "What happens after I file?" | Steps, timelines from court sources, fees explained |
| Cost and fee pages | "How is a lawyer paid?" | Explain fee structures plainly; avoid guarantees |
| FAQs | The ten calls your intake team gets | Answer briefly, link to the contact form |
| Location pages | "Lawyer near me" searches | Real address, hours and directions |

## How Do You Make Content Turn Into Calls?

Content only pays if inquiries get answered. Clio's 2024 secret-shopper test of 500 law firms, reported by the ABA Journal, found only 40% answered calls (56% in 2019), one-third replied to email, and just 18% of email replies explained next steps and cost. Among firms that replied by email, 84% did so within eight hours.

That is a gap you can fill: answer the phone or reply fast, state what happens next, and say what the first consultation costs. Also complete your Google Business Profile. Google says local results depend on relevance, distance and prominence, and that reviews can help prominence. ## The Math: Content or Ads?

All inputs are **assumptions** except the LocaliQ cost per lead.

| Input | Assumption |
| --- | --- |
| One strong practice page, written and reviewed | $600 one-off |
| Inquiries it brings per month once ranking | 2 |
| Same 2 inquiries via Google Ads | 2 x $131.63 = about $263 a month |
| Payback of the page against ads | about 2.3 months |
| Share of inquiries that sign | 20% |
| Value per signed matter | $3,000 |

At 20% signing, two inquiries a month means 0.4 signed matters, or about $1,200 of fees a month. Ten pages is a bigger bet, and the ranking assumption may not hold. The point is to compare payback against ads using your own figures. ## FAQ

### How much does content marketing cost for a law firm?

No standard price is published. Costs depend on who writes and reviews the pages. Budget for a lawyer's review time, not only the writer.

### How long until content brings clients?

No reliable timeline is published. Rankings depend on competition and quality, so measure inquiries over several months.

### Can I use client testimonials and past results?

Rules differ by state. California's Rule 7.1 prohibits misleading communications, including omitted facts. Check your bar's rules and ethics opinions first.

### Should I use AI to write legal content?

Google's guidance says it is helpful to tell readers how content was produced. Any AI draft needs review by a licensed lawyer before publication.

### What about the first reply to an inquiry?
