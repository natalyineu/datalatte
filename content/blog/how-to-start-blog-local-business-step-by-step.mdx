To start a blog for a local business, add a blog section to your existing website, list the questions customers ask, write one clear answer per post, and check results in Google's free Search Console. Google says some changes take effect in hours and others take several months, so judge the blog after weeks, not days.

You do not need a separate site. A blog helps most when it answers real questions in your area and links to the page where someone can book or call. No source here promises traffic or sales from a set number of posts.

## Should a Local Business Have a Blog?

Only if you can answer questions customers actually ask. A blog gives each question its own page that can appear in search, and it gives you something useful to share by email and social media. It does not replace a Google Business Profile, which handles most "near me" searches. Set that up first with the [Google Business Profile checklist](/blog/google-business-profile-optimization-checklist).

Skip the blog if you cannot keep it accurate. Google asks whether content is created primarily to help people or to attract search visits, and warns against summarising others without adding value.

## Step 1: Where Should the Blog Live?

Put it on your own domain, as a /blog section, so the links and visits support your main site. Options:

| Option | Starting cost | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| WordPress.com Free | $0 | WordPress.com subdomain, no custom domain, no plugins, ads shown to visitors, 1 GB storage |
| WordPress.com Personal | $4 a month (annual billing) | Plugins and a free domain for one year |
| WordPress.com Business | $25 a month (annual billing) | Plugins and free domain for one year |
| Your existing site builder | Check your plan | Most include a blog; confirm before switching |

Prices come from WordPress.com's pricing page on 1 November 2026 and can change. Squarespace's pricing page does not label monthly versus annual prices clearly enough to quote here, so check it directly.

A free subdomain is fine for a trial but weak for a business, since the address is not yours.

## Step 2: How Do You Pick Topics?

Write down real questions, not keywords. Sources: what customers ask at the counter, emails, phone calls and reviews. Then check which searches already bring people to you in Search Console, which Google describes as a free service that monitors your site's presence in Search.

Good post types for local businesses:

- Price and timing questions you can answer plainly
- How to choose between your services
- What to prepare before a visit
- Local events or seasonal needs tied to what you sell

Avoid pages that only repeat what other sites say.

## Step 3: What Should a Good Post Look Like?

Google's SEO guide says a good title is unique, clear and accurately describes the page. A good meta description is short, unique and includes the most relevant points. Use words in URLs that make sense to people, and write descriptive link text.

Structure the post for scanning. Nielsen Norman Group found 79% of test users always scanned new pages and only 16% read word by word, and recommended meaningful subheadings, lists and one idea per paragraph. Put the answer first, then the detail. See [how to write website copy](/blog/how-to-write-website-copy-local-business-examples).

## Step 4: How Do You Show Who Wrote It?

Google's people-first guidance asks whether it is clear who wrote the content and whether it shows first-hand expertise. Add an author name and a short line on who you are and what you do. If you use automation or AI to help, Google suggests explaining how it was used. Only describe real experience you have.

Avoid invented customer stories or numbers. If you quote a statistic, link to its source.

## Step 5: How Do You Link the Blog to Sales?

Each post should lead to one next step: book, call, get directions or see prices (see [landing page best practices](/blog/landing-page-best-practices)). Link to the relevant service page and keep the phone number and hours visible. Also link between related posts with descriptive link text.

## Step 6: What Should You Track?

Verify your site in Search Console and look at queries and pages. Google says to wait a few weeks before judging whether a change had a beneficial effect. Also count calls, bookings and form requests that mention the blog.

## A Worked Example: A Simple Publishing Plan

All numbers are **assumptions**, not benchmarks.

- Time per post: 3 hours to research, write and check
- Pace: 2 posts a month
- Monthly effort: 6 hours
- Value of 1 new customer from search: $150
- Break-even on time: if an hour is worth $30, six hours cost $180, so you need 2 new customers a month to cover it

Do not expect that in month one. If you cannot spare six hours, publish one post a month and keep it good.

## FAQ

### How often should a local business blog?
No source sets a number. Choose a pace you can keep accurate, such as one or two posts a month.

### How long should a post be?
Long enough to answer the question. Nielsen Norman Group found that shorter text was easier to use, but gives no fixed length for posts.

### How long until a blog brings customers?
Google says some changes take hours and others months. Review progress after a few weeks and again after a few months.

### Do I need a separate domain?
No. A /blog section on your main domain is simpler.

### Can I use AI to write posts?
Google asks you to consider how automation is used and whether the content adds value. Check every fact and add real expertise.
