Good retargeting shows the right follow-up to people who visited your site, for a limited time, and stops once they buy. The ten rules below cover audience size, time windows, frequency, exclusions and sensitive categories. Two platform facts anchor them: Google says only segments with more than 100 active users are eligible to run ads, and Meta's website audience retention field accepts 1 to 180 days.

These are practical rules for small local advertisers. Where a number comes from a platform, it is cited. Where it is advice, it is labelled as such.

## What Are the 10 Retargeting Rules?

| # | Rule | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Check the list is big enough | Google: more than 100 active users |
| 2 | Segment by what people did | Advice |
| 3 | Set a time window | Meta: 1 to 180 days |
| 4 | Cap how often people see ads | Google Display and Video |
| 5 | Exclude people who already bought | Advice |
| 6 | Match the message to the step | Advice |
| 7 | Rotate creative | Advice |
| 8 | Respect sensitive categories | Google policy |
| 9 | Count bookings, not clicks | Advice |
| 10 | Pair ads with email or SMS | Advice |

The sections below explain each rule.

## Rule 1: Is Your Audience Big Enough?

Google's help says "only segments with more than 100 active users will be eligible to run ads." A local business with a few visits a week may take time to reach that. Meta says only that you can target an audience "once it reaches a certain size," and gives no number.

If your list is small, do not force a campaign. Spend on traffic sources first, or use a broader window so more visitors are counted.

## Rules 2 and 3: How Should You Segment and Set Windows?

Segment by action: service-page visitors, booking-page visitors who did not finish, and past customers are three different people. Each needs its own message.

For the window, Meta's developer docs say retention is "between 1 and 180 days," although another line of the same page mentions 365 days, so the page is inconsistent. A short window suits fast decisions such as a table or a haircut. A longer one suits considered purchases such as a gym membership or home project. Test both on your own data.

## Rule 4: How Often Should People See Your Ad?

Google says frequency capping works in Display and Video campaigns, is not supported in Demand Gen, and for Display counts only viewable impressions. Display caps can apply at campaign, ad group or ad level, and Google recommends letting it optimize frequency automatically as an option. Video caps are set at campaign level by day, week or month.

No source gives a perfect number of views. If you can set a cap, begin low and raise it only if bookings rise. If a platform has no cap, shorten the window instead.

## Rules 5 and 6: Who Should You Exclude?

Exclude customers who have already booked, bought or filled in the form, so they stop seeing "come back" ads. This is advice, not a platform quote, but it is one of the cheapest fixes. Then match the message to the step. A visitor who looked at prices needs an offer or proof. One who abandoned a booking needs a reminder and a simple link.

Our guide to [local retargeting ads and costs](/blog/retargeting-ads-for-local-business) shows how the pieces fit, and [how to set up retargeting](/blog/how-to-set-up-retargeting-on-google-facebook-and-more) covers the pixel and tag.

## Rule 7: How Do You Avoid Ad Fatigue?

Change the image or headline when the same people keep seeing it. Platform reports show frequency and click rate, so look for falling clicks as a signal. Keep two or three versions ready: a reminder, a review or proof ad, and an offer.

Do not rely on one banner for months. A prospect who has seen it a dozen times is unlikely to react to a thirteenth.

## Rule 8: Which Businesses Face Restrictions?

Google's personalized advertising policy lists health among its sensitive interest categories. Advertisers promoting in these categories cannot use advertiser-curated audiences, which include your data segments and customer match. It also lists other categories such as alcohol and gambling.

If you run a clinic, therapy practice or similar service, check the policy before building lists. Fitness and wellness wording can sit near these categories, so read the policy for your ad text.

## Rules 9 and 10: What Should You Measure?

Count bookings, calls and visits, not just clicks. Compare retargeted visitors with similar visitors who did not see ads if you can. Also pair ads with email or SMS: for the same visitor, an email can follow up where an ad is capped. ## Worked Example: A Small Window Test

Inputs are **assumptions**: a booking page with 600 visitors a month, 70% leave without booking, a $300 retargeting budget, and a $1.50 CPC. Replace each with your own numbers.

## FAQ

### What is retargeting?

It shows ads to people who already visited your website or interacted with your business. Google calls this using your data segments.

### How many visitors do I need for retargeting?

Google requires more than 100 active users in a segment. Meta gives no number.

### How long should the window be?

Meta accepts 1 to 180 days in its website audience field. Choose a shorter window for quick decisions and test a longer one for bigger purchases.

### How often is too often?

No source publishes a safe number. Use frequency caps where they exist, and watch bookings.

### Can health businesses use retargeting?

Google restricts advertiser-curated audiences for health ads. Read the personalized advertising policy before you start.
