In BrightLocal's 2026 Local Consumer Review Survey, 89% of consumers said they expect business owners to respond to reviews, and 19% expect that reply the same day. Most small owners cannot reply that fast by hand: you are cutting hair, pulling shots or coaching a class. An AI agent can close the gap by watching for new reviews, drafting a reply in your voice and holding anything risky for you. The catch is that Google and the FTC both have rules about reviews, and a badly configured agent can break them.

This guide shows how a review agent actually works, what Google requires before you can connect one, which reviews it should never touch alone, and what it costs. It is for coffee shops, salons, groomers and studios in the US, UK, Australia and Canada.

## How a Review Agent Works

Strip away the marketing and a review agent is four steps in a loop:

1. **Fetch.** Check your Business Profile for new reviews every few hours.
2. **Classify.** Label each review by star rating and topic (praise, slow service, price, staff, safety, possible fake).
3. **Draft.** Write a reply using your tone notes and details from the review.
4. **Route.** Send low-risk drafts to a quick approval queue, and anything sensitive to you personally.

Posting is the only step with consequences, so keep it behind your approval at first.

## Getting Access: Google Requires Approval

You cannot just point software at Google reviews. Google's [Business Profile API prerequisites](https://developers.google.com/my-business/content/prereqs) say you need:

- A Business Profile that has been verified and active for 60 or more days (yours or a client's)
- A website for the business, listed on the Profile
- A Google Cloud project and a submitted "Application for Basic API Access" from an email that owns or manages the Profile

Google says a quota of 0 queries per minute in the Cloud console means the project is not yet approved, and 300 means it is. The [review data page](https://developers.google.com/my-business/content/review-data) shows the two calls an agent uses: a GET to list reviews, and a PUT to the review's `/reply` endpoint with a JSON body such as `{"comment": "Thank you for visiting our business!"}`. It creates the reply if none exists or updates the existing one. To reply through the API, a Google Workspace administrator must also enable Search and/or Maps with Business Profile for the account.

If that sounds like a lot, it is the reason most small businesses use a hosted tool that has already been approved. Ask any vendor whether it connects through Google's official API; our [AI review management service](/services/ai-agents/review-management) is built that way.

## Rules the Agent Must Follow

### Replies

Google checks every reply against its content policies. Its help page says checks usually take about 10 minutes but can occasionally take up to 30 days, and that an unapproved reply will be returned for editing. Do not let the agent post private details, admit legal fault or offer compensation in public. Our guides on [negative review scripts](/blog/how-to-respond-negative-google-reviews-scripts) and [fake reviews](/blog/how-to-deal-with-fake-google-reviews) cover the wording.

### Requests

Google's [Maps contributor policy](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114) is blunt. Merchants may not offer incentives such as payment, discounts or free goods for reviews, may not "selectively solicit positive reviews", may not discourage negative ones, and may not require staff to solicit a set number of reviews. Genuine requests are allowed. Google's [review tips page](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3474122) says you can send a shareable link or QR code.

So the agent should send the same request to every customer who visited, not only the ones who seemed happy. That excludes the common "How was your visit? 1 to 5" funnel that only forwards happy customers to Google.

### Fake reviews

The FTC's [final rule on fake reviews](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/08/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-rule-banning-fake-reviews-testimonials) bans fake reviews that misrepresent who wrote them, "such as AI-generated fake reviews". The FTC's [Q&A](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/consumer-reviews-testimonials-rule-questions-answers) adds that incentives cannot be conditioned on the review being positive, and that businesses should not intimidate reviewers or make unfounded legal threats. It says the rule has no specific ban on asking only customers you think are happy, but that the practice could still violate the FTC Act. Never have an agent write a review as a customer. That includes "customers" who are staff.

## What to Automate and What Not To

| Situation | Handling |
| --- | --- |
| 5-star with a comment | Agent drafts; owner approves in bulk for the first month, then optionally auto-post |
| 5-star, no text | Short thank-you, can be automated |
| 3-4 stars or mixed | Agent drafts; owner edits and approves |
| 1-2 stars or complaint | Owner writes or heavily edits; agent only summarises the issue |
| Possible fake or competitor | Owner decides; neutral reply and report through your Profile |
| Health, injury, legal or discrimination claims | Human only |

Add a hard stop word list: "refund", "lawyer", "injured", "sick", "police", "discrimination". Any review containing one goes straight to you.

## A Prompt That Works

Give the model the review text, star rating, your business name and a short voice guide. Something like:

> You write replies for [Business] in [Town]. Tone: warm, brief, no exclamation marks overload. Use the customer's first name and one specific detail they mentioned. Never offer discounts, never admit fault, never mention staff by surname. For 1-3 stars, thank them, apologise that the visit fell short, and invite them to contact [phone/email]. Output the reply only. If the review mentions health, safety, money or legal action, output HOLD.

The word HOLD is your safety valve: the pipeline treats it as "send to the owner". Test with ten of your own old reviews before connecting anything live. Our [auto-reply script walkthrough](/blog/ai-agent-google-reviews-auto-reply-script) shows the code side.

## Weekly Routine (15 Minutes)

1. Clear the approval queue and edit any draft that does not sound like you.
2. Read every 1-3 star review personally and decide on the real-world fix.
3. Spot-check five posted replies for wrong names or details.
4. Look for patterns: three mentions of waiting time is an operations problem, not a reply problem.
5. Check the request log to confirm every customer received the same message.

## Do This Today

1. Count your reviews from the last 90 days and how many have no reply. That is your backlog and your volume estimate.
2. Write a one-paragraph voice guide with three example replies you are proud of and three phrases you never use.
3. Check whether your Business Profile has been verified for 60 days and lists a website, which is the API prerequisite.
4. Run the prompt above on ten old reviews in a chat window and note which ones should have been HOLD.
5. Decide your approval rule (everything for the first month) and block out 15 minutes on your calendar each week.

## FAQ

**Q: Will AI replies sound fake?**
A: They can if every reply follows one template. Include a specific detail from each review, vary the openings and edit the first batch until it sounds like you. Keep a person on negative reviews.

**Q: Can an AI agent post replies automatically?**
A: Technically yes, through the Business Profile API once your project is approved. Google reviews replies against its content policies, so start with draft-and-approve rather than auto-posting.

**Q: Is it allowed to offer a discount for a review?**
A: No. Google's policy lists payment, discounts and free goods or services as prohibited incentives, and the FTC says incentives cannot depend on the review being positive.

**Q: Can I use AI to write the reviews themselves?**
A: No. The FTC rule bans fake reviews, including AI-generated ones, that misrepresent who wrote them. Ask real customers.

**Q: How do I handle a suspected fake review?**
A: Reply neutrally, report it through your Business Profile and avoid accusing the reviewer. The FTC's Q&A says false accusations about reviewers and unfounded legal threats are not allowed when used to suppress reviews.
