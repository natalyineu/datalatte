For a local business, affiliate marketing means paying partners, customers or local creators a reward when they send you a paying customer. You only pay when a sale or booking happens, which makes it lower risk than most advertising, but it needs clear rules, simple tracking and a few good partners to work.

Large online affiliate programs get most of the attention, but the same idea fits a salon, a gym or a coffee shop. A neighbour recommends you and gets a thank-you. A local blogger shares a code and earns a commission. Here is how to set up a small, practical program without overcomplicating it.

## What affiliate marketing looks like locally

An affiliate (or partner or referrer) promotes your business using a unique link, code or form. When someone uses it and becomes a customer, the affiliate receives a reward. Common local versions:

| Model | Who promotes | Typical reward | Good for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Customer referral | Your existing clients | Credit, free product or discount | Almost any local business |
| Local partner | Complementary businesses (wedding planner, physio, pet shop) | Commission or reciprocal referrals | Service businesses |
| Creator or influencer | Local bloggers, Instagram creators, community admins | Free product plus commission or flat fee | Cafes, salons, studios |
| Organisation fundraiser | Schools, clubs, charities | Percentage of sales to the cause | Restaurants, retail |
| Online affiliates | Coupon or review sites, networks | Percentage per sale | Businesses selling online |

For most small businesses, the first two are the best starting point.

## Is it right for your business?

Affiliate or referral programs suit you if:

- You earn enough per customer to pay a reward and still profit
- Customers are likely to talk to others (local, personal, visible services)
- You can track where new customers came from
- You can honour rewards promptly

They fit less well if margins are very thin or purchases are rare and anonymous. Work out your numbers first.

## Work out what you can pay

Start with the value of a new customer. For example, think through:

- What does a typical first visit or order bring in?
- How many times do customers return, on average?
- What are your direct costs for those visits?

Then decide a reward you are comfortable with. A common approach is to compare it with what you currently pay for a new customer through ads. If a new customer typically costs you $30 to $60 in advertising, a $10 to $25 reward or credit may be attractive, since you only pay on results. Use your own figures, and make sure the program still leaves a margin.

Rewards can be money, store credit, free services or a mix. Credit and free services are often better for small businesses, since they cost you less than their face value and bring the reward-giver back.

## Set simple rules

Write the terms down in plain language:

- Who counts as a new customer (first booking, never purchased before)
- When the reward is earned (after the first paid visit, not at sign-up)
- How it is delivered and how long it takes
- Any limits per person or per month
- What counts as abuse (self-referral, fake bookings)
- Whether the new customer also gets a welcome offer

Keep it fair and visible, so partners trust it and you avoid disputes.

## Choose how to track referrals

You need a reliable way to know who sent whom. Options, from simplest to most advanced:

1. **Referral by name**: The new customer says who referred them at booking. Free, but depends on honesty and memory.
2. **Unique codes**: Each partner gets a code (for example ANNA10) entered at checkout or told at booking.
3. **Unique links**: A tracked URL with UTM parameters, or a dedicated booking page per partner.
4. **Referral software**: Tools such as ReferralCandy, Referral Factory, Friendbuy or the loyalty features built into booking and point-of-sale systems can automate links, codes and payouts. Pricing varies, and many offer monthly plans or free tiers for small volumes.
5. **Affiliate platforms**: For online sales, platforms like Rewardful, Tapfiliate or Shopify apps handle commissions and reporting.

Choose the least complicated option that works for your volume. Many local businesses start with codes and a spreadsheet.

## Who to recruit

- **Your best customers**: Those who already recommend you. Ask in person, by email and at the checkout.
- **Local complementary businesses**: A wedding florist and a photographer, a physio and a gym, a groomer and a vet or pet shop. Agree on referrals in both directions where possible.
- **Local creators and micro-influencers**: Accounts with a few thousand local followers can be effective and affordable.
- **Community leaders**: Admins of local parent, runner or dog-owner groups, where allowed by group rules.
- **Employees**: Give staff a small bonus for referring new customers.

Approach people personally. Explain who your ideal customer is, what the reward is and how it works. Offer a trial yourself so they can speak with confidence.

## Stay compliant and honest

- **Disclosure**: In many countries, promoters must disclose paid or rewarded recommendations (for example the FTC in the US and the ASA/CMA in the UK). Ask partners to label posts clearly, such as "ad" or "affiliate".
- **Platform rules**: Check Instagram, Facebook and Google rules before running promotions or giveaways.
- **Regulated industries**: Some sectors, such as healthcare, finance and legal services, restrict referral payments. Check your professional rules.
- **Privacy**: Collect referral data lawfully, and get consent before contacting people.
- **Tax**: Treat commissions as business expenses, and ask an accountant about reporting for regular payouts.

This is general information, not legal advice.

## Launch plan: four weeks

**Week 1: Design the offer.** Calculate your numbers, choose the reward, write simple terms and choose your tracking method.

**Week 2: Build the basics.** Create a landing page or short form explaining the program. Prepare codes, links or a spreadsheet. Design a simple card or QR code for customers.

**Week 3: Recruit.** Invite your top 10 to 20 customers and three or four local partners. Give each a code or link and a short, ready-to-use message.

**Week 4: Launch and promote.** Announce the program on your website, email list, social profiles and at the counter. Make sure staff can explain it. Then track every referral.

## Make it easy to refer

People are busy, so lower the effort:

- Provide a ready-made message they can forward
- Offer a share link or QR code
- Put a mention on receipts, confirmation emails and packaging
- Remind customers after a good experience, when they are most likely to share

Combine this with a review request. ## Measure results

Track a few simple numbers monthly:

- Number of referrals and how many converted
- Revenue from referred customers
- Cost of rewards paid
- Whether referred customers return more often than average
- Which partners perform best

If results are good, increase rewards for top partners or expand the program. If not, simplify the offer, improve the message, or try different partners.

## Common mistakes

- Rewarding sign-ups rather than paying customers
- Complicated rules and slow payouts
- No tracking, so you cannot tell what works
- Recruiting too many partners at once and neglecting them
- Ignoring disclosure rules
- Offering a reward larger than your margin allows

## FAQ

**Q: Is affiliate marketing the same as a referral program?**
A: They are closely related. Referral programs usually reward customers for introducing friends, while affiliate programs typically involve partners or creators who promote you more broadly, often for a commission.

**Q: How much commission should I pay?**
A: It depends on your margins and what you currently pay to acquire customers. Many local businesses use a flat reward or credit instead of a percentage, set well below the profit from a new customer.

**Q: Do I need special software?**
A: Not at first. Unique codes and a spreadsheet are enough for a small program. Move to referral software when volume grows.

**Q: Do I have to disclose affiliate relationships?**
A: In many countries, yes. Promoters generally need to disclose that they are rewarded for recommendations. Check advertising regulations in your market.
