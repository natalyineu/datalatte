Affiliate marketing for a local business means rewarding people who promote you, such as regular customers, neighbouring businesses or local creators, only when their referral becomes a paying customer. You pay for results rather than attention. The model suits services that are easy to recommend and businesses whose margins can absorb a reward. Most local businesses should start with a referral or partner programme, track each referral with a code, and keep rewards well below the profit a new customer generates.

## What is affiliate marketing for a local business?

Affiliate marketing involves a brand recruiting creators and entrepreneurs, called affiliates, to promote its products or services. Affiliates share trackable links and earn commission when someone buys through them ([Referral Rock](https://referralrock.com/affiliate/affiliate-marketing-for-small-business/)). For a local business, the affiliate is often a happy customer, a physiotherapist who refers patients, or a pet shop that recommends a groomer.

Pay per sale is the most common structure. Other models exist, including pay per action, pay per lead, pay per install and pay per click ([WGU, citing industry practice](https://www.wgu.edu/blog/affiliate-marketing2208.html)). For most local services, pay per lead or a flat reward for a first paid visit is easier to explain and to track than a percentage of every sale.

## Is affiliate marketing worth it for a local service business?

Referral Rock notes that affiliate marketing works best with products that have wide appeal and margins that can support ongoing commissions. If those conditions are missing, a customer referral program is often the better fit ([Referral Rock](https://referralrock.com/affiliate/affiliate-marketing-for-small-business/)). Services are harder to promote than physical products, because affiliates reach a narrower audience and must demonstrate the offer themselves.

The wider picture is still encouraging. Salesforce reports that 81% of SMBs say customer referrals and affiliate marketing have helped them grow ([Salesforce](https://www.salesforce.com/blog/small-business/affiliate-marketing-tips-for-startups/?bc=OTH)). Keap cites a figure that affiliate programs account for 15 to 30% of online sales, though that number comes from online retail and may not transfer to a salon or café ([Keap](https://keap.com/small-business-automation-blog/marketing/digital-marketing/5-ways-small-businesses-can-use-affiliate-marketing-to-increase-sales)).

## How do you set a reward you can afford?

Keap describes 10% as the most common affiliate commission and calls it a reasonable starting point, since it attracts good affiliates without cutting too far into profit margins ([Keap](https://keap.com/small-business-automation-blog/marketing/digital-marketing/5-ways-small-businesses-can-use-affiliate-marketing-to-increase-sales)). A percentage works for a shop with a fixed margin. For a salon or studio, a flat credit or free service is usually simpler to explain and budget.

Start from gross profit. Referral Rock points out that your margins determine how much you can spend on commissions, and that a new business may not be ready to pay on every sale. Set the reward well below first-year gross profit per customer, and pay it only after the referred customer's first paid visit. Keep the reward at a level that keeps the program profitable even after admin time.

## How do you track which partner brought each customer?

Affiliates share trackable links, so each partner needs their own identifier ([Referral Rock](https://referralrock.com/affiliate/affiliate-marketing-for-small-business/)). For a small local program, a unique code per partner, entered at booking, is enough. Add a "who referred you?" question to the booking form and keep a simple sheet with the date, partner, new customer, first-visit status and reward paid.

Affiliate networks track referrals and handle payouts, but Salesforce describes them as optional ([Salesforce](https://www.salesforce.com/blog/small-business/affiliate-marketing-tips-for-startups/?bc=OTH)). Referral Rock advises against networks for small businesses because they own your data, charge fees and put you in competition with bigger brands for the best affiliates ([Referral Rock](https://referralrock.com/affiliate/affiliate-marketing-for-small-business/)). Most local programs do not need one. Tools for tracking can help later; see [AI tools that could replace a marketing assistant](/blog/ai-tools-replace-marketing-assistant-local-business) for options.

## How do you keep partners engaged?

Affiliates are an extension of your business. Salesforce recommends clear guidelines, marketing assets and regular updates so partners represent you accurately ([Salesforce](https://www.salesforce.com/blog/small-business/affiliate-marketing-tips-for-startups/?bc=OTH)). Referral Rock makes the same point from another angle: the best affiliates are partners, not promoters, and consistent communication separates programs that compound from ones that fizzle out ([Referral Rock](https://referralrock.com/affiliate/affiliate-marketing-for-small-business/)).

Give each partner a code, a ready-made message and a free trial of the service, so they recommend it from experience. Keap also suggests rewarding top affiliates and showing social proof, such as partner stories, because user-generated content often carries more weight than a brand's own claims ([Keap](https://keap.com/small-business-automation-blog/marketing/digital-marketing/5-ways-small-businesses-can-use-affiliate-marketing-to-increase-sales)). Ready-made partner copy can be drafted quickly with [ChatGPT prompts for local business marketing](/blog/chatgpt-prompts-for-local-business-marketing), then checked by you.

## What disclosure rules apply to paid referrals?

None of the sources used here set out disclosure law, so this guide does not summarise it. Once a partner is paid to promote you in public, such as in a post, video or story, that promotion may need to be labelled. Advertising regulators in the US, UK, Canada and Australia each publish current guidance, and the rules differ by country.

Two practical steps apply everywhere. Put a labelling requirement in every partner agreement, and reward referrals for paid bookings rather than for public reviews. Check the current guidance from your own regulator before you launch, and consider a professional adviser if you work in a regulated field such as healthcare or finance.

| Line | Figure (all assumption) |
| --- | --- |
| Regulars who refer in a month | 40 |
| Referrals per regular per month | 0.15 |
| Referred contacts per month | 6 |
| Share who book a first visit | 50% |
| New customers per month | 3 |
| Reward to referring customer | $20 credit |
| Welcome offer for new customer | $10 |
| Admin time (15 minutes at $25 an hour) | $6.25 |
| Cost per new customer | $36.25 |
| Gross profit per customer in year one | $180 |
| Gross profit left after reward and offer | $143.75 |
| Monthly program cost (3 customers at $36.25) | $108.75 |

The reward plus welcome offer is $30, which is about 17% of the $180 gross profit assumed here. That leaves room in the margin, but the ceiling is what matters. If gross profit per customer falls to $120, the same $30 reward would take a quarter of it. Recalculate whenever prices or margins change.

The program is also limited by how many regulars refer. Forty regulars at 0.15 referrals each produces only six contacts a month under these assumptions. Referrals can be earned at scale only slowly, so treat the program as a steady channel rather than an instant fix.

## What should you do this week?

1. Calculate gross profit per new customer in year one, and write it down as your reward ceiling.
2. Choose a flat reward well below that ceiling, payable only after the first paid visit.
3. Create one code per partner and a six-column tracking sheet, and add a "referred by" question to your booking form.
4. Write a one-page rule card covering who counts as new, when the reward is earned, limits and abuse.
5. Invite five partners: three regulars and two neighbouring businesses, each with a free trial.

## FAQ

### Is affiliate marketing the same as a referral program?

They overlap but are not identical. Customer referral programs tap the customers you already have, who recommend you to people they know. Affiliate programs rely on external partners, who may never have been your customer, and promote you to their own audience ([Referral Rock](https://referralrock.com/affiliate/affiliate-marketing-for-small-business/)).

### Should I pay commission on every sale?

Not necessarily. Referral Rock notes that a newer business may not be ready to pay commission on every sale, and may prefer store credit, discounts or other rewards for its best customers ([Referral Rock](https://referralrock.com/affiliate/affiliate-marketing-for-small-business/)).

### Do I need an affiliate network?

Usually not for a local business. Referral Rock advises against networks for small businesses because they control your data and charge fees, and Salesforce describes them as optional ([Salesforce](https://www.salesforce.com/blog/small-business/affiliate-marketing-tips-for-startups/?bc=OTH)).

### How many partners should I start with?

Start small. Five partners who are engaged will do more than fifty who never post, and you can track five by hand with codes and a spreadsheet.
