A small business can track local competitors with three free public sources and an AI assistant to summarize them. Google Alerts emails you when matching results appear, Google's Ads Transparency Center shows what ads an advertiser has run, and competitors' public Google reviews show what customers praise or dislike. Thirty minutes a week is enough. No source checked publishes how much time or money this saves, so treat the routine below as a method, not a benchmark.

The AI part is the reading, not the finding. Paste what you collected into an assistant and ask for a summary, then check it against the originals.

## What Should a Small Business Track About Competitors?

Track things that change what you do this month: new services and prices, new ads and offers, new locations, and review themes. Ignore everything else. A salon owner needs to know that a rival started advertising bridal packages. They do not need a full social media audit.

Pick three to five competitors you actually lose customers to. List them in a note with their website, Google Business Profile link and social pages. A short list is easier to keep current than a long one.

## How Do You Set Up Free Alerts?

Google Alerts is the simplest start. Google's help page says you enter a topic, optionally open Show options, and click Create Alert, and then you get emails when matching results appear. You can change the region, language, sources and how many results you see.

Create one alert per competitor name, one for each competitor's owner name if public, and one for your own business name. The help page does not list the frequency choices, so check them in the tool. Alerts only catch what Google indexes, so they miss private posts and many social updates.

## How Do You See Competitor Ads?

Use the Google Ads Transparency Center. Google's advertiser verification page says people can search for an advertiser and check information about the advertiser and its ads. The public information it lists includes ad creatives, the dates and locations where ads served, and organization contact information. Ads on Search and YouTube can be found there.

Search each competitor by name or website once a month and save screenshots of new ads. Look at the offer, the landing page and the wording. This is a quick way to learn what they pay to promote. Meta runs a similar Ad Library, but its help page did not load when this article was checked, so confirm its features yourself. Our guide to [Google reviews strategy](/blog/google-business-reviews-strategy) shows how to use what you learn.

## Can AI Read Competitor Reviews for You?

Yes, as a summarizer. Copy a competitor's recent public reviews into an AI assistant and ask it to list the three most common praises and complaints, with quotes. Then read a sample yourself, because an assistant can misread sarcasm or mix up businesses.

Respect each platform's terms before collecting data automatically. Manual copying of a few reviews is the safe route. Google's help also notes that replies to reviews show you value feedback, so use what you learn on your own profile: see [managing online reviews](/blog/managing-online-reviews-small-business) and our [AI review agent guide](/blog/ai-agent-for-google-reviews-management).

## What Does a Weekly Routine Look Like? A Worked Example

This is an **assumption** for a pet groomer with four competitors. The times are planning figures, not measurements.

| Task | Minutes per week |
| --- | --- |
| Read Google Alerts emails | 5 |
| Check Ads Transparency Center (one competitor in rotation) | 5 |
| Skim new competitor reviews | 10 |
| AI summary of the notes | 5 |
| Decide one action and write it down | 5 |
| Total | 30 |

Over a month that is about two hours. The output should be one decision, such as matching a rival's new-client offer, adding a service page, or answering a repeated complaint on your own profile. If a week produces no decision, reduce the checks.

## FAQ

### Is it legal to look at competitors' ads and reviews?
Reading public pages is normal practice. Automated scraping may break a platform's terms, so check them first. This article is not legal advice.

### Do I need paid competitive intelligence software?
No source checked says you do. The three sources above are free. Paid tools may save time at larger scale.

### How often should I check?
A weekly pass for alerts and reviews and a monthly pass for ads is a reasonable start. The sources checked do not prescribe a schedule.

### Can AI do the whole thing automatically?
It can summarize what you give it. It should not be the only reader of reviews or ads, because it can misread context.

### What if my competitor does not run ads?
Then the Ads Transparency Center will show nothing for them. Focus on alerts and reviews.
