Build it yourself with a website builder if you are a local business that needs a simple site: services, prices, hours, a map and a booking or call button. Hire a developer when you need custom booking logic, many locations, or you would rather spend your hours serving customers. Either route can work, because the real cost is your time plus the revenue a weak site loses.

## What Does a DIY Website Cost?

A builder charges a subscription and you do the work. Squarespace lists four plans: [Basic at $19 a month, Core at $29, Plus at $49 and Advanced at $99 on annual billing](https://www.squarespace.com/pricing), with a year of free domain registration on annual plans. Basic charges a 2% fee on online store sales.

WordPress.com lists [Personal at US$4 a month, Premium at $8, Business at $25 and Commerce at $45, billed yearly](https://wordpress.com/pricing/). Wix publishes plans from roughly $17.77 to $159.77 a month, but its page showed struck-through prices, so confirm the amount at checkout.

Prices are only half the cost. A builder gives you templates, hosting and security updates. It does not give you a clear message, good photos or copy that answers customer questions. Those take hours you either spend or buy.

## What Does Hiring a Developer Cost?

Published ranges vary by who is selling. WebWave, a website builder, reports in a [March 2026 cost guide](https://webwave.me/blog/how-much-does-a-website-cost) that US freelancers charge $2,000 to $10,000 for a build and $500 to $2,000 a year for maintenance. It puts fixed-price brochure sites at $1,500 to $5,000, hourly rates at $50 to $125, and agencies at $5,000 to $40,000 or more.

Treat these as rough: the source sells a competing product, and quotes depend on page count, booking tools and content. Ask three developers for a written scope. Check what is included: copywriting, photos, hosting, a year of fixes and who owns the files.

## How Much Does Your Time Cost?

Add your hours to the DIY price. A first-time builder needs time for choosing a template, writing every page, resizing images, setting up a domain and testing on a phone. The figure is personal, so value it: if an hour of your time earns or saves $30 and the site takes 40 hours, that is $1,200 of cost that never appears on an invoice.

If you teach classes, cut hair or groom dogs all day, those hours may come from evenings, which has a different price. If your calendar is open, DIY is cheaper. If every hour is booked, a developer may cost less in real terms.

## Does a Website Still Matter for Local Customers?

Yes. BrightLocal's 2026 survey of 1,002 US adults found that [54% visit a business's website after reading positive reviews](https://www.brightlocal.com/research/local-consumer-review-survey/), up from 32% in 2019. Reviews bring people in, and the website decides whether they call or book. The same page cites BrightLocal's 2025 research that only 40% of local businesses have a dedicated website.

A site that loads slowly or hides the phone number wastes that visit. Google's [Core Web Vitals guidance](https://web.dev/articles/vitals) sets good thresholds of 2.5 seconds for Largest Contentful Paint, 200 milliseconds for Interaction to Next Paint and 0.1 for Cumulative Layout Shift, measured at the 75th percentile. Builders and developers can both meet them. Test your own site before you decide.

## What Does the 3-Year Cost Look Like?

Here is a worked example. Every figure is an **assumption**, so replace it with your own quotes.

| Item (assumption) | DIY builder | Freelancer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Build | $0 | $3,500 |
| Plan or hosting, 36 months | $1,044 ($29 a month) | $540 ($15 a month) |
| Maintenance and fixes | $0 | $1,500 ($500 a year) |
| Your time: 40 hours at $30 | $1,200 | $150 (review only) |
| Total over 3 years | $2,244 | $5,690 |

The gap is about $3,450. If an average booking is worth $60 (assumption), the freelancer site needs about 58 extra bookings over 36 months, or roughly 1.6 a month, to break even. A better site may well deliver that, but only if it is clearer and faster than the DIY version. A weak freelance site does not beat a good DIY one.

## When Should You Build It Yourself?

Choose DIY when most of these are true:

- You offer a few services with fixed prices and one location.
- You can write plainly about what you do, who it is for and how to book.
- You are happy to update it yourself.
- A template with a booking link meets your needs.

Start from our [local business website checklist](/blog/local-business-website-checklist) to cover the basics. After launch, work through [website speed optimization](/blog/website-speed-optimization-small-business) so a slow template does not cost you visits.

## When Should You Hire a Developer?

Hire a professional when the site has a job a template cannot do: custom booking, memberships, multiple locations, integrations with your CRM or a design you cannot get from a builder. Also hire when you know your time is better spent elsewhere.

Check the running costs before signing. ## What Mistakes Do Small Businesses Make?

- **Skipping the brief.** Developers cannot guess your customers. Write down services, prices, areas served and the one action you want a visitor to take.
- **Choosing on price alone.** The cheapest quote often leaves out copy, photos or hosting. Compare scope, not totals.
- **Not owning the account.** Keep the domain and hosting in your name.
- **Forgetting the phone.** Test the site on a mobile device and make the call button the first thing visible.

## FAQ

**Q: Is a DIY website good enough for a local business?**
A: Often yes, for a small site with clear services, contact details and a booking link. It is weaker if you need custom features or have no time to write and maintain the content.

**Q: How much should I pay a freelancer?**
A: One 2026 guide from a builder vendor puts US freelancer builds at $2,000 to $10,000, with brochure sites at $1,500 to $5,000. Quotes vary, so get three written scopes.

**Q: Can I start DIY and hire a developer later?**
A: Yes. Keep your domain and written content, and a developer can rebuild on a better platform. Check how easily you can export content from your builder first.

**Q: Do I need a website if I have a Google Business Profile?**
A: BrightLocal's 2026 survey found that 54% of consumers visit a website after reading positive reviews, so a website still supports bookings.
