Zenoti's 2025 beauty and wellness benchmark report says top-earning salons rebook 30% of clients within 24 hours, against an industry average of 10%. That is a three-to-one gap, and it comes from a front desk habit, not a marketing budget. The report is vendor data from US and Canadian businesses on Zenoti's platform for 2024, with no sample size stated, so treat the numbers as a direction. But the direction is clear: the salons that ask at the right moment, in the right way, fill more of their diary before the client leaves the building.

This guide is for salon owners and stylists in the US, UK, Australia and Canada. It is the in-person half of retention: the script at the chair, the hand-off to the front desk, objection answers and a monthly check. Zenoti's report says guests cancel 8% of salon appointments and no-show on 3%. Two cautions. First, "rebooked within 24 hours" is a platform measure, not the same as "left the chair with an appointment". Second, you will see higher rebooking figures quoted elsewhere (60 to 90% at checkout). We found those in vendor and coaching blogs with different definitions and no clear method, so we do not use them. Do not chase a headline percentage. Measure your own.

A simple measure: of the clients you serve in a week, how many left with their next appointment booked? Your booking software (Square Appointments, Vagaro, Fresha, Booksy, GlossGenius and others) can often report this, or you can tally it on paper for a month.

## Step 1: Recommend, Do Not Ask

You are the expert. A question ("Would you like to book again?") invites "I will check my diary". A recommendation gives a plan. The pattern is always the same: say how long the result will look good, name a target date, and offer two options.

**At the chair, near the end of the service:**

"Your cut will look its best for about six weeks. I would like to see you the week of the 14th to keep the shape. Does a morning or an afternoon suit you better?"

**For colour:**

"To keep your roots fresh, I recommend a touch-up in about five weeks. I have Thursday at 10 or Saturday at 2. Which works?"

**For a first-time client:**

"It was lovely to meet you. I would like to see you again in about six weeks so we can keep building on this. Shall I put you down for the 23rd?"

Avoid "when would you like to come back?" because it makes the client do the planning. The weeks in these scripts are examples. Use the interval you would genuinely recommend for that client's hair.

## Step 2: Set Your Own Return Windows

Return times depend on the service, the hair and the client's goals. These starting points are my rules of thumb, not published standards, so adapt them to your salon and write them on a card by the till:

| Service | Starting window (assumption) |
| --- | --- |
| Short cuts and fades | 3 to 5 weeks |
| Longer cuts and trims | 6 to 10 weeks |
| Root colour touch-up | 4 to 6 weeks |
| Highlights or balayage | 8 to 12 weeks, toner visits in between |
| Blowouts and styling | As needed, or for events |
| Treatments | Per product guidance |

Then check them against your own data. Pull the last 100 repeat clients from your booking system and note the actual gap between visits for each service. Your real median gap beats any table. If clients who book at five weeks keep coming back and those who book at nine weeks drift away, move your recommendation.

## Step 3: Hand Off Smoothly at the Front Desk

The stylist recommends and the front desk completes it. Both should say the same thing.

**Stylist to desk:** "Jordan is due in five weeks for colour. Could you look for Thursday morning?"

**Desk to client:** "I have the 18th at 10 or the 20th at 2. Which would you prefer?"

Then confirm it out loud and by text: "You are booked for Thursday 18th at 10 with Sam. You will get a reminder two days before."

If the client is unsure, offer a hold, not a no: "I can pencil you in and text you. You can move it up to 24 hours before without a fee." Check that your cancellation policy matches what you promise, and that it is clear and fair. Book before payment is taken, while the client is still enjoying the result.

## Step 4: Handle the Common Objections

Short, calm answers keep staff from freezing. Do not push; a friendly second offer is enough.

- **"I do not know my schedule yet."** "No problem. I will book you in so you have a slot, and you can move it free until 24 hours before."
- **"I will call you."** "Of course. Our week fills up fast, so I will pencil in a spot and text you tomorrow to confirm."
- **"It is too expensive."** Listen first. Perhaps offer a lower-cost maintenance visit between big appointments, such as a gloss or a trim.
- **"I am growing it out."** "Lovely. A trim every few months keeps the ends healthy. Shall we pencil one in for the spring?"

## Step 5: Follow Up With Messages

The script handles clients in the chair. A simple message routine handles the rest:

1. **Confirmation text** straight after booking.
2. **Reminder** 24 to 48 hours before, with an easy reschedule link.
3. **Thank-you message** the next day, with a review prompt if the client is happy.
4. **Gentle nudge** when someone passes their usual window with no booking.

On reviews: Google's help page says you can ask customers to visit a Google link or scan a QR code, and that offering incentives, such as free or discounted goods or services, in exchange for reviews is strictly prohibited. So ask, but do not trade a discount for a five-star review. Our [Google Business Profile reviews guide](/blog/google-business-profile-reviews) has wording.

Texting rules differ by country. In the UK, the ICO says marketing emails and texts to individuals need specific consent, with a limited soft opt-in for existing customers (see our [UK salon email and SMS guide](/blog/email-sms-marketing-uk-salons)). Keep appointment reminders about the appointment, not about offers. Check US, Australian and Canadian rules with your booking vendor or an adviser before you send promotional texts.

<Callout type="tip">Put the recommended return date in the client's notes after every visit. Next time, anyone on the team can say: "Last time we planned five weeks. How is your colour feeling?"</Callout>

## Step 6: Review the Numbers Monthly

Once a month, give yourself ten minutes with three numbers:

- Rebooking rate per stylist, as a coaching tool and not a league table.
- No-show and late-cancel rates. Zenoti's 8% and 3% are a loose yardstick, not a target.
- How many lapsed clients returned after a nudge.

If one stylist has a lower rate, shadow a few checkouts. The gap is usually a missing recommendation, not a missing skill.

## The Math

Example only. Every number below is an **assumption** for illustration, except that the Zenoti rebooking figures are the benchmark for what is possible. Replace them with your own.

Assumptions: a salon serves 200 clients a month, average ticket $85, and a client who leaves with a booked appointment has a 90% chance of showing, while one who leaves unbooked and rebooks later has a 60% chance of ever coming back within the window.

| | Today | Modest gain | Strong gain |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Share leaving with a booked appointment (assumption) | 30% | 45% | 60% |
| Booked clients | 60 | 90 | 120 |
| Expected return visits from booked (x 90%) | 54 | 81 | 108 |
| Unbooked clients | 140 | 110 | 80 |
| Expected return visits from unbooked (x 60%) | 84 | 66 | 48 |
| Total return visits | 138 | 147 | 156 |
| Extra visits vs today | 0 | 9 | 18 |
| Extra revenue at $85 | $0 | $765 | $1,530 |

How to read it: moving from 30% to 45% of clients leaving with an appointment adds about 9 return visits a month under these assumptions, or around $765 in revenue. The real benefit is bigger than this table, because a booked chair also fills your diary weeks ahead and makes your income more predictable. It uses revenue, not profit, and the 90% and 60% return rates are placeholders. Measure your own show and return rates for booked versus unbooked clients over three months, then replace them.

## Rebooking Checklist for Your Team

Print this and keep it by the front desk:

- The stylist states how long the result will last.
- The stylist gives a target date, not a vague "soon".
- The desk offers two specific slots.
- The appointment is booked before payment is taken.
- A confirmation text goes out immediately.
- The next-visit plan is saved in the client notes.
- Clients who leave without booking get a friendly text within two days.

## Do This Today

1. Count last week's clients and how many left with their next appointment booked. That is your baseline.
2. Write the two-option script for your three most common services on a card for every station.
3. Agree one hand-off phrase between stylists and the front desk, and practise it at tomorrow's start-of-day huddle.
4. Add the recommended return date to the client notes after every visit.
5. Turn on confirmation and reminder texts in your booking software, with a reschedule link and your cancellation policy.

## FAQ

**Q: What is a good salon rebooking rate?**
A: There is no single verified figure. Zenoti's 2025 report says top salons rebook 30% of clients within 24 hours and the average is 10%, but other sources use other definitions. Measure your own and improve it month by month.

**Q: Should clients pay a deposit to rebook?**
A: Some salons use deposits for long colour appointments or peak times. Keep policies clear, fair and visible at booking. Many prefer a free-change window.

**Q: What if a client wants to wait and see?**
A: Offer to hold a slot with free changes until a set time, and send a gentle follow-up text. That keeps the door open without pressure.

**Q: Do I need special software?**
A: Not to start. A paper tally and a clear script work. Software makes reminders and reporting easier as you grow. Our [booking app comparison](/blog/booksy-vs-vagaro-vs-fresha-best-booking-app-salons-2026) covers the main options.

**Q: Does this work for a barbershop or nail salon?**
A: The pattern (recommend, name a date, offer two slots) works anywhere clients return on a cycle. Adjust the intervals to your service.
