A good FAQ page answers the questions customers ask just before they buy, in plain language, grouped by topic and written so both people and Google can read it. Collect real questions from your calls, messages and reviews, answer each in two to five sentences, link to the next step, and keep the page up to date.

An FAQ page does two jobs. For customers, it removes doubt: price, safety, timing, what to expect. For search, it targets the long, specific questions people type into Google or ask voice assistants. Here is how to build one that does both.

## Step 1: Gather real questions

Do not guess. Mine the places where customers already tell you what worries them:

- Phone calls and voicemails (keep a tally for two weeks)
- Direct messages on Instagram and Facebook
- Email enquiries and contact form messages
- Questions asked at the counter, at the front desk or on first visits
- Google reviews, particularly the ones that mention surprises
- The "People also ask" box when you search your main service plus your town
- Your Google Business Profile Q&A section
- Autocomplete suggestions in Google
- Competitors' FAQ pages, for gaps and ideas, not to copy

Aim for 15 to 30 candidates, then narrow to the ones that come up most often or block purchases.

## Step 2: Sort by objection

Objections are the reasons someone hesitates. They fall into predictable groups:

| Objection | Typical question | Example answer focus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Price | "How much does it cost?" | Give a range and what affects it |
| Trust | "Are you insured, licensed or qualified?" | State credentials plainly |
| Time | "How long does it take?" | Realistic timing |
| Risk | "What if I'm not happy?" | Your policy, guarantee or process |
| Fit | "Do you work with [my situation]?" | Who you serve and who you do not |
| Logistics | "Where are you and where do I park?" | Address, hours, access |
| Booking | "How do I book or cancel?" | Steps, deadlines, fees |

Answering the uncomfortable questions, especially price, tends to build more trust than avoiding them.

## Step 3: Write answers that work

Principles for each answer:

1. **Lead with the answer.** The first sentence should respond directly. Explanations follow.
2. **Be specific.** "Most full grooms take two to three hours depending on coat and size" beats "it depends".
3. **Use your customers' words.** Take phrasing from emails and reviews, not from industry jargon.
4. **Keep it short.** Two to five sentences is usually enough. Link to a longer page for complex topics.
5. **Offer a next step.** End key answers with a pointer, such as "See our price list" or "Book online".
6. **Be honest about limits.** If you do not do something, say so and who might.

Avoid marketing speak. People read FAQs to check facts, not to be sold to.

## Step 4: Organise the page

Structure matters, especially once you have more than a handful of questions.

- Use H2 headings for categories, such as Booking, Pricing, Your First Visit, Policies.
- Use each question as an H3 or as an accordion item.
- Put the most important questions at the top.
- Add a short intro and a link to contact you if the answer is not here.
- Add a table of contents with jump links for longer pages.

Accordions (expand and collapse) keep long pages tidy, but make sure the answer text is in the page HTML and accessible with the keyboard, so it can be read by search engines and screen readers. See our [website accessibility guide](/blog/website-accessibility-small-business) for tips.

## Step 5: Add FAQ schema carefully

FAQ structured data (FAQPage schema) tells search engines which text on the page is a question and answer. Google has significantly limited FAQ rich results in recent years, showing them only for a narrow set of well-known government and health sites, so do not expect expanded results on your own site. Schema can still help machines understand the page, and is worth adding if it is easy, but is not the reason to write an FAQ.

If you add it:

- Mark up only questions and answers visible on the page.
- Use JSON-LD, the format Google recommends.
- Validate it with Google's Rich Results Test or the Schema Markup Validator.
- Do not use FAQ schema for advertising or for content that is not an actual FAQ.

Most website platforms (WordPress with Yoast or Rank Math, Squarespace and Shopify through apps or code) have a way to add it.

## Step 6: Place FAQs where the objections arise

One FAQ page is a good start, but customers rarely visit it first. Also place short Q&A sections on:

- Service pages, with questions about that service
- Pricing and booking pages
- Landing pages for ads, near the call to action
- Location or "contact" pages

This is where an FAQ does its best conversion work. Our guide to [landing page best practices](/blog/landing-page-best-practices) explains how to place objection handling near buttons.

## Step 7: Optimise for search

To help the page rank:

- Make the page title and H1 clear, such as "Dog Grooming FAQ | [Business Name], [Town]".
- Include your location and service naturally where it fits.
- Write each question the way people phrase it, such as "How much does a haircut cost in [town]?"
- Link from the FAQ to your service pages and from those pages back to it.
- Make sure it loads quickly and is easy to use on a phone.
- Keep your Google Business Profile consistent with the answers on your site, since it has its own Q&A and details.

## Step 8: Keep it up to date

An out-of-date FAQ is worse than none. Schedule a review every quarter:

- Check prices, hours, policies and links
- Add new questions from recent enquiries
- Remove answers that no longer apply
- Check which FAQ pages and sections get traffic in Google Search Console and Analytics

Track whether enquiries about the same topics fall after you answer them, which is a good sign the page is working.

## Starter questions to adapt

Adjust these to your business:

- How much does [service] cost, and what is included?
- How do I book, change or cancel an appointment?
- What happens at my first visit?
- Do you offer a guarantee or refund?
- Are you insured, licensed or certified?
- Which areas do you serve?
- What payment methods do you accept?
- Is there parking or step-free access?
- Do you offer gift cards or memberships?
- How long does [service] take?

## Common mistakes

- Using FAQs as a place to stuff keywords
- Questions nobody actually asks
- Long, vague answers
- Hiding the price question
- Duplicating the same text across many pages
- Forgetting to update after a change in policy

## FAQ

**Q: How many questions should a FAQ page have?**
A: Quality beats quantity. Many local businesses do well with 10 to 25 real questions, grouped by topic. Add more as new questions come up.

**Q: Does an FAQ page help SEO?**
A: It can, by targeting the specific questions people search and by keeping visitors on your site longer. It is one part of a broader local SEO approach, not a shortcut.

**Q: Should every FAQ answer have a link?**
A: Not every one, but link where a next step is natural, such as a price list, booking page or policy.

**Q: Do I need FAQ schema?**
A: No. It is optional, and Google rarely shows FAQ rich results for small business sites now. Add it if it is simple on your platform, and make sure it matches the visible text.
