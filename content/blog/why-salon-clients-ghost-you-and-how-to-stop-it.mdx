Here is the number that should worry you. In Boulevard's analysis of platform data from more than 4 million clients, reported by Salon Today, 45% of first-time clients came back for a second appointment at an average salon, and 70% did at the top-decile salons. Same industry, same hair, a 25-point gap. Most of that gap is not talent. It is whether the next visit was easy to book and whether anyone followed up.

This guide is for salon, barbershop and spa owners in the US, UK, Australia and Canada who keep losing clients quietly. It separates ghosting into types, shows what the surveys say clients leave over, and gives you a system you can run from your booking software. No-shows (clients who book then vanish on the day) are a related but different problem; our guides on [reducing no-shows](/blog/reduce-no-shows-appointment-business) and [salon automation](/blog/ai-automation-for-hair-salons) cover reminders in depth, so this page focuses on the quieter loss: clients who just stop coming back.

## What the Data Says About Drift

Three sources are worth knowing. All come from software vendors or trade press, so they describe tendencies, not guarantees.

**Boulevard's retention report** (reported by Salon Today, October 2023) analysed data from January 2022 to March 2023 covering more than 11 million appointments, 4 million unique clients and over 30,000 businesses. Beyond the figures above, it reports that 70% of clients with a second appointment book a third (81% at best-in-class salons). Online first bookers returning more often than walk-ins is a correlation: people who book online may differ from walk-ins in other ways. But it fits what clients say next.

**Square's client loyalty survey** (June 2024, US and Canada, as covered by Small Business Trends; Square's own release would not load for this review) found about one in three consumers are in an "open relationship" with their stylist or barber, meaning open to switching. Among those, the reasons given were:

- Difficulty booking appointments: 57%
- Services getting too expensive: 53%
- Either side moving: 50%
- Stylist not listening to requests: 41%
- Personalities not meshing: 34%

Of clients who tried someone new, 61% regretted it and 57% went back to their original provider. Asked what would bring switchers back, 82% said easy rescheduling and 75% said better communication before or between appointments. The article does not report sample size or fieldwork dates.

**Phorest's loyalty survey** (reported by Salon Today, September 2022) lists why clients stay with a hair salon: service quality 65%, price 63%, relationship with the provider 58%, convenience 51%, availability 40%, hygiene 28%, decor 13%. It also says 84% of Americans would likely follow a favourite stylist to a new location, though the article headline says 86%. Sample size was not stated.

What to take from all three: drift is mostly logistics and listening, not decor. Booking friction, price surprises and feeling unheard beat everything else. Those are fixable.

## Diagnose Before You Fix

Open your booking software and run this 30-minute audit. You need no special tool, only a client list with last-visit dates.

1. **Export clients with a last visit 90 days or more ago.** Adjust the cut-off if your services run on longer cycles.
2. **Sort them into four buckets.**
   - *One-and-done:* visited once, never returned.
   - *Stylist-linked:* their usual stylist left, moved or cut hours.
   - *Price-linked:* last visit followed a price rise or a bill bigger than the quote.
   - *Unknown drift:* regulars who simply stopped.
3. **Count each bucket.** The biggest bucket is where to start.
4. **Check your rebooking rate:** what share of clients leave with their next appointment already booked? Most systems report this. If yours does not, count a week of checkouts by hand.

If one-and-done is your biggest bucket, your first-visit experience and follow-up need work. If stylist-linked is biggest, you have a handover problem, not a marketing problem.

## Fix 1: Make the Next Visit Easy to Book

Given that booking trouble tops Square's list and easy rescheduling tops the "what would bring me back" list, the highest-value fix is friction. Check these:

- **Book the next visit at checkout**, with a specific recommendation: "Your colour looks best refreshed in six to eight weeks; shall I pencil in a Thursday evening?" The wording and objection handling are in our [salon rebooking script](/blog/salon-rebooking-script-that-gets-clients-back). The six to eight weeks is an example; use the interval you would give a real client.
- **Put a one-tap reschedule link in every confirmation and reminder.**
- **Turn on online booking** if you have not. Boulevard's online-vs-walk-in gap is a pattern, not proof, but there is no downside to letting people book at 11pm.
- **Show real availability.** If your online calendar shows nothing for three weeks, clients will assume you are full and drift.

## Fix 2: Make Clients Feel Heard

Forty-one percent of Square's switchers cited a stylist not listening. Two cheap habits help:

- **Write a consultation note** after every first visit: what they asked for, what you did, what to change next time. Whoever sees them next reads it first.
- **Send a check-in 24 to 48 hours later:** "How are you getting on with the cut? If anything isn't right, reply and we'll fix it." Replies must reach a human. A client who raises a small issue privately is a client you can keep. One who says nothing quietly books elsewhere.

## Fix 3: Take the Sting Out of Price

Square's 53% for expensive services is a warning about surprises as much as price levels. Publish a price list with the "from" price for add-ons (toner, long hair, treatments), quote before starting, and announce price rises by message two to four weeks ahead. Do not respond to price drift with permanent discounts; they teach clients to wait.

## Fix 4: Handle Stylist Changes Properly

If a stylist leaves, contact their clients yourself, introduce the replacement, share the consultation notes and offer a short chat before the first appointment. This is the most overlooked drift source, given that Phorest's survey reports the relationship as a top reason clients stay.

## Fix 5: A Win-Back Sequence That Respects the Client

For clients past their usual interval, send a short sequence rather than a blast. The timings below are my suggestions, not benchmarks. Set them by your own average gap between visits.

| When | Message |
| --- | --- |
| Usual interval plus 1 to 2 weeks | Friendly nudge with a booking link |
| Plus 6 weeks | Something useful: a care tip or a new service |
| Plus 3 months | A small, genuine reason to return, such as a complimentary add-on |
| Plus 6 months | Personal: "We miss you. Did something go wrong?" with a reply option |

Stop the sequence when they book or opt out. Our [customer reactivation campaigns guide](/blog/customer-reactivation-campaigns) goes deeper on message wording. Check the consent rules where you operate before texting or emailing promotional messages, and keep appointment reminders free of offers.

Personal beats polished. A two-line message from the stylist the client actually knows will outperform a designed newsletter for most small salons. That is a rule of thumb from experience, not a measured result, so test it on 20 lapsed clients and see.

## The Math

How much is the gap between 45% and 70% worth? Assume a salon that gets 40 new clients a month. Everything except the 45% and 70% (Boulevard figures) is an **assumption** for illustration.

| | Tough | Base | Strong |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| New clients a month (assumption) | 40 | 40 | 40 |
| Share who return (45% and 70% from Boulevard; 55% assumed) | 45% | 55% | 70% |
| Returning clients | 18 | 22 | 28 |
| Visits per returner in the next 12 months, including the second (assumption) | 4 | 4 | 4 |
| Visits | 72 | 88 | 112 |
| Average ticket (assumption) | $85 | $85 | $85 |
| 12-month revenue from one month's new clients | $6,120 | $7,480 | $9,520 |

Moving from 45% to 70% on 40 new clients a month adds about $3,400 of revenue per monthly cohort over a year. Over twelve cohorts, that compounds quickly. Two cautions: this is revenue, not profit, and the top-decile result may not be reachable for every salon. Even closing a third of the gap, to roughly 53%, is worth thinking about. Use your own average ticket and visit frequency before you plan around the result.

The reverse view helps too. If your rebooking at checkout is 30% today and you lift it to 50%, you have 20% more future appointments booked before the client reaches the door.

## Do This Today

1. Export every client whose last visit was 90 or more days ago and sort them into the four buckets above.
2. Work out your current rebooking rate for the last 30 days, using your software or a hand count.
3. Add a one-tap reschedule link to your confirmation and reminder messages, and check how many weeks ahead your online calendar shows availability.
4. Send a personal "we miss you" message from the stylist to your 20 most valuable lapsed clients.
5. Write a one-paragraph handover plan for what happens if a stylist leaves: who contacts clients, within what time, with what notes.

## FAQ

**Q: Why do salon clients stop coming back?**
A: Square's survey of people open to switching points to booking difficulty (57%), price (53%), moving (50%), not feeling listened to (41%) and personality mismatch (34%). Your own lapsed-client buckets will tell you which applies to you.

**Q: What is a good rebooking rate?**
A: Boulevard's data shows 45% of first-timers return at the average salon and 70% at top-decile salons. That is a first-to-second visit figure, not checkout rebooking. Measure your own checkout rebooking rate for 30 days to set a baseline.

**Q: When should I contact a client who has not rebooked?**
A: One to two weeks after their usual interval, then at wider intervals. Stop if they book, opt out or tell you they have moved on.

**Q: Should I offer a discount to win clients back?**
A: Start with service and access: an easier booking link, a personal message. Use a small incentive later in the sequence, and keep it modest so regulars do not feel penalised.

**Q: Do I need special software?**
A: Not necessarily. Most booking apps cover reminders, rebooking and basic messaging. Add an email or SMS tool when you outgrow them.
