Here are two numbers to hold side by side. A Facebook page with 2,000 followers reaches about 87 of them with a typical post, going by Socialinsider's 2026 data for small pages. And $300 of Meta lead ads buys roughly 11 leads at LocaliQ's all-industry average cost. Neither number is "better". They answer different questions, and mixing them up is how local businesses end up either posting into a void or paying for clicks that land on a dead profile.

This guide is for salons, cafés, studios, groomers and similar local businesses in the US, UK, Australia and Canada. It does not repeat the generic "do both" advice. Instead it gives you a way to test the split with your own numbers over 30 days. Dollar figures are US-based benchmarks; for pounds or Australian and Canadian dollars, run the same math with your own prices.

## What the Data Says About Organic Reach

Socialinsider's [2026 reach study](https://www.socialinsider.io/blog/social-media-reach/) analysed 872,075 posts from brand pages between January 2025 and August 2026 (published September 3, 2026). Its reach rate by account size is the figure that matters for a small local page:

Three takeaways:

- **Small pages do relatively well.** Facebook pages with 1 to 5K followers reached 4.35% of followers per post; pages of 100K to 1M reached 0.50%. Instagram showed the same pattern, from 6.65% down to 3.50%.
- **Format matters.** On Instagram the study lists carousels at 4.50%, Reels at 4.10% and images at 4.00%. On Facebook, images were 1.20%, albums 1.15%, status posts 0.80%, Reels 0.55% and links 0.20%. So on Facebook, a link post to your booking page does worst; put the link in the comments or use a photo.
- **Treat the headline averages with care.** The same page quotes 1.25% for Facebook in its summary and 1.80% in the body, and two different Instagram figures. The size table is the most usable part. These are brand-page averages, not your page.

Notice what "reach rate" means: the share of your followers who saw the post. It says nothing about new people in your town who do not follow you yet. That is the real limit of organic for a local business with a small following, and it is where paid comes in.

## What Paid Costs, Honestly

LocaliQ's [Facebook advertising benchmarks](https://localiq.com/blog/facebook-advertising-benchmarks/) (updated September 23, 2026) give all-industry averages of $0.60 per click for traffic campaigns and $1.80 per click, 2.70% click-through and $27.39 per lead for lead campaigns. ## Where Your Customers Actually Are

Pew Research Center's [social media fact sheet](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/fact-sheet/social-media/) (survey of 5,022 US adults, February to June 2025) found 71% of US adults use Facebook, 50% Instagram and 32% TikTok. By age, Facebook use peaks at 80% for ages 30 to 49 and is still 57% for those 65 and over. Instagram is 80% for ages 18 to 29 but 19% for 65 and over.

So pick the platform by who buys from you, not by what looks fashionable. A dental practice or family-oriented café probably reaches more of its buyers on Facebook; a nail studio with a 20-something clientele has a case for Instagram. Pew data covers the US only; check your own country's figures if you serve the UK, Australia or Canada.

## The Real Job of Organic: The Profile Check

Here is the case for keeping organic alive even when its reach is small. BrightLocal's [Local Consumer Review Survey](https://www.brightlocal.com/research/local-consumer-review-survey/) (1,002 US adults, published February 2026) found that 24% of consumers visit a business's social media channels after reading positive reviews. That is one in four people checking your page right before they decide. Your ads, your Google listing and your word of mouth all send people to that profile.

So organic has two jobs:

1. **Be a credible destination.** Recent photos, real staff, real customers, current opening hours and prices.
2. **Produce the raw material for ads.** The posts that earn saves, shares and comments become your ad creative. Our guide to [getting customers to post about you](/blog/user-generated-content-local-business-get-customers-to-post) covers the permission and disclosure side, and the [restaurant Instagram guide](/blog/instagram-marketing-for-restaurants) shows a content rhythm you can copy for any visual business.

## The Math

Here is a one-month comparison. Benchmarks come from the sources above; everything marked **assumption** is a placeholder to replace with your own numbers.

| | Organic | Paid (Meta lead ads) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Followers (assumption) | 2,000 | n/a |
| Reach per post | 87 (2,000 x 4.35%, Socialinsider) | n/a |
| Posts per month (assumption) | 13 (3 a week) | n/a |
| Time per week (assumption) | 4 hours | 1 hour |
| Value of your time (assumption) | $25 an hour | $25 an hour |
| Monthly cash cost | $0 | $300 |
| Monthly time cost | $400 | $100 |
| Total monthly cost | $400 | $400 |
| Leads | unknown, track it | 11 ($300 / $27.39) |
| Lead to customer rate (assumption) | 20% | 20% |
| New customers | to be measured | 2.2 |
| Cost per customer | to be measured | about $182 |

How to read it:

- **Organic is not free.** At 4 hours a week, valuing your time at $25, it costs $400 a month. To match paid's $182 per customer, organic would need to bring in about 2.2 customers a month. If your log shows fewer, you are overspending time, or the posts are not doing the job.
- **The paid line is generous.** It uses the all-industry lead cost. In Beauty & Personal Care the lead cost is $50.91, which gives about 5.9 leads, 1.2 customers and roughly $340 per customer on the same assumptions.
- **Your own close rate decides this.** Going from 20% to 30% lead-to-customer cuts paid cost per customer by a third without touching the ad account. That is a reply-speed problem.

Revenue is not profit. Subtract delivery costs before you decide what a customer is worth. Our [Facebook ads budget guide](/blog/facebook-ads-budget-local-business-how-much-to-spend) shows how to set the ceiling.

## A 30-Day Split Test

Rather than argue about ratios, run a simple test.

**Week 1: fix the destination.** Update profile photo, bio, hours, link and highlights. Add five to ten photos of real work. Link to a single booking page. Add a tracking tag to that link: Google's [UTM guide for Analytics](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/10917952) says to always use utm_source, utm_medium and utm_campaign, and that values are case sensitive, so stick to lowercase (for example `utm_source=facebook`, `utm_medium=organic` or `paid`).

**Week 2: post to learn.** Publish three to four posts, mixing a photo post, a carousel or short video, and a customer story with permission. On Facebook, avoid bare link posts, given the 0.20% figure above. Note which posts get saves, shares and messages.

**Week 3: spend small and specific.** Pick one offer with a clear next step (a first appointment, a trial class, a lunch deal). Build one ad from your best organic post, or a simple lead form. Set a daily budget you can afford to lose and a tight radius around the places your customers actually travel from.

**Week 4: count customers.** Tally customers by source: UTM clicks, codes, "how did you find us" answers and messages. Calculate cost per customer for each channel using the table above. Keep what worked, drop the rest.

## Which Way to Lean

- **Few followers, little budget:** Lean organic for a month to build the destination and your first reviews, but still run the 30-day test with a small paid spend, because a small page can only reach people who already follow it.
- **Quiet days or empty slots:** Lean paid. You need people who do not follow you, on a schedule.
- **Launch, event or seasonal offer:** Paid, with organic posts to build proof beforehand.
- **Regulars you want back:** Organic plus email or SMS. See email marketing for small business.
- **Short video:** TikTok and Reels can reach strangers organically, but local conversion is hard to measure. Our [organic vs paid TikTok comparison](/blog/tiktok-organic-vs-paid-strategy) covers that platform separately.

## Mistakes That Waste the Test

- Boosting every post instead of one with a goal.
- Sending ad clicks to a profile with nothing posted in three months.
- A radius much bigger than your customers will travel.
- Changing the ad every couple of days before there is enough data to judge.
- Counting likes as success.
- Leaving messages and comments unanswered. They are often the real leads.

## Do This Today

1. Open your page insights and write down your follower count and average reach per post for the last 10 posts. Compare it with the 4.35% Facebook and 6.65% Instagram figures for small pages.
2. Add a "how did you find us?" question to your booking form, till or intake sheet.
3. Make one booking link with UTM tags for organic and a second for paid, both in lowercase.
4. Pick one offer and set a test budget you can afford to lose for 30 days. Use your industry's cost per lead from the table to estimate how many leads that buys.
5. Block 20 minutes each week to tally customers by source and calculate cost per customer for both channels.

## FAQ

**Q: Is organic social media dead for local businesses?**
A: No, but it reaches mainly existing followers. Socialinsider's 2026 data shows small Facebook pages reaching about 4.35% of followers per post. The profile also matters: BrightLocal found 24% of consumers visit a business's social channels after reading positive reviews.

**Q: How much should a local business spend on social ads?**
A: There is no official number. Work backwards: LocaliQ's all-industry Meta cost per lead is $27.39, so $300 buys about 11 leads. Multiply by your own lead-to-customer rate and compare with what a customer is worth to you.

**Q: Should I boost posts or use Ads Manager?**
A: Boosting is quick for a single post; Ads Manager gives more control over objective, audience and placement. Neither makes a weak offer convert. Start with whichever you will actually track, and move to Ads Manager once you are running repeat campaigns. This is general guidance, not a Meta rule.

**Q: How long before I can judge paid social?**
A: Clicks arrive within days, but judge on customers, not clicks, and allow at least a few weeks and enough leads to read a pattern. The 30-day test above is a minimum, not a guarantee.

**Q: Which platform should I pick first?**
A: The one your customers use. Pew's US data shows Facebook use is 80% among ages 30 to 49 while Instagram is strongest under 30. Start with one and do it well.
