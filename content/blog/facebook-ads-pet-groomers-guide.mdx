Facebook ads can fill a grooming calendar when they reach people who live within driving distance and have a clear reason to book. Vendor guidance suggests starting around $10 to $20 a day and targeting a radius of roughly 10 to 20 miles. Before-and-after photos and short transformation videos are the formats most often recommended. Past clients are usually the cheapest audience to reach first, and cold local prospects come next. ## How much should a pet groomer spend on Facebook ads?

A practical starting point is the daily budget a vendor guide gives for new campaigns: [$10 to $20 per day](https://barklead.com/post/using-facebook-ads-for-groomers) (secondary). Start at the low end, watch which ads bring in bookings, and then move money toward the ones that work. Judge the test on booked appointments, not on reach or profile visits, since neither pays for a grooming chair.

Run the test long enough to see a pattern. A two to four week window is a reasonable minimum for a salon with a steady flow of bookings, because a few weeks of data can swing on one busy weekend. Keep the budget steady during the test so the results can be compared fairly.

## Which audience should a groomer target first?

Start with people who have already been clients. An export from the booking system of clients whose last groom is overdue gives the ad a warm audience, and the message only needs to offer a reason to rebook. Check the data rules that apply in your country before uploading any customer list, and include only customers you have a lawful basis to market to.

For cold targeting, [one vendor guide](https://www.gethookd.ai/learn/facebook-ads-for-pet-grooming-examples-strategy/) (secondary) recommends a 10 to 20 mile radius around the salon, plus pet-owner and breed interests. Another guide suggests a [10 to 15 mile radius](https://barklead.com/post/using-facebook-ads-for-groomers) (secondary). Mobile groomers should build the radius from a map of recent bookings rather than a circle around the home base, because routes cluster by area.

| Audience | Where the list comes from | What to measure | Role in the plan |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lapsed clients | Booking system, last groom overdue | Rebooked grooms by ad | Test first, cheapest to reach |
| Cold local prospects | Radius and pet-owner interests | Booking requests and first grooms | Test second, inside the travel radius |
| Website visitors | Pixel on the booking or price page | Completed bookings | Add once the pixel is live |

## What creative works for grooming ads?

Before-and-after photos are the most common recommendation, and the format is simple: the same dog, shot from the same angle, with the transformation shown side by side. Keep the photos well lit and use only images of clients who have given permission.

Short video is the second format. One vendor guide suggests a [15 to 30 second time-lapse](https://www.gethookd.ai/learn/facebook-ads-for-pet-grooming-examples-strategy/) (secondary) that starts with the messy before state. Check the aspect ratio and length rules for each placement before exporting, and keep the price or the suburb in the caption. The [CTV creative specs guide](/blog/ctv-advertising-creative-specs-guide-2026) covers video specification habits that also apply to feed video.

## What is the market for pet groomers?

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reports a [median annual wage of $35,530](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/personal-care-and-service/animal-care-and-service-workers.htm) (primary) for animal care and service workers in 2025, and projects 11 percent employment growth from 2025 to 2035. These figures cover the whole category, including pet sitters, trainers and kennel staff, so they describe the labor market rather than groomer revenue.

The projected 69,400 openings a year mostly reflect workers who leave the field. For a salon, that means hiring and retention are a real cost. A campaign that brings in bookings must not overload the team, so plan ad volume against the number of groomers you can schedule each week.

## Worked budget: a lapsed-client rebooking campaign

The inputs below are assumptions, not sourced figures. Replace them with your own booking data after one cycle.

Assumption inputs: 400 lapsed clients uploaded to the audience, 3 percent rebook after the campaign and follow-up sequence, average groom value of $85, and ad plus reminder cost of $150 for the month.

Step one: 400 clients multiplied by 3 percent gives 12 rebooked grooms. Step two: 12 grooms multiplied by $85 gives $1,020 in gross revenue. Step three: $1,020 divided by $150 gives about 6.8 in revenue for each dollar spent. This ignores groomer time, product costs and add-ons, so treat it as a ceiling for planning.

A checklist keeps the test honest:

1. Export the lapsed list from the booking system and remove anyone without a lawful marketing basis.
2. Set one radius per location, based on a map of recent bookings.
3. Run two ad versions, one before-and-after photo and one short video, against the same audience.
4. Record rebooked appointments by ad, not just clicks.
5. After two to four weeks, keep the stronger version and shift a fixed share of the budget to it.

## FAQ

### How much should a pet groomer spend on Facebook ads?

Vendor guidance suggests $10 to $20 a day for a new campaign, with spend raised only after the ads show bookings. Use the booking system to count rebooked appointments before increasing the budget.

### Do Facebook ads work for mobile groomers?

They can, with tighter geography. Build targeting around the areas and days of the route rather than a single radius around a home base. Creative that names the suburbs served that week gives a clearer reason to book.

### Should groomers discount first appointments?

An add-on for lapsed clients often works better than a percentage discount, because it avoids training regulars to wait for sales. If discounts are used, keep them small and limited to gaps in the schedule.

### How often should grooming ads run?

A small always-on campaign aimed at recent lapsed clients, with new photos each month, is easier to manage than a large push that runs only in peak season. Check results at least every few weeks.
