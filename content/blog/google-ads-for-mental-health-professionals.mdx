Yes, therapists, counselors and other mental health professionals can use Google Ads ethically and effectively, as long as ads describe services honestly, avoid promising outcomes, respect Google's healthcare and sensitive-category policies, and handle tracking and client privacy carefully. A focused Search campaign for terms like "anxiety therapy in [city]" can put your practice in front of people who are looking for help right now. This guide covers keywords, ad copy, landing pages, privacy, budget and measurement. It is general marketing guidance, not legal advice, so check your licensing board's advertising rules and consult a professional if you handle protected health information.

## 1. Choose Keywords That Match What You Treat

Aim for specific, local, service-based searches:

- "anxiety therapy [city]"
- "couples counseling [city]"
- "trauma therapist near me"
- "online therapy for depression [state]"
- "child therapist [neighborhood]" (only if you see children)

Use location targeting around your office and any states where you are licensed to see clients, especially for telehealth. Use negative keywords to filter out people looking for something else, for example "free", "jobs", "salary", "quiz", "test", "worksheet", "pdf", "book" and "support group" if you do not run one. Review the search terms report regularly, as mental health searches can include people in crisis who need immediate help rather than a therapy appointment.

<Callout type="warning">If someone may be in crisis, an ad for therapy services is not the right resource. Consider showing a crisis resource on your landing page, such as 988 in the US, and avoid bidding on crisis-related terms.</Callout>

## 2. Write Ad Copy That Is Honest and Respectful

- Describe what you treat and where: "Anxiety Therapy in Denver" or "Licensed Counselor for Couples".
- State credentials accurately, such as your license type and state.
- Mention practical details people need: telehealth availability, evening hours, insurance accepted, or self-pay options.
- Do not promise cures or timeframes. Avoid phrases such as "Cure your anxiety" or "Stop panic attacks in two weeks".
- Do not use fear or shame as a hook.
- Avoid implying you know a viewer's personal health condition, since Google's policies restrict that kind of personalization.

Check your professional association's advertising guidelines and your state licensing board's rules on testimonials, specialty claims and fees. Many boards limit or prohibit client testimonials, so do not assume reviews can be quoted in ads.

## 3. Build a Landing Page That Builds Trust

Send ads to a page on your own website rather than a directory profile, so you can control the message and measure results.

- Make it fast and easy to use on a phone.
- Open with who you help and how, in plain language.
- Show your credentials, photo and a short description of your approach.
- Display fees, insurance information and availability if you can.
- Offer a simple way to contact you: a call button or short form that asks for minimal information.
- Include your privacy policy and avoid collecting sensitive details in the first form.

## 4. Know Google's Policies on Healthcare and Sensitive Categories

Google applies stricter rules to health-related advertising. In general, expect:

- Restrictions on personalized advertising for sensitive health topics, which can limit remarketing and some audience targeting.
- Policies on healthcare and medicines that may require certification for certain services, especially in addiction treatment advertising.
- Ad disapprovals when copy seems to make unrealistic claims or targets people based on personal health.

These policies change and vary by region, so read Google's current advertising policies for healthcare before launching, and appeal rather than rewording blindly when an ad is disapproved. Do not rely on any single feature to guarantee approval.

## 5. Handle Tracking and Privacy Carefully

Conversion tracking is useful, but mental health practices need extra care:

- Track outcomes that do not reveal health information, such as a phone call from an ad or a contact form submission, rather than passing detailed form content to analytics tools.
- Avoid placing marketing pixels on pages where clients enter protected health information, such as intake or portal pages.
- If you are a HIPAA covered entity, confirm with a qualified professional how analytics and advertising tools may be used, and whether a business associate agreement is needed.
- Keep your intake and booking system separate from your marketing tracking where possible.

## 6. Budget and Expected Return

There is no single right budget. Cost per click for therapy-related terms varies widely by city and specialty, so check Keyword Planner for your market first. A practical approach:

1. Estimate the lifetime value of a client: average session fee times the typical number of sessions.
2. Estimate the share of calls that become booked clients.
3. Work backward to the most you can pay per call and per new client.

Start with a budget you can sustain for a couple of months, focus on one or two services, and add more only after the first is working. A small, well-targeted campaign usually beats a broad one. See our [Google Ads budget guide](/blog/google-ads-budget-for-small-business) for more detail.

| Step | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| Start | One ad group for your most common service |
| Targeting | Your office area and licensed states only |
| Measure | Calls and form submissions, not clicks |
| Review | Weekly search terms report and monthly cost per new client |
| Scale | Add services only after the first is profitable |

## FAQ

**Q: Is it worth running Google Ads as a solo practitioner on a small budget?**
A: It can be, if the cost to get a new client is lower than that client's lifetime value. Estimate it before you start, use a tight local radius, and check results regularly. Poor setup, not the channel, is the most common cause of disappointment.

**Q: Can I mention conditions like anxiety, depression or PTSD in my ads?**
A: Generally yes, when you describe what you treat in a factual way, such as "Anxiety Therapy in Chicago". Avoid promising results or implying you know a viewer's personal health status. Check Google's current healthcare policies.

**Q: Do I have to accept every lead from an ad?**
A: No. You can screen during the first call and refer people elsewhere if they are not a good fit.

**Q: How do I cut spam or irrelevant clicks?**
A: Add negative keywords, limit location targeting, review the search terms report weekly and avoid very broad match types.

**Q: Should I send traffic to my Psychology Today profile instead of my website?**
A: A page on your own site gives you more control over the message and measurement. Directory profiles can complement ads but are harder to track.

**Q: Do I need a Google Business Profile?**
A: Not to run ads, but it helps you appear in map results and builds trust.
