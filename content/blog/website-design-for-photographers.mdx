A photographer's website has two jobs: show your best work quickly and make it easy to enquire. The hard part is that photos are heavy. Google rates a Largest Contentful Paint of 2.5 seconds or less as good, and its image guidance says images are often the largest contributor to page size. Use WebP or AVIF where you can, set image sizes and keep the first image eager-loaded.

This guide covers portfolio structure, image handling, pricing, builders and a worked example. Builder prices are from the vendor's page on the date checked and may change.

## How should a photographer organise the portfolio?

Show fewer, better galleries by type of work: weddings, portraits, products. Lead with the work you want to be hired for, because visitors judge you from the first screen. NN/g says people spend 57% of viewing time above the fold, and that images should reflect your real offering. Make the first image your strongest.

Add a short caption with the place or type of shoot. Each gallery page should end with a booking button. Our [Instagram guide for photographers](/blog/instagram-marketing-for-photographers) shows how to send traffic to it.

## How do you keep image-heavy pages fast?

Follow Google's image guidance:

- Use descriptive file names and alt text rather than keyword lists.
- Use supported formats: JPEG, PNG, WebP, SVG, AVIF and others.
- Use srcset or picture for different screen sizes, with an img fallback.
- Consider an image sitemap so Google can find images it might miss.

web.dev adds that loading="lazy" suits images below the fold, but not the first-screen image, because it delays Largest Contentful Paint. Dimensions on every image also reduce layout shift. See our [speed guide](/blog/website-speed-optimization-small-business).

## Should a photographer show prices?

Show something. NN/g reports that prospective customers rank price as their top information need, that participants left sites that hid it, and that ranges or sample scenarios work when exact prices are not possible. A "packages start from" line with what is included lets clients self-qualify. See our [pricing page guide](/blog/website-pricing-page-local-business).

Do not copy competitor numbers. Use your own costs and say what changes the total, such as hours, travel and editing.

## How do clients find you?

BrightLocal says 54% of consumers visit a business's website after reading positive reviews, so collect Google reviews and show them. Local searches need a Business Profile, a service area and consistent details. ## Worked example: what does a package page add?

All inputs are **assumptions**.

| Input (assumption) | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Monthly visitors | 700 |
| Enquiry rate without prices | 2% |
| Enquiry rate with a "from" package page | 2.5% |
| Share of enquiries that book | 30% |
| Average booking value | $600 |

Without: 700 x 2% = 14 enquiries; 4.2 bookings; $2,520. With: 700 x 2.5% = 17.5 enquiries; 5.25 bookings; $3,150. The difference is $630 a month. The lift is invented. Track enquiries by source for a few months, and note enquiries that never reply as well as those that book.

## FAQ

### How many photos should a gallery have?

No verified standard exists. Choose the strongest work, and let page speed set the limit.

### Do I need a blog?

Not necessarily. A few location or session-type pages with real photos and captions do more for a local photographer than a long, general blog.

### Should I use watermarks?

No source reviewed here covers this. Decide based on how you license your work.

### Which format is best for images?

Google supports WebP and AVIF, among others. Test file size and quality on your own photos.

### How do I measure enquiries?

Use a contact form with a "how did you find me" question and GA4 key events. See our [website ROI guide](/blog/website-roi-local-business).
