If a wedding package is worth $3,000 and you are happy to spend $450 in ads to book one, then at 5 inquiries per 100 clicks and 15 bookings per 100 inquiries, the most you can pay for a click is about $3.38. Go above that and you lose money on average. Every keyword decision below comes back to that ceiling: a keyword is "good" only if people who type it book at a price your package can carry.

Those three inputs ($450, 5%, 15%) are my assumptions for illustration, not benchmarks. This article gives you the keyword groups, match types, negatives and campaign structure. It deliberately does not quote search volumes or click prices per keyword, because we could not open Google Keyword Planner for this guide and any figure we printed would be invented. You will get your real numbers from your own account in about ten minutes (see "Do This Today").

For the full budget, tracking and landing page picture, read our [Google Ads for photographers guide](/blog/google-ads-for-photographers). This page is the keyword layer on top of it. It is written for photographers in the US, UK, Australia and Canada, so swap "zip code" for "postcode" and dollars for your currency as needed.

## Think in Intent, Not in Lists

A generic "top 10 photography keywords" list fails because "photographer" covers jobs worth $200 and jobs worth $5,000. Group keywords by what the searcher wants and what the job is worth, then give each group its own ad group and landing page.

| Intent | Example keywords (replace [city]) | Match type | Landing page |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Wedding, ready to hire | "wedding photographer [city]", [wedding photographer [city]], "wedding photography packages [city]" | Phrase + exact | Wedding page with pricing guide and date field |
| Wedding, venue-led | "[venue name] wedding photographer", "wedding photographer [region]" | Phrase | Page featuring that venue, if you have shot there |
| Elopement and small weddings | "elopement photographer [location]", "intimate wedding photographer [city]" | Phrase + exact | Elopement page |
| Engagement and couples | "engagement photographer [city]", "engagement photos [city]" | Phrase + exact | Engagement page with sessions and prices |
| Family and newborn | "family photographer [city]", "newborn photographer [city]", "maternity photographer [city]" | Phrase + exact | One page per session type |
| Headshots and business | "headshot photographer [city]", "corporate headshots [city]", "team photos [city]" | Phrase + exact | Business page with packages |
| Commercial and events | "event photographer [city]", "product photographer [city]", "real estate photographer [city]" | Phrase | Service page per type |
| Brand | [your studio name], [your name photography] | Exact | Home or portfolio |

Notes on the table:

- **"Near me" versions** ("wedding photographer near me") are worth testing. Google uses the searcher's location, so they behave like a city term. Test them as a separate ad group so you can judge them by booked clients, not clicks.
- **Only list services you want.** If you do not shoot real estate, leave that row out.
- **Specific beats generic.** A keyword naming the job and the place filters for a buyer; a bare "photographer" filters for nobody.

## Match Types: What Google Actually Says

Google's [keyword match type guide](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/2497836) defines three:

- **Broad match** (no symbol) can show your ad on searches related to the keyword, including ones that do not share its direct meaning. Google says it is critical to use Smart Bidding with broad match.
- **Phrase match** (in quotation marks) can show on searches that include your keyword's meaning. The meaning can be implied, and the search can be a more specific version of it.
- **Exact match** (in square brackets) can show on searches with the same meaning or intent as the keyword. Google says it gives the most control but reaches fewer searches than phrase or broad.

At a studio budget, start with phrase and exact. Use phrase for discovery in each ad group, and exact for the two or three terms you already know book (your main city plus your main service). Leave broad match until you have conversion tracking running and a mature negative list; Google's own advice ties it to Smart Bidding, which needs conversion data to learn from. If you only get a handful of inquiries a month, it will have little to learn from.

Because phrase and exact now match on meaning, not just spelling, you do not need to list every variation ("photographer", "photography", "photographers") separately. Check the search terms report to see what Google actually matched.

## Negative Keywords for Photographers

Photography attracts students, job seekers, hobbyists and people shopping for gear. Google's [negative keywords help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/2453972) explains how they work, and two details matter:

- A broad-match negative blocks a search that contains all the negative terms, in any order. A single-word negative such as `jobs` therefore blocks any search containing "jobs".
- Negatives do not match close variants. Blocking "flowers" does not block "flower", so add singular and plural forms yourself.

Start with this list and add it at campaign level or as a shared list:

| Group | Negatives |
| --- | --- |
| Learning | `course`, `courses`, `class`, `classes`, `school`, `tutorial`, `tutorials`, `how to`, `lessons`, `workshop`, `degree` |
| Jobs | `jobs`, `job`, `career`, `careers`, `salary`, `hiring`, `internship`, `assistant`, `second shooter` |
| Gear and software | `camera`, `cameras`, `lens`, `lenses`, `equipment`, `rental`, `rent`, `software`, `presets`, `preset`, `app`, `drone`, `tripod` |
| Free and stock | `free`, `stock`, `stock photos`, `clipart`, `royalty free`, `template`, `templates` |
| Wrong product | `printing`, `print shop`, `frame`, `frames`, `passport`, `id photo`, `photo booth` |
| DIY | `diy`, `do it yourself`, `at home` |

Check each against your own offer before pasting. If you sell prints, drop `printing`. If you hire second shooters, drop `second shooter`. If you run workshops, drop `workshop`. Negatives should reflect what you do not sell, not a generic list.

Then use the [search terms report](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/2472708). Google describes it as showing the actual searches that triggered your ads and got impressions and clicks, with the matching keyword and match type alongside. Review it weekly, and add the irrelevant searches as negatives. Our guides on negative keywords and [stopping wasted budget](/blog/google-ads-negative-keywords-stop-wasting-budget) show how to apply them.

## Campaign Structure That Works at Studio Size

Keep it small enough that each ad group collects data.

1. **Campaign 1: Weddings and elopements.** The highest-value jobs, with their own budget so a cheap portrait click never starves it. Ad groups: wedding, elopement, venue-led (optional).
2. **Campaign 2: Portraits and sessions.** Engagement, family, newborn, maternity. These jobs are smaller, so the click ceiling is lower (see The Math).
3. **Campaign 3: Business and commercial.** Headshots, events, product, real estate, only if you want that work.
4. **Campaign 4: Brand.** Your studio name in exact match. It is cheap protection so that a competitor cannot sit above you when someone searches your name.

Use one location setup per campaign, matched to where you will actually travel, and add destination areas as separate locations. Google's [daily budget help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/6385083) says daily spend can reach up to twice your average daily budget, with a monthly cap of 30.4 times the daily budget. Divide a monthly figure by 30.4 when you set it.

## The Math

The same keyword can be a bargain for one job and a loss for another. This table shows the ceiling click price for different jobs. Every input is an **assumption** for illustration. Replace them with your own average package and the rates from your inquiry spreadsheet.

| | Wedding | Engagement session | Headshot | Brand search |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Package value (assumption) | $3,000 | $400 | $250 | $3,000 |
| Max ad cost per booking, 15% of package (assumption) | $450 | $60 | $37.50 | $450 |
| Click to inquiry rate (assumption) | 5% | 6% | 6% | 15% |
| Inquiry to booking rate (assumption) | 15% | 25% | 25% | 25% |
| Ceiling click price | $3.38 | $0.90 | $0.56 | $16.88 |

Formula: ceiling CPC = max cost per booking x inquiry rate x booking rate. For weddings: $450 x 0.05 x 0.15 = $3.38.

What this tells you:

- **Small sessions need cheap clicks.** If the click price for "engagement photos [city]" in your market is above $0.90 under these assumptions, the keyword loses money unless the client also buys prints, an album or returns for family sessions. That is a real reason to bundle sessions into higher-value packages.
- **Brand clicks justify a high bid** because those people already know you, so the inquiry and booking rates should be much higher than for cold searches.
- **Do not trust a ceiling you have not tested.** Use your first four to six weeks of data to replace every assumption. If you have fewer than about 10 inquiries per ad group, you have a guess, not a result.

To see the actual click estimate for your city, set your location in Google's [Keyword Planner](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/7337243). Google describes it as giving keyword ideas with estimated monthly searches and average cost, and it requires your account setup, including billing information, to be complete. Keywords with very low volume may return nothing. Compare the estimate with your ceiling before you commit budget.

## Do This Today

1. Write down your average package value for each job type and the most you would pay in ads to book one (the table above uses 15% as an example, which is my assumption; pick your own).
2. Open Keyword Planner in your Google Ads account, set your city or travel area, and enter "wedding photographer [city]" and your two next-best terms. Note the estimated monthly searches and average cost, and compare them with your ceiling click price.
3. Build one campaign per group above, with one ad group per job type, phrase and exact match only, and a separate landing page for each.
4. Paste the negative list, edit it to match what you sell, and book a recurring 20-minute slot every week to read the search terms report.
5. Start an inquiry sheet (date, keyword if known, job type, quote sent, booked, value). After 30 days, drop or fix any ad group that has spent twice your target cost per inquiry with none.

## FAQ

**Q: Which keywords are best for wedding photographers?**
A: Start with "wedding photographer [city]" in phrase and exact match, plus your region and any venues you have shot at. Judge them by booked weddings, not clicks. Which one is cheapest depends on your market, so check Keyword Planner and your search terms report.

**Q: Should I bid on "photographer near me"?**
A: Test it, but not as your first group. It is broad (it could mean any job) so it often attracts mixed enquiries. Put it in its own ad group and compare its cost per booking with your job-specific terms.

**Q: Should I use broad match?**
A: Not at the start. Google says broad match works with Smart Bidding, which needs conversion data. Get tracking in place and a solid negative list first.

**Q: How many negative keywords do I need?**
A: Start with the list above, edited to your services, and grow it from the search terms report. There is no magic number. Google warns that too many negatives can reduce your reach, so only add terms you are sure you do not want.

**Q: Should I bid on other photographers' names?**
A: Usually not. It is rarely cost-effective for a small studio, and in my view it adds reputation risk. Spend that budget on your own city and job terms. See [Facebook ads for photographers](/blog/facebook-ads-for-photographers) if you want another channel that does not depend on search.
