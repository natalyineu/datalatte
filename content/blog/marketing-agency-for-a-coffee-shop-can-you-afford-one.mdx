Most coffee shops cannot afford a full-service agency retainer out of profit. In Intuit QuickBooks data for 12 US states, a $2,000 monthly retainer ($24,000 a year) equals 123% of the average coffee shop's annual profit (median across states) and 3.7% of its revenue. A $1,000 retainer equals 61% of profit. Only a shop with a clear way to earn the fee back should sign.

This article gives the numbers and the questions to ask. It does not rank agencies. Pricing figures are quoted as published by vendors and surveys, and the profit math comes from a dataset DataLatte fetched on 2026-11-03.

## What does an agency retainer equal in a coffee shop's revenue and profit?

The dataset has average yearly revenue and profit for coffee shops (NAICS 722515, Snack and Nonalcoholic Beverage Bars) in 12 states. The table divides three retainer levels by those averages. Revenue shares are small, which is why retainers look affordable. Profit shares are large, which is why they are not.

Caveat: these are averages of businesses that keep their books in QuickBooks, not of all coffee shops in a state. The dataset has net profit only and no gross margin, so it cannot show how much of each sale is left after ingredients and cups.

## How many states can absorb a retainer from profit?

Count the states where the yearly fee is below average profit. At $1,000 a month that is 9 of 12 states. At $2,000 it is 4 of 12 (Georgia, California, Ohio, Pennsylvania), and each of those is only just under 100% except Pennsylvania at 61%. At $3,000 only Pennsylvania (91%) stays under.

Read it as a ceiling test. A shop that pays the fee out of profit it already earns can barely do so. A shop that expects the agency to bring in the money to cover the fee is making a bet, and the next sections show how large the bet is.

## What do agencies actually charge?

Published figures vary widely because they measure different things. All numbers below are as published by each source and are vendor or survey data, not a quote for any specific shop.

- Clutch's digital marketing pricing guide (updated September 21, 2026) says "$5,000 - $50,000 per month" based on data for over 100,000 firms, and that most agencies on Clutch charge $25 to $49 an hour. For SEO, PPC, email and social it lists $100 to $149 an hour. Clutch gives no small-business or minimum-project figures on that page.
- WebFX's local SEO pricing page says local SEO "costs an average of $500 - $3,000 per month for monthly retainers", with its own service "starting at $575/per month". The same page's size table lists $100 to $500 a month for businesses of 11 to 50 employees.
- SEO.com's 2026 local SEO guide (updated September 14, 2026) also lists $100 to $500 a month for 11 to 50 employees, and $75 to $100 an hour for hourly work.

These sources disagree by an order of magnitude. Clutch's range covers firms of every size, while the WebFX and SEO.com figures are for local SEO only and are sold by firms that sell it. Neither speaks for what a given coffee shop would be quoted for ads plus SEO plus reporting.

**Assumption:** the shop pays $2,000 a month and the agency's work brings in extra sales with 65% left after ingredients and cups. The shop would need $24,000 / 0.65 = about $36,923 in extra yearly sales to break even on the fee alone, which is about 5,275 extra $7 tickets a year, or roughly 101 a week. Ad spend, tax and staff time come on top.

Whether that is realistic depends on the shop's own numbers. Replace the three assumed inputs with real ones. The point of the exercise is to know the break-even number before signing, not to trust this one.

## What should you ask an agency before signing?

Use this checklist. The first items rest on Google's own help pages, and the last on the FTC's small-business advertising guide.

1. **Who owns the ad accounts?** Google Ads Help describes access levels (email-only, read-only, standard, admin). Admins can manage other users' access and accept or unlink manager accounts. Ask that the shop's own login stays Admin and that the agency gets Standard access or a manager link the shop can remove.
2. **Is there a second administrator?** Google's page on managing access recommends "adding at least one additional administrator to your account" so tag access is not lost if one admin becomes unavailable.
3. **What will be counted as a result?** Google describes conversions as the actions you choose to measure, such as a purchase, sign-up or phone call. Ask which actions will be tracked, and who sets that up.
4. **Who owns the Meta ad account and business portfolio?** Meta's Business Help Center has pages on creating a business portfolio and on adding people and assigning assets to it. Their text did not load when DataLatte opened them, so no claim from those pages is made here. Read them and ask the agency which portfolio will hold the account.
5. **How do you exit?** Ask for notice period, minimum term, and whether reports, creative files and account history stay with the shop.
6. **What is promised?** The FTC's small-business guide says advertisers "must have evidence to back up their claims", that a money-back guarantee is "not a substitute for substantiation", and that an agency can also be held responsible for misleading ads. Be wary of guaranteed rankings or sales figures.
7. **What does the fee cover?** Ask whether ad spend is separate, whether there is a setup fee, and what reporting is included.

## FAQ

### How much does a marketing agency cost for a coffee shop?

No source reviewed publishes a coffee-shop-specific price. Published ranges run from $100 to $500 a month for small-business local SEO (SEO.com, WebFX) to $5,000 to $50,000 a month across all agencies on Clutch. Get written quotes for the exact scope.

### Can a coffee shop afford a marketing agency?

On QuickBooks averages, a $1,000 monthly retainer is below average profit in 9 of 12 states, and a $2,000 retainer in 4 of 12. Averages hide big differences between shops, and the data covers QuickBooks users only.

### Are digital marketing agencies for coffee shops worth it?

It depends on whether the work earns more than it costs. Work out the break-even number of extra sales first, as in the worked example above, and ask how results will be measured.

### Should the coffee shop own its ad accounts?

Google's help describes Admin access as the level that manages other users and manager links, so the owner keeping Admin is the safer setup. Ask the same of any Meta ad account.

### Is a freelancer cheaper than an agency?

Clutch says freelancers will likely charge less but gives no figure. Compare quotes for the same scope and check what you get in reporting and account access.
