Push notifications bring lapsed customers back when they are sent at set points of inactivity. One win-back guide recommends triggers at 7, 14, and 30 days without activity, with 2 to 3 nudges tied to usage thresholds ([Propello Cloud](https://propellocloud.com/blog/win-back-campaigns/)). That guide targets app users, so a local business has to translate it into visits. This article covers timing, a worked revenue example with labelled assumptions, and what the evidence does and does not prove about repeat business.

## When should a push notification go out after a customer stops visiting?

The clearest timing in the sources comes from the same win-back guide. It advises that push notifications trigger at 7, 14, and 30 days of inactivity, based on usage thresholds ([Propello Cloud](https://propellocloud.com/blog/win-back-campaigns/)). Those day counts are a starting point, not a universal rule. A cafe with a near-daily habit and a salon with a six-week rebooking cycle should not run the same clock.

A second reference point is the lapsed threshold. One customer reactivation guide suggests setting it at roughly 1.5 to 2 times the typical repeat-visit interval for each service ([Winback Engine](https://winbackengine.com/blog/customer-reactivation-guide/), secondary). If a typical interval is 14 days (assumption), the lapsed threshold falls between 21 and 28 days.

Map the schedule to that threshold. Send the first nudge when a customer crosses it, then follow up at roughly twice that point, and stop after the third message if there is no response. Keep the sequence to the 2 to 3 nudges the guide describes.

## What does a worked push campaign look like?

The model below uses assumed inputs only, so replace every figure with your own data before relying on it. The structure matters more than the numbers: lapsed customers, a response rate, and an average spend.

Inputs (all assumptions): 300 opted-in customers; a typical visit interval of 14 days; a lapsed threshold of 21 days (1.5 times the interval); 120 customers who cross that threshold in a month; 8% of them return after a push; an average return spend of $18; a 10% offer costing $1.80 per visit; and 2 pushes per month.

Calculation: 120 lapsed customers multiplied by 8% gives 9.6 returning visits per month. At $18 each, that is $172.80 in revenue. The 10% offer costs about $17.28 across those visits, leaving roughly $155.50 in net revenue before product costs and platform fees.

Treat that as a test budget, not a forecast. Split the lapsed group in half, send the push to one half only, and compare return rates after 30 days. The [A/B testing guide for local businesses](/blog/a-b-testing-for-local-business) explains how to set up that comparison without sample-size guesswork.

## How do you keep messages from becoming noise?

Automation does not guarantee loyalty. One pet grooming marketing provider states that its rebooking and reminder workflows do not guarantee repeat visits or eliminate missed appointments ([TailWerks](https://tailwerks.com/industries/pet-grooming-marketing-agency/)). Push notifications are one input among several, and they fail quietly when they are too frequent.

None of the sources reviewed sets a weekly frequency limit, so the cap has to come from your own opt-out data. Set a monthly maximum in your platform, track unsubscribes after each send, and pause a segment if opt-outs rise after a change in frequency.

Reminders for booked appointments belong in a separate flow. For businesses that take bookings, the [appointment booking agent guide](/blog/ai-agent-for-appointment-booking-local-business) covers how automated rebooking fits alongside promotional pushes.

## Which offers tie a push to a visit?

An offer works best when it is tied to a specific visit window. One illustrative example in the Adwave guide to marketing automation describes a local boutique that sends a repeat-customer offer 30 days after a customer's last visit ([Adwave](https://adwave.com/resources/marketing-automation-for-small-businesses), secondary). That example is a hypothetical goal, not measured data.

Rotation also matters. A salon case described by BAER PM says its calls to action and promotions change every month, offering different deals to encourage repeat visits ([BAER PM](https://baerpm.com/2024/03/15/marketing-madness-how-brands-are-capitalizing-on-the-ncaas-biggest-event/)). Rotating the offer keeps a single discount from training customers to wait for it.

Pair pushes with a monthly email. Madsun Media says a simple monthly email keeps a business top of mind and drives repeat sales at almost no cost ([Madsun Media](https://www.madsunmedia.com/blog/small-business-marketing-australia), secondary). Email and push can carry the same offer in different formats.

## How do you start this month?

Work through these steps in order. Each one uses data you already have.

1. Export every opted-in customer with their last visit date and the service they booked.
2. For each service, record the typical interval between visits, then set the lapsed threshold at 1.5 to 2 times that interval.
3. Write an opt-in prompt that states a specific reason to allow notifications, such as same-day cancellation openings, and say how often you will send.
4. Build the 7, 14, and 30 day triggers for lapsed customers and cap total sends at two or three per customer per month.
5. Hold out a control group and compare return visits after 30 days. Keep the offer that beats the control, then retest.

Once the schedule runs, check the customer-facing replies as well. The [customer service agent guide](/blog/ai-agent-for-customer-service-local-business) explains how to handle replies to promotional messages without adding staff hours.

## FAQ

### Are push notifications the same as text messages?

The sources here do not compare costs or opt-in rules between push and text. Check each platform's pricing page and consent requirements before choosing a channel.

### How many messages should I send per week?

None of the sources reviewed sets a weekly frequency. Start with the 2 to 3 nudges in the lapsed sequence and monitor unsubscribes in your own data before increasing volume.

### Do I need a mobile app to send push notifications?

The win-back guide was written for app users, so it assumes an app. Your platform's help page will state which channels it supports, including browser-based options if you do not have an app.

### Is a push campaign worth it for a single-location business?

It depends on how many regular customers you have and what they spend. Run the worked example above with your own figures. If the recovered revenue does not cover the time and platform cost, focus on other channels first.
