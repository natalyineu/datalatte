Here is the sum that decides whether Microsoft Advertising is worth your time. Say a Google click costs you $5 and 8 in 100 clicks become a lead: that is $62.50 a lead. If Bing clicks are 30% cheaper but only 6 in 100 convert, you pay about $58. If Bing clicks are only 10% cheaper and 5 in 100 convert, you pay $90. Same platform, same budget, a 50% swing. You will not know which one you are in until you test it with your own numbers, and a $300-a-month test is enough to find out.

This guide is for owners of local businesses in the US, UK, Australia and Canada who already run Google Ads, or are about to. It covers whether Microsoft Advertising (still widely called Bing Ads) suits your business, how to launch by importing from Google, how to set local targeting, how to track leads, and how to decide at the end. Dollar figures are US-based; use the same sums with your own currency.

## What You Are Buying

Microsoft Advertising sells search ads that appear on Bing and on partner properties. Microsoft's own [advertising site](https://about.ads.microsoft.com/) names Yahoo, DuckDuckGo and Ecosia among its partnerships. That site is promotional and gives no audience numbers with a method behind them, so use it for what is offered, not for how big it is.

One practical detail comes from an [Adswerve guide](https://adswerve.com/technical-insights/how-to-measure-duckduckgo-traffic-in-ga4-with-microsoft-advertising): DuckDuckGo has no self-serve ad platform, it serves its search ads through Microsoft Advertising's syndicated partner network, and there is no separate opt-in. Turning off search partners therefore also removes DuckDuckGo. The same guide notes that its clicks usually appear in Google Analytics as `bing / cpc`, so partner traffic can blend into your Bing numbers unless you pull a publisher report.

## How Big Is It, Really?

Smaller than Google, which is the whole point of managing expectations. A [Searchlab roundup](https://searchlab.nl/en/statistics/microsoft-ads-statistics-2026) cites Statcounter data for March 2026 putting Bing's US desktop search share at 14.2%, or 18.7% including Yahoo, which uses Bing results. Searchlab is a search agency and that is a second-hand figure for desktop only. The roundup also gives mixed-up totals (it lists different audience numbers for different networks), so ignore its user counts.

Two takeaways:

- **Volume will be a fraction of Google's.** Expect a handful of leads a month from a small local budget, not dozens.
- **Desktop skews higher.** The same roundup says the average Bing user is older than Google's average. It cites Comscore for ages 45 versus 38, which we could not verify. Check your own Google Analytics for the device and age mix of the customers you want.

## Is Bing Cheaper? Sources Disagree

You will read that Bing clicks cost about a third less than Google. Here is what we found:

- The Searchlab roundup says the average US search CPC is $1.37 on Microsoft versus $2.06 on Google, citing WordStream's 2026 benchmarks, and that Merkle puts Microsoft about 33% lower. We could not open either original.
- WordStream's own recent search benchmarks, as described in search results, blend Google and Microsoft data. WordStream's only Bing-specific report we could find is from 2017. Treat any "Bing is X% cheaper" figure as a direction, not a promise.
- Tinuiti's Q4 2025 benchmark, as summarised on a reposting site, says Microsoft's CPCs rose 5% year on year while Google's non-brand CPCs ended the year down 1%. That is about change, not level, but it shows the gap is not fixed.

So: Bing is often cheaper per click, by a margin nobody has measured for your town. The math section below shows why cost per lead is the number to watch, and why a lower CPC alone settles nothing.

## Who Should Test It

A good fit:

- **Trades and home services** where someone searches a specific job (fencing, plumbing, roofing).
- **Professional and medical services** where customers research on a work or home PC.
- **Any business already profitable on Google** and ready for a second source of search leads.

A weak fit: mobile-first, impulse-visit businesses such as a neighbourhood café, where Google Maps and your Business Profile do most of the work. If that is you, start with [Google Ads for coffee shops](/blog/google-ads-for-coffee-shops) and revisit Bing later.

## Step 1: Account and Tracking Tag

1. Sign up at ads.microsoft.com, set your currency and time zone (check both before saving).
2. Create a UET tag. Microsoft's [Universal Event Tracking guide](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/advertising/guides/universal-event-tracking) says UET is a prerequisite for conversion tracking and remarketing, that you create the tag once and add it to every page of your site, and that a site-wide layout is the best place for it.
3. Create conversion goals for what you actually want: a form submit, a booking, a click-to-call. The same guide lists goal types including URL and event goals.

Do this before you spend anything. Without it you are buying clicks you cannot count.

## Step 2: Import From Google Ads, Then Edit

Microsoft's [import page](https://about.ads.microsoft.com/en-us/solutions/tools/import-google-ads-to-microsoft-advertising) says you can import everything or select campaigns, that the elements imported can include campaign structure, ad copy, keywords, ad groups and targeting settings (varying by campaign type), and that you should review imported settings, bids, targeting, keywords and creative before going live. It also mentions granular import options to adjust bids and budgets during import, and recurring imports to stay in sync.

Our advice:

- **Import one campaign, your best one.** Not the whole account.
- **Set the budget during import** so it does not copy your Google budget. Set it manually.
- **Skip the recurring import** at first. Synced changes you did not mean to make are an easy way to waste money.
- **Review the keyword list.** Remove broad terms that only worked on Google because of its Smart Bidding data.
- **Copy negative keywords across.** Our negative keywords guide explains the lists.

Assets (what Google used to call extensions) like call, location and sitelinks matter here too. Our [Google Ads assets guide](/blog/how-to-use-google-ads-extensions-local-business) lists the ones to add; Microsoft has its own versions of each.

## Step 3: Local Targeting

Microsoft's API documentation for the [radius criterion](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/advertising/campaign-management-service/radiuscriterion?view=bingads-13) shows you can target a radius around a zip code, coordinates or landmark, from 1 to 500 miles (1 to 800 km). The companion [location intent setting](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/advertising/campaign-management-service/locationintentcriterion?view=bingads-13) says the default is to show ads to people in, searching for, or viewing pages about your target location, and that you can choose "people in" only. As with any platform, treat location targeting as approximate.

For a local business:

1. Set a radius from your real customer data (the zip or postcodes of your last 50 customers).
2. Choose **people in your targeted location** if you do not want clicks from searchers elsewhere who merely read about your town. That is our recommendation, not a rule.
3. Exclude areas you do not serve using location exclusions (the API pages above describe negative locations as a separate criterion; check the exact options in your account).
4. Match the ad schedule to hours when someone answers the phone. See call tracking for local business if calls are your main lead.

## Step 4: Budget

Microsoft sets no recommended local budget in the sources we could open. Use the test budget in the math below: roughly 25% of your Google spend, or $300 a month for a $1,200 Google account, and run it for at least 8 weeks. Low volume means results arrive slowly. Resist changing things every few days. For Google-side planning, see our Google Ads budget guide.

## The Math

Take a $1,000 Google budget as the baseline and add a $300 Bing test. Every row below is an **assumption** for illustration. The click prices and lead rates are guesses, because no verified local benchmark exists for Bing.

| | Google (baseline) | Bing: weak | Bing: base | Bing: strong |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Monthly budget (assumption) | $1,000 | $300 | $300 | $300 |
| Cost per click (assumption) | $5.00 | $4.50 | $3.50 | $3.25 |
| Clicks | 200 | 67 | 86 | 92 |
| Click-to-lead rate (assumption) | 8% | 5% | 6% | 8% |
| Leads | 16.0 | 3.3 | 5.1 | 7.4 |
| Cost per lead | $62.50 | $90 | $58 | $41 |

How to read it:

- **The break-even is cost per lead, not CPC.** In the weak column Bing clicks are 10% cheaper, but you still pay 44% more per lead.
- **The sample is small.** Bing leads at 3 to 7 a month means a single week can swing your cost per lead wildly. Wait for at least 15 leads before deciding.
- **Quality counts too.** Track leads that become paying customers, not just form fills. A lead from Bing that is worth twice as much changes the answer.

## Review Weekly, Decide at Eight Weeks

Weekly: read the search terms report and add negatives, pause keywords that spent about twice your target cost per lead with no leads, and check which partner sites sent clicks. Monthly: compare Bing and Google cost per lead and cost per customer side by side.

At week eight, use a written rule: keep Bing if cost per lead is within about 20% of Google's (a threshold we suggest, not a standard), grow it by 20% if it beats Google, and stop if it is worse after 15 leads. Also check your free Bing Places for Business listing is claimed and correct. Third-party guides say you can import from your Google Business Profile, but we could not open Microsoft's own steps, so check them at bing.com/forbusiness.

## Do This Today

1. Pull your Google Ads cost per lead and cost per customer for the last 90 days, so you have a number to beat.
2. Create a Microsoft Advertising account and a UET tag, add the tag site-wide, and set up one conversion goal for your main enquiry.
3. Import only your best-performing Google campaign, set the budget to about 25% of your Google spend, and skip recurring imports.
4. Set a radius from your last 50 customers' zip codes, choose "people in" targeting, and copy your negative keywords across.
5. Write your decision rule on paper (for example, "keep if cost per lead is within 20% of Google after 15 leads"), and book an 8-week review in your calendar.

## FAQ

**Q: Is Bing Ads cheaper than Google Ads?**
A: Often, but nobody has published a verified local figure. A Searchlab roundup cites a 33% lower average CPC, while Tinuiti data shows Microsoft CPCs rose in 2025. Judge on your own cost per lead.

**Q: Can I import my Google Ads campaigns?**
A: Yes. Microsoft's import tool brings in campaign structure, keywords, ads and targeting settings (exact items vary by campaign type). Review everything before going live.

**Q: Do I need a separate tracking tag?**
A: Yes. Microsoft's documentation says a UET tag is a prerequisite for conversion tracking and remarketing.

**Q: Does Bing include Yahoo and DuckDuckGo?**
A: Microsoft names Yahoo and DuckDuckGo as partners. Per Adswerve, DuckDuckGo ads run through the syndicated partner network, so turning off search partners removes it.

**Q: Should a small business run both Google and Bing?**
A: Once Google is profitable, a small Bing test is reasonable. Keep it only if cost per lead, or better still cost per customer, is acceptable.
