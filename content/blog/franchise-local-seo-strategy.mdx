In BrightLocal's 2026 survey of 1,002 US adults, 68% said they would only use a business rated 4 stars or higher, and 31% wanted 4.5 or better. Reviews belong to a single Google Business Profile, not to your brand. So a franchise with a great flagship and one sloppy branch has one location quietly losing the customers who check before they walk in. That is the whole problem of franchise local SEO in one fact: every location is its own competitor in its own neighbourhood, and the brand name only helps so far.

This guide is for franchisors and multi-unit operators in the US, UK, Australia and Canada: coffee, salons, fitness, pet care and similar. It covers how Google ranks local results, the profile and page setup each location needs, review and governance rules, how to measure per location, and a break-even calculation so you know what local SEO can afford to cost per branch. It sets out what Google's pages say and marks my advice as mine.

## How Google Ranks Locations (and What You Cannot Change)

Google's [help page on local ranking](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091) names three factors: relevance (how well a profile matches the search), distance (how far the business is from the searcher) and prominence (how well known it is, with the number of websites linking to a business and its reviews and ratings cited as signals). The same page states "There's no way to request or pay for a better local ranking on Google."

For a franchise that has three consequences:

- **Distance is fixed** by where each unit sits. You cannot optimise your way closer to the customer.
- **Relevance is yours to improve:** accurate categories, services, hours and a matching page.
- **Prominence is partly inherited and partly local.** A known brand starts ahead, but reviews and links accumulate branch by branch.

A common failure is a brand site with one store locator and no individual pages. The locator is fine for visitors, but it gives Google little to match to each unit.

## Step 1: Fix the Data Once, Centrally

Before any tactic, put the facts for every location in one master sheet or a location management tool: exact sign name, full address, local phone number, hours (including holidays), categories, services and the location page URL. Vendors in this space include Yext, BrightLocal, Uberall, Semrush Local and Moz Local; prices are per location and vary, so ask for current quotes. For a handful of units, a spreadsheet and a named owner can be enough.

## Step 2: One Profile Per Physical Location

Google's [guidelines for representing your business](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177) say, on the points that matter to a franchise:

- "Do not create more than one page for each location of your business" (the exceptions are separately listed practitioners and departments).
- P.O. boxes and mailboxes at remote locations are not acceptable, and a rented mailing address where you do not operate is a virtual office, which is not eligible.
- Business names should exclude taglines, hours, phone numbers, URLs and service or location details. So no "Brand Coffee Best Latte Austin" in the name field; the city goes in the address, not the title.

Beyond the guidelines, my advice: the brand, not the franchisee, should own each profile, with the franchisee added as a manager. That way a departing operator does not take the listing with them.

**Bulk verification.** Google's [bulk verification help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/4490296) says it requires 10 or more profiles from the same business, does not count duplicate, suspended or disabled profiles toward that minimum, excludes service-area businesses, and does not allow agencies with more than one business in one account. A smaller network verifies location by location.

## Step 3: A Real Page for Every Location

Each location needs its own page on the brand site, not a name swap on a template. Include:

| Element | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Unique title and heading with the town | Matches what people search |
| Address, phone, hours, map embed | Must match the profile |
| Local content | Landmarks, parking, manager or team intro |
| Local offers and services | Differentiates the page |
| Real local photos | Shows the actual place |
| Reviews from that location | Builds trust |
| LocalBusiness structured data | Helps Google understand the entity |
| One clear action | Book, order or call |

On structured data, Google's [LocalBusiness documentation](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/local-business) says `name` and `address` are required, other properties such as hours, phone and geo coordinates are recommended, and each location should be marked up as its own LocalBusiness using the most specific subtype available. It also says the markup can go on any page of your site, and it does not say each location must have its own page; that part is my advice.

**Avoid doorway pages.** Google's [spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) define doorway abuse as sites or pages "created to rank for specific, similar search queries", and list "multiple domain names or pages targeted at specific regions or cities that funnel users to one page" as an example. A page for a real branch with real details is not that. Fifty pages for towns where you have no unit is.

Give franchisees a template with required fields (hours, team, one local story, photos) and optional sections, so the page ends up genuinely different, not just renamed.

## Step 4: A Crawlable Site Structure

Keep it simple: a locations hub (`/locations`), regional pages where you have several branches (`/locations/texas`), and individual pages (`/locations/texas/austin/south-lamar`). Link from the homepage to the hub, from the hub to each branch, and between nearby branches. Check that your store locator is built from real links, not a map widget that search engines cannot read. ## Step 5: Reviews Are a Local Job

Reviews accrue to one profile, so every branch needs its own habit. BrightLocal's [2026 survey](https://www.brightlocal.com/research/local-consumer-review-survey/) reports that 97% of consumers read online reviews for local businesses, 41% say they always do, 74% look for reviews from the last three months and Google is the most used platform at 71%. That makes recency a branch-level job.

- Give each store a direct review link and a QR code.
- Train staff to ask at a good moment, without pressure.
- Reply to every review within a couple of days in the brand voice with local detail.
- Report rating, count and response rate by branch.

One firm rule. Google's [review policy](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114) says merchants may not offer rewards for posting reviews, may not selectively solicit positive reviews or discourage negative ones, and staff should not pressure customers to leave ratings on the premises. A franchisor review contest with prizes would break that. More in our [guide to Google Business Profile reviews](/blog/google-business-profile-reviews) and [how to get more 5-star Google reviews](/blog/how-to-get-more-5-star-google-reviews-local-business).

## Step 6: Local Content and Local Links

Prominence also comes from links. Each branch can earn them locally: sponsor a school team or charity run and ask for a link to the branch page, join the chamber of commerce, get listed in community directories, and send a short press note when a branch opens or hits a milestone. Post updates from each profile. Hand franchisees a monthly checklist with three jobs, not thirty.

## Step 7: Governance Between Franchisor and Franchisee

Many franchise SEO problems are organisational. Decide and write down:

1. **Ownership.** The brand holds profile ownership; franchisees are managers.
2. **Edit rights.** Lock brand elements; allow local fields (hours, team, offers).
3. **Speed.** A turnaround time for hours and holiday changes.
4. **Review replies.** Local managers, brand guidelines.
5. **Closures and transfers.** Update or close profiles promptly so stale listings do not confuse customers.

Put it in your franchise operations manual.

## Step 8: Measure Per Location

Report by branch, not just by brand:

- Calls, direction requests and website clicks from each profile.
- Rankings for core searches in each branch's own area. Rankings vary block by block, so use a rank tracker with several points or a grid.
- Review count, average rating and response rate.
- Visits and key events on each location page, set up with [Google Tag Manager](/blog/google-tag-manager-for-small-business).
- Bookings or orders by source.

Compare a branch against similar branches, not against the brand average. See [data analytics for small business](/blog/data-analytics-for-small-business).

## The Math

What can local SEO afford to cost per branch? Break-even depends on how many extra customers the work brings in. Every figure below is an **assumption** for illustration; replace them with your own.

| | Low-ticket (coffee) | Mid-ticket (salon) | Higher-ticket (fitness) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Local SEO cost per branch per month (assumption) | $150 | $150 | $150 |
| Gross profit per new customer (assumption) | $8 | $30 | $60 |
| New customers needed per branch to break even | 19 | 5 | 2.5 |
| Share of profile actions that become new customers (assumption) | 10% | 10% | 10% |
| Extra profile actions needed per branch | 188 | 50 | 25 |
| For a 10-branch network: monthly cost | $1,500 | $1,500 | $1,500 |

How to read it: break-even customers = monthly cost / gross profit per new customer. The low-ticket case needs far more extra footfall to pay for the same work, which is why coffee-style networks lean on cheap, repeatable tasks (review links, a template page, automated listings) rather than custom work per branch. Check your own profile insights for actual call, direction and website-click counts before you apply any conversion share. Remember that gross profit per new customer is only the first visit, and repeat visits improve the picture.

## Common Mistakes

- One phone number or profile shared across several locations.
- Duplicate listings after a rebrand or move.
- Copy-pasted pages with only the town name changed.
- Virtual offices or mailboxes treated as locations.
- Franchisees creating unofficial profiles or pages.
- Closed locations left active.
- Reporting only brand-level traffic.

## Do This Today

1. Build the master sheet: sign name, address, phone, hours, categories and page URL for every location, and mark every mismatch against the live Google profile.
2. List which profiles the brand actually owns and which a franchisee or former operator controls, and fix ownership of the top five by revenue first.
3. Pick your three weakest-rated branches and set up a review link, a QR code and a reply routine at each.
4. Rebuild one location page properly (unique content, photos, hours, LocalBusiness markup) and use it as the template for the rest.
5. Create a one-page monthly report by branch with calls, direction requests, review count, average rating and ranking in the local area.

## FAQ

**Q: Should each franchise location have its own website?**
A: Usually one strong brand site with a real page per location is easier to manage and concentrates links in one place. Microsites need tight control. Whichever you choose, avoid doorway-style pages for towns where you have no branch.

**Q: Can franchisees create their own Google Business Profiles?**
A: Google's guidelines say not to create more than one page per location, so an unofficial second profile risks a duplicate. It is better for the brand to own profiles and add franchisees as managers.

**Q: What tools do franchises use?**
A: Yext, BrightLocal, Uberall, Semrush Local and Moz Local are common, alongside Google's own tools. Pricing varies by location count, so compare quotes. Google says there is no way to pay for a better local ranking.

**Q: How long before rankings improve?**
A: There is no official timeline. Fixing wrong data can show effects sooner than building reviews and links. Track by branch and judge over a few months, my rule of thumb rather than a Google figure.
