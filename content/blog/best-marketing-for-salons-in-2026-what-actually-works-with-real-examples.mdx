The best marketing for a salon in 2026 is a complete, well-reviewed Google Business Profile, a system that turns first-time visitors into second-time visitors, and only then paid ads or local creator partnerships. Boulevard's data on more than 11 million appointments shows why the order matters: 45% of clients return after a first visit on average, and 70% at the best salons. Winning the second visit is cheaper than buying a new first one.

This guide is for hair, nail and beauty salons in the US, UK, Australia and Canada. Every number below comes from a source listed at the end. ## Which Salon Marketing Channel Should Come First?

Start with the channel that catches people already looking for a salon: Google Maps and Search. Google says local results are mainly based on relevance, distance and prominence, and that more reviews and positive ratings can help your local ranking. You cannot change distance. You can change relevance (a complete profile with accurate categories, services and photos) and prominence (reviews and how well known you are).

BrightLocal's Local Consumer Review Survey 2026, a US panel of 1,002 adults, reports that 97% of consumers read reviews for local businesses, 68% will only use a business rated four stars or more, and 74% only care about reviews written in the last three months. A salon with a stale or thin review history loses clients before any ad is seen.

A practical order for a salon with limited time:

1. Profile and reviews (free, covers searches like "hair salon near me").
2. Rebooking and follow-up (low cost, protects the clients you already won).
3. Paid search for high-intent services such as color, extensions or bridal.
4. Local creators and social content, once the first three can handle extra demand.

## How Many First-Time Clients Actually Come Back?

Fewer than half. Boulevard's report, based on about 4 million clients at more than 2,500 businesses between January 2022 and March 2023, gives these rates:

| Step | Average salon | Best-in-class salon |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First visit to second appointment | 45% | 70% |
| First visit to third appointment | 39% | 57% |

Boulevard also reports that best-in-class salons retain 56% more first-time visitors than average ones, and that average salons need about twice as many new clients to reach the same revenue. Clients whose first appointment was booked online came back for a second visit 78% of the time, against 39% for walk-ins.

A UK and Ireland survey tells a similar story. Phorest's Consumer Insights Report 2025 (716 salon guests) reports new client retention of just 29%. The two figures use different definitions and different countries, so do not average them. Both say the same thing: most first-time clients do not return without a prompt.

The fixes Boulevard and Phorest point to are simple. Offer online booking, follow up after the first visit, and make it easy to book the next appointment before the client leaves. ## What Does the Second Visit Add? A Worked Example

All inputs below are **assumptions** for illustration, except the 45% and 70% return rates, which come from Boulevard. Replace the rest with your own numbers.

| Input | Value |
| --- | --- |
| New clients per month (assumption) | 40 |
| Average ticket (assumption) | $90 |
| Second-visit rate at an average salon (Boulevard) | 45% |
| Second-visit rate at a best-in-class salon (Boulevard) | 70% |

At 45%, 40 new clients produce 18 second visits. At 70%, they produce 28. The 10 extra visits at $90 are worth $900 a month from one month of new clients, before counting third and later visits. A similar jump through ads would require buying more first visits. At the $39.25 cost per lead in the LocaliQ benchmark for the category, and assuming one in three leads books (an assumption), 10 extra clients would cost roughly $1,180 in lead costs alone.

That comparison is a guide, not a promise. The point is the direction: improving rebooking first makes every later marketing dollar go further.

## Is Google Ads Worth It for a Salon?

It can be, for services with a clear price and a high ticket. WordStream by LocaliQ's 2026 benchmarks list Beauty and Personal Care at a $4.62 average cost per click, a 6.75% click-through rate, a 10.35% conversion rate and a $39.25 cost per lead. The all-industry averages in the same report are $5.42 per click, 6.64%, 8.18% and $66.69 per lead. A conversion here means a lead action such as a call or a form, not necessarily a completed booking, and the data blends Google and Microsoft search campaigns.

Use the figures as a range for planning. Your city, services and landing page decide the real result. Pick services where the ticket justifies the click, send each ad to a page for that service with a booking button, and track calls and bookings separately. ## Do Influencers and Local Creators Work for Salons?

They can, but this guide found no verified benchmark for results, so treat promised multipliers such as "2x more bookings" with caution. What can be sourced is the rulebook, and it is strict.

The FTC's guidance for influencers says a material connection includes receiving free or discounted products or services, and that it must be disclosed. The disclosure should sit with the endorsement itself, not only on a profile page or inside a pile of hashtags, and in a video it should be in the video. If a salon gives a creator a free service in return for a post, the creator should say so in the post. Put that requirement in writing before the appointment.

A low-risk way to test the channel:

- Choose a few local creators whose audience actually lives near the salon. Check where their commenters are, not just the follower count.
- Agree the service, the post format, the disclosure wording, the booking link and permission to reuse the content.
- Give each creator a unique link or code, so bookings can be traced.
- Judge the test by bookings and revenue against what the free service cost you, not by likes.

For paid social, start with a small test budget. Agency case studies for salon ads exist, but they are self-reported by the vendors selling the service and rarely show methodology, so none are quoted here.

## How Do You Measure Salon Marketing?

Track four numbers per channel and review them monthly:

1. **Leads:** calls, forms, direction taps, DMs.
2. **Bookings:** how many of those became an appointment.
3. **Revenue:** what those appointments were worth.
4. **Second visits:** how many of those new clients returned.

Ask "how did you find us?" at booking or checkout and record the answer in the same place every time. Attribution for in-salon bookings is rarely perfect, so a simple, consistent question beats a complicated system you will not maintain.

Divide the cost of each channel by the number of new clients who booked a second visit. That cost per retained client is a fairer comparison than cost per click.

## FAQ

### What is the best marketing for a salon in 2026?

A complete Google Business Profile with recent reviews, a rebooking and follow-up routine, and paid search for high-ticket services. Add local creators after those work. Boulevard reports 45% of clients return after a first visit on average and 70% at the best salons.

### How much does Google Ads cost for a salon?

WordStream by LocaliQ's 2026 benchmark lists $4.62 per click and $39.25 per lead for Beauty and Personal Care. Your cost depends on city and service, so use it as a planning range.

### Does influencer marketing work for salons?

It can for visual services, but there is no verified benchmark for results. Test with a small number of local creators, use trackable links, and make sure the creator discloses any free service as the FTC requires.

### How many new salon clients come back?

Boulevard reports 45% on average and 70% at the best-performing salons. Phorest's UK and Ireland survey reports 29%. The definitions and samples differ.

### How do I track which marketing brings clients?

Use unique links or codes, a booking source field and a "how did you find us?" question at checkout. Then compare cost per retained client by channel.
