What should a small business do first in Google Analytics 4 (GA4)? Fix six settings: mark your booking or call action as a key event, switch event data retention to 14 months, exclude your own visits, tag your campaign links, link Google Search Console and read engagement rate instead of old bounce rate. None of this needs a developer. Each setting below is taken from Google's own help pages, and the cost figures in the worked example are labelled assumptions.

This is a checklist for a site that already has GA4 installed. For installation, start with our [GA4 setup guide for local business](/blog/google-analytics-4-setup-local-business).

## Why Are You Using GA4 and Not Universal Analytics?

Universal Analytics no longer exists. Google's help center says standard Universal Analytics properties stopped processing new data on July 1, 2023, and that access to the interface and API ended on July 1, 2024 ([Google Analytics Help: Google Analytics 4 has replaced Universal Analytics](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/9973999)). If you still open an old dashboard, you are reading history, not current performance.

GA4 counts activity as events, not sessions and pageviews alone. That is why the first job is telling GA4 which event is the one that pays: a booking, a call tap, a form submission.

## What Is a Key Event and Which One Should You Pick?

Google defines a key event as "an event that measures an action that's particularly important to the success of your business", and says any collected event can become one ([Google Analytics Help: Key events](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/9267568)). You can also create a Google Ads conversion from a key event, which Google says reduces discrepancies between Google Ads and Analytics.

Pick the action closest to revenue:

| Business | Best key event | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Salon or groomer | Booking completed | A booking is revenue |
| Coffee shop | Tap on directions or phone | Footfall is the goal |
| Fitness studio | Trial or class booked | The first visit is the sale |
| Any service business | Quote form submitted | Starts a sales conversation |

Mark one primary key event first. Add more only when you will act on them. Our [hair salon GA4 guide](/blog/google-analytics-for-hair-salons) shows four events in detail, and the [Tag Manager guide](/blog/google-tag-manager-for-local-website-analytics) covers building tap events without code.

## Should You Change Data Retention?

Yes, if your property is still on the shorter setting. Standard properties can keep event data for 2 months or 14 months. Google says the setting only affects explorations and funnel reports, not standard aggregated reports ([Google Analytics Help: Data retention](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/7667196)).

Why this matters to a small business: an exploration showing how visitors reached a booking page becomes useless if it can only look back 2 months. Seasonal businesses such as coffee shops and gyms need to compare this January with last January. Find the setting in Admin, under your property's data settings. The page does not state which setting is the default, so check your property rather than assuming.

## How Do You Stop Counting Your Own Visits?

Your staff, you and your agency can inflate traffic. Google's method is two steps: define internal traffic by IP address under the web data stream's tag settings, then create a data filter that excludes events with that traffic type ([Google Analytics Help: Internal traffic](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/10104470)).

Know these limits before you start:

- Start the filter in **Testing** state. It tags matching data so you can check it in Explore without deleting anything.
- Switching to **Active** is permanent. Google says excluded data is never processed.
- A filter can take 24 to 36 hours to take effect.
- It works for website traffic only, and you need the Editor role.
- A property can have up to 10 data filters.

A shop on a shared or changing IP address may find this unreliable. In that case, ask staff to use a separate browser profile for checks.

## Which Attribution Model Should You Use?

Google lists three models for Analytics properties: data-driven attribution, paid and organic last click, and Google paid channels last click. Data-driven uses your account's data to estimate each click's contribution, and conversions can be reattributed for up to 7 days after they occur. Paid and organic last click ignores direct traffic and gives 100% of the credit to the last channel clicked ([Google Analytics Help: Attribution models](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/10596866)).

For a small site with few conversions, the practical advice is simple. Do not cancel a channel because it shows few last-click bookings. Check how that channel appears earlier in the path before deciding. Google's page does not say which model is the default for key events, so look at your attribution settings.

## What Do Engagement Rate and Bounce Rate Mean Now?

GA4 redefined both. Engagement rate is the percentage of engaged sessions. Bounce rate is the percentage of sessions that were not engaged. A session counts as engaged if it lasts longer than 10 seconds, has a key event or has 2 or more page views ([Google Analytics Help: Engagement rate and bounce rate](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/12195621)).

That gives you a clean test for a local site. A visitor who opens your menu page, reads for 15 seconds and leaves counts as engaged. A visitor who lands and leaves in 5 seconds does not. Compare engagement rate by landing page: a low rate on your booking page, with healthy traffic, is a page problem, not a traffic problem.

## Worked Example: Which Channel Deserves the Next 100 Dollars?

All numbers are **assumptions** for illustration.

| | Instagram ads | Google Ads | Newsletter |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Monthly spend | $300 | $300 | $20 |
| Sessions | 900 | 400 | 120 |
| Primary key events (bookings) | 6 | 12 | 5 |
| Cost per booking | $50 | $25 | $4 |
| Key event rate | 0.7% | 3.0% | 4.2% |

How to read it: Instagram brings the most visitors and the fewest bookings per dollar. The newsletter is cheapest per booking but small, so it cannot absorb the next 100 dollars. Google Ads would be the first place to test more budget, then watch whether cost per booking holds. This only works if links are tagged. See our guide to [UTM parameters](/blog/utm-parameters-track-every-marketing-campaign). Untagged social links often land in direct traffic, which hides the true source.

## What Should You Link and Check Weekly?

Link Google Search Console. Google says it adds two reports: Google Organic Search Queries and Google Organic Search Traffic, showing landing pages with Search Console and Analytics metrics. You need the Editor role on the property and verified ownership of the Search Console property ([Google Analytics Help: Link Search Console](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/10737381)).

A 15-minute weekly routine:

1. Open the acquisition report and compare key events by channel.
2. Check engagement rate for your top three landing pages.
3. Look at organic search landing pages from Search Console.
4. Write down one action, such as a changed button or a paused ad.

## FAQ

**Q: Is GA4 enough, or do I need my booking software too?**
A: Use both. GA4 shows where visitors came from. Your booking system shows what was actually booked. Compare them monthly.

**Q: Why do my GA4 numbers differ from my sales?**
A: GA4 measures website behaviour. Offline sales, app bookings, ad blockers and cookie choices can all reduce what it sees. Treat it as a sample, not a ledger.

**Q: Should I delete the Universal Analytics property?**
A: Google says access to Universal Analytics data ended on July 1, 2024. Check whether you still need anything exported before removing the property.

**Q: How long does setup take?**
A: The six settings above take a few hours the first time, including testing. That is an estimate, not a Google figure.

**Q: Do I need a cookie banner?**
A: It depends on where your visitors are and how you use data. Ask a local adviser, especially for UK and EU visitors.
