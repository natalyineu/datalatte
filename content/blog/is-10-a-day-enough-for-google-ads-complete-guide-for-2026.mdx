Yes, $10 a day is enough to run a small local Google Ads test, but not enough to run a big campaign or to compete in expensive categories. Google counts a $10 average daily budget as up to $304 a month. At WordStream by LocaliQ's 2026 average click costs, that buys roughly 150 clicks in restaurants and food, about 66 in beauty and personal care, and about 31 in legal services. Whether that produces customers depends on your category, your city and your landing page.

This guide is for local businesses in the US, UK, Australia and Canada. Every figure comes from a source listed at the end, and the worked examples are labelled as assumptions. The earlier version of this article contained client results that could not be verified, so they were removed.

## What Does a $10 Daily Budget Actually Mean?

It is an average, not a hard daily limit. Google's help page says your spend can vary by day, so on some days you might not reach your average daily budget and on others you might exceed it. The daily spending limit is two times the average daily budget for most campaigns, and the monthly limit is 30.4 times the average daily budget. Google's own example: $304 a month is $10 a day.

So a $10 budget can spend up to $20 on a busy day, and you will not be charged more than $304 in a month. A budget that is set too low relative to click prices simply stops showing your ads earlier in the day.

## Is $10 a Day Enough? A Worked Example

All inputs are **assumptions** for illustration, except the cost per lead, which uses the Beauty and Personal Care benchmark of $39.25. Replace the rest with your own numbers.

| Input | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Monthly spend | $304 |
| Cost per lead (LocaliQ 2026 benchmark) | $39.25 |
| Leads per month | about 7.7 |
| Share of leads who book (assumption) | 40% |
| New customers per month | about 3 |
| Value of a new customer over 6 months (assumption) | $250 |
| Revenue from the test month | about $770 |

On these assumptions the test returns about $770 on $304, before staff time and costs. If only 20% of leads book, it returns about $385, still above the spend. If the cost per lead is double the benchmark and only 20% of leads book, it returns about $190 and loses money. The result swings on three inputs: cost per lead, booking rate and customer value. That is why the first month should be a measurement exercise, not a verdict.

Small samples mislead. With roughly 8 leads in a month, one extra lead changes your cost per lead by more than 10%. Google recommends measuring Smart Bidding performance over periods with at least 30 conversions, which a $10 budget may take several months to reach.

## When Is $10 a Day Not Enough?

When the cost per lead is high relative to what a customer is worth. In the table above, a legal or home-improvement click costs two to five times a restaurant click, and one lead costs $91 to $132. At $304 a month you would see about two or three leads, too few to judge the campaign or to let automated bidding learn.

Smart Bidding also needs conversion data. Google's help page says you need conversion tracking enabled and that some strategies rely on a minimum volume of historical conversions, depending on the campaign type. If you cannot track calls or form submissions, even a bigger budget would be spent blind.

If your category is expensive, there are three options: raise the budget, narrow the target to your highest-margin service, or use channels that suit a smaller budget, such as a well-kept Google Business Profile. ## How Do You Set Up a $10 Campaign Properly?

Four settings decide most of the result.

1. **Location.** Google says radius targeting requires a radius of at least 1 km, and that a very small target may show ads only intermittently or not at all. It recommends broad geo targeting ("Presence or interest") as a best practice on Search, and suggests considering "Presence" if you only want to reach users physically in your locations. Target where your customers are, which may not be your address.
2. **Match types.** Google defines broad match as showing on related searches, phrase match as searches that include the meaning of your keyword, and exact match as searches with the same meaning or intent. Exact gives the most control and the least reach. With a small budget and little conversion data, start with phrase and exact.
3. **Negative keywords.** They stop ads showing on searches you do not want. Google notes that negatives do not match close variants, so add singular and plural forms yourself, and warns that too many can shrink your audience. The [negative keywords guide](/blog/google-ads-negative-keywords-stop-wasting-budget) has starter lists.
4. **Conversion tracking.** Track calls, form submissions and bookings before spending. Without it the budget cannot be judged or optimized.

Send each ad group to a page that matches the search. The [landing page best practices](/blog/landing-page-best-practices) cover what to put on it.

## How Can You Stretch a $10 Budget?

Spend it on fewer things, more precisely.

- **One service, one city.** Pick the service with the highest margin and the clearest search intent, rather than ten services at once.
- **Check the Search terms report weekly** and add irrelevant searches as negatives.
- **Run the campaign when you can answer.** If the phone goes unanswered, a click is wasted. Check your own call and form response times before adding budget.
- **Use manual or simple bidding at first.** Google's help page says Smart Bidding works best with broad match because the wide set of searches helps it learn, but it also needs conversion data, which a small account lacks. Move to automated bidding once you have conversions.
- **Wait for data.** A weekly look is useful, but judge results over several weeks, not days.

## When Should You Spend More Than $10 a Day?

When tracked results show profit and the budget is the limit. A practical test:

1. Track leads and bookings for at least a month.
2. Work out revenue per new customer, then the most you can pay for one.
3. If your cost per customer is below that ceiling, raise the budget in steps (for example by 20% to 30%) and watch cost per lead for a few weeks.
4. If cost per lead rises sharply, stop increasing and fix the ads, keywords or landing page first.

If your cost per customer is above the ceiling, more budget will not help. Fix the landing page, the keywords or the offer, or choose another channel.

## FAQ

### Can I really get results with $10 a day?

Sometimes. In a lower-cost category such as restaurants, WordStream by LocaliQ's 2026 benchmark of $2.05 per click implies about 148 clicks from a $304 month. In an expensive category such as legal services it implies about 31. Results depend on your city, offer and landing page.

### How long should I run a $10 a day campaign before deciding?

Long enough to collect meaningful data. Google recommends measuring Smart Bidding over periods with at least 30 conversions. At $10 a day that may take months in some categories, so judge cost per lead and booking rate over several weeks and compare with your break-even.

### Does Google charge exactly $10 each day?

No. Google treats $10 as an average. Daily spend can reach twice the average daily budget, and monthly spend is capped at 30.4 times the daily budget.

### Should I use Smart Bidding on a small budget?

Only with conversion tracking in place. Google says Smart Bidding requires conversion tracking and that some strategies rely on minimum historical conversion data. Many small accounts start with simpler bidding and move over once they have conversions.

### What if my industry is expensive?

Check the benchmark for your category, compute leads per month from $304, and compare with what a customer is worth. If the numbers do not work, raise the budget, narrow the target, or use other channels first.
