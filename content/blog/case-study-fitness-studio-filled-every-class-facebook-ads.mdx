Facebook ads can fill fitness studio classes, but the cost depends on three numbers: monthly spend, cost per booking, and the price of one class. The clearest documented example is a vendor case study of a local service business in Orlando, not a studio. It spent $1,500 a month for 90 days and reported 138 qualified leads and 48 customers. Its figures do not fully reconcile, so the worked budget below should come before any spending decision.

## What did the best documented campaign actually produce?

The vendor, ACT Global Marketing, reports a budget of $1,500 per month, a campaign length of 90 days, 138 qualified leads, 48 converted customers, and $96,000 in revenue, with a stated 12.4x return on ad spend ([ACT Global Marketing](https://actglobalmarketing.com/facebook-ads-for-local-businesses/)).

The numbers invite a check. Three months at $1,500 is $4,500 in total spend. That works out to about $32.61 per qualified lead and $93.75 per customer. Revenue of $96,000 against $4,500 implies about 21x, not 12.4x. A 12.4x result would need roughly $7,742 in spend. The page does not explain the gap, so treat the ROAS as a vendor claim.

This is also a service business, not a studio. A one-off service job carries a different customer value than a class-based membership, so the revenue figure cannot be copied directly.

## How do you turn ad spend into a class-fill budget?

Start with the one number a studio controls: class price. A boutique studio class ranges from $20 to more than $30 ([Secondmeasure](https://secondmeasure.com/?p=364)). The table uses $25 as an assumption inside that range.

Bookings needed to cover spend equal monthly spend divided by class price. This counts only the first class and ignores repeat attendance.

| Monthly ad spend (assumption) | Bookings to cover spend at $25 (assumption) | Cost per booking at 40 bookings (assumption) |
|---|---|---|
| $500 | 20 | $12.50 |
| $1,000 | 40 | $25.00 |
| $1,500 | 60 | $37.50 |

At $1,000 a month, the campaign only pays back on first classes if 40 people book at $25 each. Most studios earn more from repeat attendance, but this check keeps the budget honest. Cost per click alone is a weak guide. ARC's salon case reports Facebook and Instagram cost per click of $1.19 to $1.37 for a 1 to 2 mile radius ([ARC](https://arccreativeco.com/what-a-salon-booking-costs-on-google-ads/)). A studio paying an assumed $1.30 per click still needs enough bookings per click to beat its breakeven count.

## Which targeting and offers did the sources test?

Vertical Impression recommends cold-audience Facebook ads targeting demographics within 3 to 5 km of the studio ([Vertical Impression](https://www.verticalimpression.com/guides/how-to-market-health-wellness-business-2026)). A tight radius suits a studio, since members usually come from a short drive. Interests and age can narrow the audience after the radius is set.

Offers are where studios differ. A static ad for IM=X Pilates and Fitness in Lafayette offered a first class for $18 ([Sprites](https://www.sprites.ai/gym-static-ads)). TruFusion's "First Barre Class Free" static ad has run 648 days on the same directory. That suggests the offer held up, but running time is not proof of results.

Simple creative can also outperform. One case cited by HeyOz found that simple static ads built for an underserved persona became three of the top four performing ads in the account ([HeyOz](https://heyoz.com/blogs/high-performing-ai-facebook-ad-examples-that-drive-sales), secondary). Test one audience and one offer at a time. The [guide to the best Facebook ad formats for local businesses](/blog/best-facebook-ad-formats-local-business-2026) covers which formats suit small budgets.

## How much of the budget should go to retargeting?

Retargeting can lift results, but the upside needs care. A Google case study cited by Outbrain reports that retargeting can produce a conversion uplift of more than 160% in some cases ([Outbrain](https://www.outbrain.com/blog/advanced-retargeting-tips/), secondary). Treat that as a best case, not an expected return.

Set a retargeting share as an assumption. For example, 20% of a $1,000 monthly budget is $200. Then check whether retargeted visitors book at a lower cost than cold traffic. If they do not, the offer or the audience window needs changing.

Keep the window short and the offer specific, such as a fixed first-class price rather than a generic "book now." Retargeting spend should be judged on bookings, the same as cold spend.

## Why do bookings leak after someone clicks?

Ad spend can be wasted after the click. Mariana Tek advises sending an SMS reminder a few hours before class to reduce no-shows ([Mariana Tek](https://www.marianatek.com/blog/group-fitness-studio-marketing-strategies/)). A reminder costs little compared with a paid spot that goes empty.

The booking path matters too. Instagram and Facebook let businesses add a Book Now button to their profile ([Appointy](https://blog.appointy.com/2020/12/11/social-media-marketing-for-fitness-studio-gym/)). An ad should end on a working booking link, not a general homepage.

Off-peak time is another gap. In the Wellhub survey, only 20% of owners had a developed strategy to monetize unused off-peak capacity ([Health & Fitness Association](https://www.healthandfitness.org/86-of-gym-and-studio-owners-grew-membership-this-year-even-as-costs-and-competition-rise/)). Paid campaigns can target slow time slots, not only peak classes. The [fitness studio social media strategy guide](/blog/fitness-studio-social-media-marketing-strategy) covers how these channels fit together.

## What should you check before you scale spend?

Work through these steps before raising the budget:

1. Confirm your class price and the bookings it implies, using the breakeven table above.
2. Set a 3 to 5 km radius first, then test interests in a separate ad set.
3. Run one offer against one audience for at least a full class cycle.
4. Track bookings, not clicks, and compare cost per booking with your breakeven count.
5. Confirm the reminder and Book Now link work before adding spend.
6. Increase budget only when cost per booking holds across several weeks.

## FAQ

### What does a Facebook ad campaign cost for a fitness studio?

The documented vendor case spent $1,500 a month for 90 days, but it covered a local service business in Orlando, so the sources give no direct studio benchmark. Set your own budget by dividing monthly spend by your class price. That gives the bookings you need just to cover the spend.

### Is the reported 12.4x return on ad spend reliable?

Not on its own. The reported revenue and spend imply about 21x, so the figures do not reconcile. Measure bookings and revenue from your own booking system before relying on any ROAS figure.

### Should a small studio target by radius or by interests?

Start with a radius of 3 to 5 km around the studio, as one vendor guide recommends. Then test interests in a separate ad set so you can see which one drives bookings.

### Can a small studio test Facebook ads without a large budget?

Yes. Use the $500 row of the table as a starting point, test one audience and one offer, and track cost per booking against your breakeven count before increasing spend.
