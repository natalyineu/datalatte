LocaliQ's 2026 benchmarks put the average click on a Meta traffic ad for a Restaurants & Food business at $0.45. At that price, $300 buys roughly 667 clicks. Cheap clicks are not the problem for a coffee shop. The problem is sending them at the wrong hour. Your 8am rush does not need an ad, and your 2pm lull does. This guide is about timing: finding your slow hours, writing an offer that only works then, and working out whether the extra visits pay for the ad.

It is written for café owners in the US, UK, Australia and Canada. Benchmarks are US-based; use your own prices in pounds or other dollars. For the account setup itself (objectives, tracking, placements), use our [complete Meta setup guide for coffee shops](/blog/meta-ads-for-coffee-shops-complete-guide), then come back here to decide when and for whom to run ads.

## Who You Can Actually Reach

Pew Research's [2025 social media fact sheet](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/fact-sheet/social-media/), a survey of 5,022 US adults fielded February to June 2025, gives usage by age:

| Age group | Use Facebook | Use Instagram |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 18 to 29 | 68% | 80% |
| 30 to 49 | 80% | 62% |
| 50 to 64 | 74% | 40% |
| 65 and over | 57% | 19% |

The practical reading: if your slow-hour customers are students and young workers, Instagram is where most of them are. If they are parents, remote workers in their 30s and 40s, or retirees, Facebook reaches more of them, and in the 50-plus groups it is the clear leader. The same ad can run on both through automatic placements, but the creative should suit whoever fills your gap. These are US figures; check your own audience estimates in Ads Manager for the UK, Australia and Canada.

## Remember What You Are Competing With

The National Coffee Association's [Spring 2025 data trends release](https://ncausa.org/Newsroom/More-Americans-Drink-Coffee-Each-Day-Than-Any-Other-Beverage-Bottled-Water-Back-in-Second-Place) (survey fielded January 2025 by Dig Insights) reports that 66% of US adults drink coffee daily and 46% had specialty coffee in the past day. It also reports that 71% of past-day drinkers had coffee prepared only at home, 16% only out of home and 13% both.

That last split is the useful one for advertisers. Most coffee in the US is made at home, so your ad is not competing with the café next door as much as with the kettle. A weak offer ("Visit us!") gives nobody a reason to leave the house. A specific one ("2 to 5pm: any pastry free with a large drink") does.

## Step 1: Find Your Slow Hours

Do this before spending anything. Pull sales by hour from your till or point-of-sale system for the last four to eight weeks and average each day. You want the hours where staff are standing around and seats are empty. Write down:

- Your two or three weakest blocks (for many cafés, mid-afternoon and Sunday evening, but check your own data).
- How many extra drinks per hour you could serve without adding staff.
- Your average spend per visit and your margin on it.

That last number decides how much an extra visit is worth. If you do not know your margin, our [local marketing budget guide](/blog/local-marketing-budget-guide) walks through the basics.

## Step 2: Write One Offer Per Gap

An offer for a slow hour has three parts: a reason to come, a time window, and a limit. Examples:

| Gap | Offer idea | Who it suits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Weekday 2 to 5pm | Free pastry with any large drink, 2 to 5pm | Remote workers, parents after school, students |
| Weekday 9 to 11am (after the rush) | Bring a colleague, second coffee half price | Office workers on a meeting break |
| Sunday evening | Free refill on a filter coffee after 4pm | Students, people planning the week |
| Early morning in winter | Free upgrade to a large before 8am | Commuters, shift workers |

Put the time window in the headline and on the landing page. The window itself does part of the scheduling for you: nobody can use a 2 to 5pm offer at 8am, so you do not have to rely on scheduling tools. Keep the offer costing you pennies relative to a visit, and say plainly who can claim it and how.

## Step 3: Decide How to Time the Spend

Two budget types behave differently, and Meta's own developer documentation on [budgets](https://developers.facebook.com/documentation/ads-commerce/marketing-api/bidding/overview/budgets.md) explains the first. A daily budget is "the average amount you're willing to spend" each day, and on strong days up to 25% more than the daily budget may be spent (its example is $12.50 on a $10 budget). A lifetime budget is the amount you will spend over the whole run, and Meta paces it over the period, with spend varying by day.

On hour-by-hour scheduling, a September 2025 guide from the tool vendor [Madgicx](https://madgicx.com/blog/meta-campaign-scheduling) states that Meta ad scheduling works only with lifetime budgets, not daily budgets. That is second-hand and not confirmed against Meta's own Help Center, so check the options in your Ads Manager. If you do use a schedule, run ads in the hours before the gap (people decide while the gap is coming) rather than during the rush.

For most single cafés, a simpler plan works: one daily budget, an offer that is time-limited by its own wording, and a weekly look at the numbers. Do not make large edits in the first few days; judge by redemptions, not impressions.

## Step 4: Match the Creative to the Daypart

Shoot three 8 to 12 second vertical clips on your phone, one per gap:

1. **The afternoon one.** Natural light through the window, the pastry case, a person settling in with a laptop. Text on screen in the first three seconds: "2 to 5pm: free pastry with a large drink".
2. **The meeting-break one.** Two cups on a table, the barista handing them over. "Bring a colleague. Second coffee half price, 9 to 11am."
3. **The weekend one.** Sunday-evening quiet, a filter coffee being poured.

Use real staff and your real space. Add your street name in the copy, since the ad is for people within walking distance. Avoid claims you cannot back up, such as "best coffee in town", unless a published source says so.

## Step 5: A Seasonal Calendar That Moves the Gaps

Slow hours shift through the year, so plan four to six short pushes instead of one always-on campaign:

- **Late summer, back to school and campus return.** Student and parent routines change; a "study hours" offer fits an afternoon gap.
- **October 1, International Coffee Day.** According to Wikipedia's [entry on the day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Coffee_Day), the International Coffee Organization set the date. It is a low-effort hook for a one-week offer each autumn; check the date for your own country before you plan around it.
- **Winter.** Early dark and cold weather change when people go out; a warm-up offer fits late afternoon.
- **Your own events.** A new seasonal drink, a latte-art night or a loyalty-card launch.

Pick dates from your own calendar of slow weeks, not from a generic marketing list. If a week is already busy, skip it.

## Step 6: Measure Against a Baseline

A redemption is not the same as a new sale, because some people would have come in anyway. A rough test that needs no pixel:

1. Record average sales for the gap hours over the four weeks before the ads.
2. Run the offer for four weeks.
3. Compare the gap-hour sales and the number of redemptions. The increase over the baseline is your best estimate of extra visits.

Also log redemptions by phrase or ad (staff tally, a QR code, a stamp on a loyalty card). Cost per redemption is the headline metric; clicks are only a diagnostic. This is a rough method, not a controlled experiment. A slow week caused by weather will make the ads look better or worse than they are.

## The Math

Take a $300 monthly budget aimed at one afternoon gap. Every row is an **assumption** for illustration, except that the Strong column uses the $0.45 LocaliQ Restaurants & Food click. Replace the numbers with yours after four weeks.

| | Tough | Base | Strong |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Monthly budget (assumption) | $300 | $300 | $300 |
| Cost per click (assumption) | $0.90 | $0.60 | $0.45 |
| Clicks | 333 | 500 | 667 |
| Click to redemption rate (assumption) | 1.5% | 3% | 5% |
| Redemptions | 5 | 15 | 33 |
| Share who would not have come anyway (assumption) | 60% | 70% | 80% |
| Extra customers | 3 | 10.5 | 27 |
| Cost of free item per redemption (assumption) | $1.20 | $1.20 | $1.20 |
| Margin per visit: $8 basket at 65% (assumption) | $5.20 | $5.20 | $5.20 |
| Margin from the first extra visit | $16 | $55 | $139 |
| Net after first visit (ad plus offer cost) | -$290 | -$263 | -$201 |
| Total visits per extra customer to break even | 19.6 | 5.8 | 2.5 |

How to read it: the first visit rarely pays for the ad, so coffee ads work only if the customer returns. The Base case needs about six visits per extra customer, which is realistic for someone who adopts your café for the afternoon, and the Tough case needs about twenty, which is not. Margins here are revenue minus cost of goods, before wages and rent.

Two conclusions:

- **Your break-even click price** = (margin per visit x expected visits - cost of the free item) x click-to-redemption rate x share who would not have come anyway. For Base behaviour over six visits: ($5.20 x 6 - $1.20) x 0.03 x 0.70 = $0.63. Paying more per click than that loses money on average.
- **Repeat visits are the lever.** A loyalty card and an email address on visit one change the answer far more than shaving 10 cents off a click. See our guide to [email marketing for coffee shops](/blog/ai-assisted-email-marketing-for-coffee-shops).

<Callout type="coffee" title="Stop When the Gap Fills">
If your 2 to 5pm block is busy three weeks in a row, retire the offer. Paying to fill a seat that would be filled anyway is the fastest way to turn a profitable test into a loss.
</Callout>

## FAQ

**Q: How much do Facebook and Instagram ads cost for a coffee shop?**
A: LocaliQ's 2026 benchmarks list $0.45 per click for Restaurants & Food traffic campaigns and $0.60 across all industries. At $0.45, $300 buys about 667 clicks; your own price depends on your radius and season.

**Q: Should I advertise during my busiest hours?**
A: Usually not. If the shop is already full, an ad adds cost without adding sales. Aim spend at the hours with empty seats.

**Q: Facebook or Instagram?**
A: Pew's 2025 survey found Instagram use at 80% among ages 18 to 29 and Facebook at 80% among ages 30 to 49 in the US. Run automatic placements first, then check the breakdown.

**Q: Do I need the Meta pixel?**
A: Not for a first offer test. A unique phrase, a QR code or a loyalty stamp is enough. A pixel matters once you take online orders.

**Q: How long before I know if it works?**
A: Run at least four weeks, my rule of thumb, so you can compare against a baseline and see repeat visits.
