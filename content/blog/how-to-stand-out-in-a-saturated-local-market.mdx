BrightLocal's 2026 survey found that 47% of consumers will not use a local business with fewer than 20 reviews, and 68% want at least four stars. In a crowded market, you are not just competing on service. You are competing in a filter, and most customers apply the filter before they ever read your tagline.

That changes what "standing out" means. It is not a cleverer slogan. It is two jobs: be obviously right for one kind of customer, and have visible proof that you are. This guide is for owners of cafés, salons, groomers, studios and similar local shops in the US, UK, Australia and Canada. Review figures below come from a US survey, so treat the exact percentages as a guide for your market.

## What Customers Check Before They Choose

BrightLocal's [Local Consumer Review Survey 2026](https://www.brightlocal.com/research/local-consumer-review-survey/) asked 1,002 US adults and published in February 2026. Its headline numbers:

The same survey says 31% will only use a business rated 4.5 stars or higher, up from 17% the year before, and 32% want reviews from the last two weeks. On replies, it reports that 80% are more likely to use a business that responds to all its reviews, while 50% are put off by templated replies.

Google describes its own side of this. In its [help page on improving local ranking](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091), Google says local results are mainly based on relevance, distance and prominence. It says more reviews and positive ratings can help your local ranking, and that there is no way to pay for a better one. You cannot change your distance from the customer. You can change relevance (how clearly your profile matches what they typed) and prominence (how well known and well reviewed you are).

So the plan has three parts: say something specific so you are relevant, collect proof so you are prominent, and make sure the customer's first look matches the promise.

## Step 1: Audit Five Competitors in One Hour

Search your main service plus your town, as a customer would, and open the top five or six results. For each, write down:

- Rating and review count, and the date of the most recent review
- First line of their Google description and their website headline
- Main service and any published prices
- Whether their photos show real people and premises or stock images
- What they never mention

The last item is where gaps hide. If nobody mentions parking, same-day slots, allergy-friendly options, evening hours or a specific customer group, you may have an opening, provided it is true for your business. Our [competitor analysis guide](/blog/competitor-analysis-local-business) has a template for this.

## Step 2: Pick the One Difference That Is True and Hard to Copy

A useful difference is true, matters to a specific customer and cannot be copied overnight.

| Type | Example angle | Hard to copy? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Audience | New mums, runners, seniors, anxious pets | Moderately |
| Specialty | Balayage, espresso training, puppy first groom | Yes, builds over time |
| Convenience | Late hours, mobile service, online booking | Easy to copy |
| Experience | Small classes, quiet room, personal attention | Moderately |
| Values | Local sourcing, community work | Only if genuine |

Price is the weakest option, because someone cheaper can always appear. (That is my view, not a research finding.) Ask your five best customers two questions: why did you pick us the first time, and why do you come back? Their words are better than yours.

Then complete this sentence: "We help [specific customer] get [specific result] by [what we do differently]." If you cannot finish it in one breath, you are not specific enough yet. Examples: "Cat-only grooming in a quiet room", "6 a.m. classes for shift workers", "Cuts for kids without the wait".

## Step 3: Make Your Google Profile Carry It

For most local businesses the Business Profile is the shop window. Make it carry your angle:

- **Primary category.** Google's [business information guidelines](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177) say to use as few categories as possible and the most specific ones that describe your core business. They also say not to use categories as keywords. Our [categories guide](/blog/google-business-profile-categories-guide) shows how to choose.
- **Business name.** Use your real-world name. Google says not to add taglines, service descriptions or location details to it, and that unnecessary information can lead to suspension.
- **Description.** Google says it should explain your services or products, mission and history, honestly, and should not focus on promotions or prices and must not contain links. Put your specific angle in the first sentence, because that is what people see first.
- **Photos.** Real photos of your team, space and work beat stock images. Our [photo guide](/blog/how-to-optimize-google-business-profile-photos) covers what to shoot.

Work through the [full profile checklist](/blog/google-business-profile-optimization-checklist), or see our [Google Business Profile service](/services/google-business-profile) if you want it done for you.

## Step 4: Turn Reviews Into Both Proof and Positioning

Reviews do two jobs here. They pass the 20-review, 4-star filter, and they tell you which words customers use for your difference.

**Ask correctly.** Google's [review help page](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3474122) says you can ask customers to leave reviews through a Google link or QR code, and that customers must be signed into a Google Account. It also calls offering free or discounted goods or services in exchange for reviews "strictly prohibited". So no discounts for reviews.

**Ask at the happiest moment.** Hand over the QR code at the till, or send the link in the thank-you text an hour after the visit. Our [review guide](/blog/how-to-get-more-5-star-google-reviews-local-business) has scripts.

**Mine the words.** Read your last 30 reviews. If "so patient with my nervous dog" appears five times, that phrase belongs in your website headline and profile description.

**Reply to every review, specifically.** BrightLocal's numbers on replies (80% more likely to use a business that replies to all) and on templates (50% put off) point the same way: write short, personal replies that mention something from the review.

## Step 5: Make the First Screen of Your Website Repeat the Promise

The visitor arriving from Google has just seen your profile. If your site opens with "Welcome to our family-run business", the promise breaks. Put the same sentence from Step 2 in the headline, add one real photo, your location and one action ("Book", "Call"). Check it on a phone. Our [above-the-fold guide](/blog/above-the-fold-design-local-business) covers the layout.

Then write the pages customers actually search for: prices, what to expect on a first visit, how to choose between options. Our [content marketing guide](/blog/content-marketing-local-businesses-what-actually-works) explains how to pick topics.

## The Math

What is a better review rate worth? This table uses **assumptions** throughout. Swap in your own figures.

| | Now | After 90 days of asking |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Customers per month (assumption) | 500 | 500 |
| Share who leave a Google review (assumption) | 1% | 4% |
| New reviews per month | 5 | 20 |
| Reviews gained over 3 months | 15 | 60 |
| Profile starts at (assumption) | 12 reviews | 12 reviews |
| Reviews after 3 months | 27 | 72 |
| Clears BrightLocal's 20-review filter? | Month 2 | Month 1 |
| Monthly profile views (assumption) | 1,000 | 1,000 |
| Share who call, book or get directions (assumption) | 4% | 5% |
| Actions per month | 40 | 50 |
| Value per action (assumption: half become customers worth $60) | $30 | $30 |
| Extra value per month | | +$300 |

How to read it: the review rate is the lever with the most room, because most happy customers never think to leave one. The profile-action rate and value figures are guesses used only to show how to price the effort; your Business Profile insights show your real action counts. One extra action per day at $30 is $900 a month, so the reasoning is worth doing with your own numbers. If you ask everyone and still get under 2%, the problem is probably the ask (no link, wrong moment), not your customers.

## Do This Today

1. Open Google Maps, search your service plus town, and fill the competitor list from Step 1 for the first five results.
2. Interview or message your five best customers: why did you choose us, why do you come back? Write the answers verbatim.
3. Write your one sentence from Step 2 and put it at the top of your Google description and website headline.
4. Create your review link and QR code, print the QR code for the counter, and add the link to your thank-you message.
5. Read your last 30 reviews, reply to any unanswered ones with something specific, then block 15 minutes every Friday to reply to new ones.

## FAQ

**Q: What if my business really is the same as everyone else's?**
A: The core service often is. Differentiate on who you serve, how easy you are to book, how you communicate and what customers say about you. Those are all things you can improve.

**Q: Is it risky to niche down?**
A: It feels risky, but a clear focus tends to bring better-matched customers and sharper reviews. That is my experience of local markets rather than a measured result. You can still serve other customers who walk in.

**Q: Should I compete on price?**
A: Not as your main strategy. Intro offers for first-timers are fine. A permanent low-price position is hard to defend because someone can always undercut you.

**Q: How many reviews do I need?**
A: BrightLocal reports 47% of US consumers will not use a business with fewer than 20 reviews, so 20 is a sensible first target. Recency matters too, since 74% look at reviews from the last three months.

**Q: How long before repositioning shows up in the numbers?**
A: Profile and website changes can move calls within weeks. Reviews and ranking build over months. Check your Business Profile insights monthly.
