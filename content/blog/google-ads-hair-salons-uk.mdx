## What Does Google Ads Cost for a UK Hair Salon?

No primary source reviewed publishes an audited UK cost per click for hair salons. The nearest benchmark is a US compilation from WordStream, which lists an average search cost per click of $2.69 across all industries and $6.40 for consumer services ([WordStream](https://www.wordstream.com/blog/ws/2016/02/29/google-adwords-industry-benchmarks)). That page is a vendor summary of US averages, so it does not describe a UK town.

A salon's real cost depends on its keywords, its local competition and its ad quality. Two salons in the same city can pay very different prices for the same search. The only reliable figure is the one Google shows for your own keywords and location, which is why the next section matters.

Agency estimates from the UK exist, but the sources reviewed did not verify them, so they are not used in this guide. Any figure from them should be checked against Keyword Planner before it goes into a budget.

## How Do You Get a Real Cost for Your Town?

Start in Google's Keyword Planner. Google's help documentation describes it as a tool for finding relevant keywords and estimating how they perform. Set the location to your town or postcode area rather than the whole country, because a national average can overstate or understate what a local salon pays.

Pick the five highest-value services, such as colour, bridal hair, extensions, men's cuts and treatments. Record the estimated cost range for each one. Those numbers, not a published league table, should drive the budget.

Keep a simple spreadsheet with one row per keyword. Note the monthly search volume, the cost range, and whether the search names a service or a place. That sheet becomes the input to the budget calculation in the worked example below. Reviewing it monthly shows which services earn their place in the account.

## Which Keywords Should a Salon Start With?

Choose services where a single booking is worth the most. Bridal and occasion hair, colour and balayage, and extensions usually carry higher spend per client than a basic trim, so they often justify a higher cost per click. Put the town or area in the keyword so the ad matches local intent.

Keep the list short. A test with five to ten keywords is easier to read than a large account with dozens of loosely related terms. The [mistakes guide for hair salons](/blog/google-ads-mistakes-hair-salons-make-and-how-to-fix-them) covers the most common ways broad lists waste budget. Salons that also rely on local listings can compare the paid side with the [local SEO guide for hair salons](/blog/hair-salon-local-seo-guide).

Negative keywords stop ads showing for searches you do not want, such as jobs, courses or training. Review the search terms report every week during the first month and add negatives as they appear. This is the cheapest way to protect a small budget.

## How Does Quality Score Affect What You Pay?

Google describes Quality Score as a diagnostic tool on a scale from 1 to 10, available at the keyword level. A higher score means the ad and landing page are more relevant and useful to someone searching for that keyword than other advertisers' ads and pages ([Google Ads Help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/6167118)).

It is built from three components: expected click-through rate, ad relevance and landing page experience. Each is rated "Above average," "Average" or "Below average." An "Average" or "Below average" rating points to something specific to fix, such as a generic headline or a booking page that does not mention the service.

Google also states that Quality Score is not a key performance indicator and is not an input in the ad auction. Do not chase the number for its own sake. Use it to find which keyword and which page need work, then judge the campaign on bookings. For a fuller view of how paid search fits the wider budget, the [complete Google Ads guide for hair salons](/blog/google-ads-for-hair-salons-complete-guide) sets out the setup steps.

## What Should You Track?

Set the booking as the goal. Track phone calls, online booking clicks and form submissions, and ask new clients how they heard about the salon. A campaign that produces clicks but no bookings is not working, however good the click numbers look.

Google's reporting can show calls and clicks, but the count only means something if the call length threshold matches a real enquiry. A two-second accidental call is not a client. Set the threshold to match what a genuine booking conversation takes, then check the figure against the diary each month.

Compare the cost per new client with the value of a first visit. If a first visit is worth £70 and the cost per new client is £90, the campaign loses money before repeat visits are counted. Track rebooking as well, since repeat visits decide whether paid clicks pay off over time.

## What Does a Budget Buy? A Worked Example

Every input in this example is an **assumption**. The cost per click, conversion rate, booking rate and first-visit value are planning figures for illustration, not UK benchmarks. Replace them with your own Keyword Planner estimates and diary data before making a decision.

| Input | Assumption |
| --- | --- |
| Monthly budget | £300 |
| Cost per click | £2.50 |
| Clicks (£300 ÷ £2.50) | 120 |
| Conversion rate (call or booking) | 8% |
| Enquiries (120 × 8%) | 9.6 |
| Enquiries that become a booking | 60% |
| New clients (9.6 × 60%) | 5.8 |
| Cost per new client (£300 ÷ 5.8) | about £52 |
| Average first visit value | £70 |

At these inputs, a first visit covers the cost per new client with some margin. Now raise the cost per click to £4.00 and keep everything else the same. Clicks fall to 75, enquiries to 6, and new clients to about 3.6. The cost per new client rises to about £83, which is more than the assumed first visit.

The lesson is that cost per click is only one input. A keyword that costs more per click but converts well can outperform a cheap keyword that attracts idle searchers. Note that the WordStream figure of $2.69 is a US average and is not an input to this table.

## FAQ

### Is there an official UK cost per click for hair salons?
Not in the sources reviewed. Use Keyword Planner with your own town as the location to get the range for your keywords.

### Can a UK salon run Google Ads on a small budget?
Yes, if the keyword list is short and the booking goal is clear. The worked example uses an assumed £300 a month to show the arithmetic.

### What does a Quality Score of 1 to 10 tell me?
It rates keyword-level ad relevance and landing page quality on a 1 to 10 scale. It is a diagnostic, not a direct cost input, so use it to find weak keywords and pages.

### Should a salon use Display ads?
Google describes Display campaigns as reaching people across 35 million websites and apps, including YouTube and Gmail ([Google Ads Help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/2404190)). For most salons, Search is the sensible first step, because it targets people already looking for a service.
