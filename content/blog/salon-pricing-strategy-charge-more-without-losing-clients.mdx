The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics tracks "haircuts and other personal care services" as part of the Consumer Price Index. Between August 2019 and August 2026 that index rose 37.6%, while the index for all items rose 30.6%. If your menu has barely moved since 2019, your real price has fallen behind your costs. The useful question is not "will clients leave?" but "how many clients can I lose before a raise stops paying?" The answer is usually a smaller number than owners fear, and it is simple arithmetic.

This guide is for salon and barbershop owners and independent stylists in the US, UK, Australia and Canada. The index data is American, so use it as a method rather than a UK, Australian or Canadian benchmark. Dollar examples are illustrations with labelled assumptions, not market prices.

Two points from these sources:

- **BLS index:** "Haircuts and other personal care services" (index base 1982-84 = 100) went from 166.2 in August 2019 to 228.7 in August 2026. Between August 2025 and August 2026 it rose 4.2%, against 3.4% for all items. It is a national average and covers other personal care services too, so it is a guide, not your local market.
- **Kline + Co. salon data:** Kline, a market research firm, reported that U.S. salon revenue in the first quarter of 2026 was $8.5 billion, down 6.9% year over year. Transactions fell 7.9%, while the average service price rose only 1.3%, below U.S. inflation of roughly 3%. Clients are stretching the time between visits. This shows prices lagging costs while visits fall. It does not show that price rises cause fewer visits, and it does not prove any particular client would accept a given rise.

Square's 2025 Future of Beauty report, as summarised in a syndicated article written for Square, says 71% of beauty business owners plan to increase prices that year. The survey covered 2,000 owners and managers across the US, Canada, the UK and Australia. That is a self-reported intention, but it suggests you are not the only one adjusting.

## Step 1: Find Your Cost-Based Floor

Before you think about what clients will tolerate, work out what your price would be if it had only tracked the index. Take the price of one core service on the date you last raised it, and multiply by the ratio of today's index to the index then.

The table uses an assumed $80 service price. Replace it with your own.

| Last price rise | Index then (Aug) | Multiplier to Aug 2026 | $80 service becomes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| August 2021 | 184.2 | 1.24 | $99 |
| August 2022 | 192.4 | 1.19 | $95 |
| August 2023 | 202.1 | 1.13 | $91 |
| August 2024 | 211.6 | 1.08 | $86 |
| August 2025 | 219.4 | 1.04 | $83 |

If the number is far above your current price, a staged rise is reasonable. If you raised recently, you may only need a small adjustment. Your own costs matter more than any index: rent, product, software and wages. BLS reports a median wage of $17.25 an hour for barbers, hairstylists and cosmetologists combined (May 2025), including tips. About 48% of hairdressers, hairstylists and cosmetologists and about 80% of barbers are self-employed. That matters because many of you pay chair rent or carry fixed costs yourselves: when they rise, your price has to follow.

## Step 2: Decide What to Raise

Not every service needs the same rise. These are recommendations, not rules:

- **Raise the services with a waiting list first.** If a service is booked out three weeks ahead, demand is exceeding supply.
- **Raise time-heavy services more than quick ones.** Color, corrections and long appointments use more of your chair time.
- **Keep one or two entry-level services where they are.** Not as a trick, but so a new client has a way in.
- **Fold hidden charges into the menu.** If every colour client pays a "toner" or "consultation" charge anyway, put it in the service price. See the pricing rules below.

A 5% to 10% rise on core services every year is easier on clients than a 25% jump after four years of silence. That is judgement, not a measured rule.

## Step 3: Announce It Like a Business

Tell people early, plainly and in the same channels you use for bookings. A workable sequence:

1. **Four to six weeks before:** Email or text everyone on your client list, and put a note on your booking page.
2. **At the appointment before the change:** Mention it in person, and offer to rebook at the current price if they book within the notice period. (A notice window is a courtesy; you are not obliged to offer one.)
3. **On the day:** Update your online menu, Google Business Profile and social bios the same day.

A simple message you can adapt:

> Hi [name], a quick note from [salon]. From [date], our prices change: [service] goes from [old price] to [new price]. We haven't adjusted our menu since [month, year], and costs for products, rent and staff have gone up. Your appointments already booked will stay at today's price. If you'd like to book your next visit before [date], reply here and we'll hold your usual slot.

Apologising repeatedly, over-explaining or offering a surprise discount to "make up for it" tends to weaken the message. State the date, the new price and the reason in a few lines.

## Pricing Rules to Know

A price rise itself is not regulated in most places. What is regulated is how you show prices.

- **United States.** The FTC's Rule on Unfair or Deceptive Fees, effective May 12, 2025, requires businesses to show the total price including mandatory fees. According to the FTC's own FAQ, it covers only live-event tickets and short-term lodging, so salons are not covered. State laws vary, so check your own state.
- **Canada.** The Competition Bureau says never to promote an unattainable price: advertising a price that customers cannot pay because of mandatory fees is a concern under the Competition Act, except for government-imposed charges such as sales tax.
- **United Kingdom.** According to a Reed Smith summary of the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act, mandatory fees must be included in the price shown from the outset, and drip pricing is banned. That is a law-firm summary; read the CMA's current guidance.
- **Australia.** Check the ACCC's current guidance on price display.

The practical rule across all four: if every client pays a charge, it belongs in the price on your menu and booking page.

## The Math

Break-even for a price rise depends on how many clients you lose. If revenue is price times clients, the share of clients you can lose before revenue falls is the rise divided by one plus the rise.

| Price rise | Break-even client loss (revenue) | Break-even if 10% of price is variable cost (assumption) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5% | 4.8% | 5.3% |
| 8% | 7.4% | 8.2% |
| 10% | 9.1% | 10.0% |
| 15% | 13.0% | 14.3% |

Now an example chair. Every row is an **assumption** for illustration: 25 appointments a week, an average ticket of $90, 48 working weeks, and an 8% rise to $97.20.

| | Lose 0% | Lose 5% | Lose 7.4% (break-even) | Lose 10% |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Appointments per week | 25 | 23.75 | 23.15 | 22.5 |
| Weekly revenue | $2,430 | $2,309 | $2,250 | $2,187 |
| Annual revenue (48 weeks) | $116,640 | $110,808 | $108,000 | $104,976 |
| Change vs. $108,000 today | +$8,640 | +$2,808 | $0 | -$3,024 |
| Chair time freed per week | 0 | 1.25 slots | 1.85 slots | 2.5 slots |

How to read it: at 8%, you can lose about 7 of every 100 clients before revenue falls. The freed slots are not dead time if you have a waitlist or can refill them with new clients. If you are not full, the problem is demand, not price, and a rise alone will not fix it. Revenue is also not profit: stylists on commission, product use and card fees all change the break-even, so run the numbers with your own costs. Track the clients you actually lose for two months before judging.

## FAQ

**Q: How much can I raise prices before clients leave?**
A: No public source gives a reliable figure for salons, and anyone quoting one is guessing. Use the break-even math above: an 8% rise breaks even at about a 7.4% revenue-based client loss. Then measure your own client loss for two months.

**Q: Should I raise prices for existing clients or only new ones?**
A: Most owners raise for everyone on a set date and give a notice period. A two-tier list is harder to explain and to run. That is a recommendation, not a rule.

**Q: What if my competitors are cheaper?**
A: Compare menus on like-for-like services, including the extras they charge for. Their sticker price may leave out items you include. Raise if your waiting list and reviews say demand is strong; otherwise look at your marketing first.

**Q: Should I offer a discount to soften the rise?**
A: A notice period and a chance to rebook at the old price is usually enough. Ongoing discounts erode the rise you just made. A package for regulars or a loyalty perk is a better fit.

**Q: Do I have to give notice of a price rise?**
A: The sources used here do not set a notice period for salon price rises. Advance notice is good practice. Check your local consumer protection rules, and any rules for membership or pre-paid packages, before changing prices on existing bookings.
