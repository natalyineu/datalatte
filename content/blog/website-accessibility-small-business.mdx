Making a small business website accessible means people with visual, hearing, motor or cognitive disabilities can use it, and the fixes also tend to help everyone on a phone, in bright sunlight or in a hurry. Start with a handful of high-impact changes: alt text, readable colour contrast, clear headings, keyboard-friendly navigation, labelled forms and captions.

Accessibility is partly about doing right by customers and partly about practicality. Many accessibility improvements overlap with good usability and good SEO. In some countries, businesses also face legal expectations around accessibility, though the rules vary by place and business type. This guide is practical guidance, not legal advice. If legal compliance is a concern, speak to a qualified professional.

## The standard to aim for

The widely used reference is the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG), published by the W3C. Most organisations aim for Level AA of WCAG 2.1 or 2.2. WCAG is organised around four ideas: content should be perceivable, operable, understandable and robust. You do not need to memorise it. The checklist below covers the issues small business sites most commonly have.

## Quick checklist

| Area | What to check | Effort |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Images | Meaningful alt text on informative images | Low |
| Colour | Text contrast of at least 4.5:1 for normal text | Low |
| Headings | One H1, logical H2 and H3 order | Low |
| Links and buttons | Descriptive text, not "click here" | Low |
| Keyboard | Everything usable without a mouse, visible focus | Medium |
| Forms | Visible labels, clear error messages | Medium |
| Video and audio | Captions and transcripts | Medium |
| Text size and zoom | Readable at 200% zoom, no horizontal scroll on mobile | Medium |
| Motion | No auto-playing carousels or flashing content | Low |
| Documents | Accessible menus and PDFs, or HTML alternatives | Medium |

## Fix 1: Add useful alt text

Alt text is a short description that screen readers announce in place of an image. Describe what matters in context:

- Informative image: "Barista pouring a flat white with leaf latte art"
- Product photo: "Blue ceramic mug, 350ml, with handle"
- Decorative image (background swirls, dividers): leave the alt empty so screen readers skip it
- Images of text: avoid them, or repeat the text in alt text

A photo of your menu should not be the only place your menu exists. Publish it as real text too, so screen readers, search engines and translation tools can read it.

## Fix 2: Check colour contrast

Low-contrast text, such as light grey on white or white on a pale photo, is among the most common problems. WCAG asks for a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text. Free checkers include the WebAIM Contrast Checker and the contrast tools in browser developer tools. Do not rely on colour alone to convey meaning, for example "required fields are in red". Add a symbol or text as well.

## Fix 3: Use headings properly

Screen reader users often navigate by headings, so they should form an outline of the page:

- One H1 for the page title
- H2 for main sections, H3 for subsections beneath them
- Do not skip levels or choose headings only for their visual size

If you use a website builder, use its heading styles rather than bolding text. This also helps search engines understand your pages.

## Fix 4: Write meaningful link and button text

"Click here" and "Read more" mean nothing out of context, and screen reader users can list all links on a page. Use phrases such as "View our spring class timetable" or "Book a grooming appointment". Make buttons and links large enough to tap easily on a phone.

## Fix 5: Make the site keyboard friendly

Some people cannot use a mouse and rely on the Tab, Enter and arrow keys. Test your own site:

1. Put your mouse aside and press Tab repeatedly.
2. Can you reach every link, button, menu and form field?
3. Can you see where you are (a visible focus outline)?
4. Can you open and close menus, pop-ups and booking widgets?
5. Can you escape out of any pop-up?

Do not remove the focus outline in your styles unless you replace it with something just as visible. Cookie banners and chat widgets are common culprits, so test them too.

## Fix 6: Label your forms

Contact, booking and newsletter forms are the point of most small business websites, so they must work for everyone.

- Give each field a visible label, not only placeholder text that disappears.
- Mark required fields in text and not only colour.
- Show clear error messages that say what went wrong and how to fix it.
- Keep forms short.
- Check that CAPTCHA options include an accessible alternative.

## Fix 7: Add captions and transcripts

If you publish video on your site or social channels, add captions. They help people who are deaf or hard of hearing, and also viewers watching without sound. Platforms like YouTube, Instagram and tools like Descript or CapCut can generate automatic captions, but you should review and correct them. For longer audio or video, offer a transcript.

## Fix 8: Make text readable and layout flexible

- Use a legible font at a comfortable size, typically 16px or larger for body text.
- Leave generous line spacing and keep lines to a comfortable length.
- Make sure the page still works when users zoom to 200% or increase the browser's text size.
- Test on a phone, since layout problems often show up there first.
- Avoid auto-playing carousels, sound and flashing content. If used, give users controls to pause.

## Fix 9: Menus, flyers and PDFs

Restaurants, salons and studios often publish menus or timetables as PDFs or images. Scanned PDFs are not readable by screen readers. Offer the same information as web text, or create PDFs from the original document with proper structure and text.

## Test your site

Free and low-cost tools help you find issues, though automated tests catch only a portion of problems:

- **WAVE** (WebAIM) and **Lighthouse** (built into Chrome) for quick audits
- **axe DevTools** browser extension for more detailed results
- **WebAIM Contrast Checker** for colours
- A screen reader such as NVDA (free, Windows), VoiceOver (built into Mac and iPhone) or TalkBack (Android) for a manual check
- Ask a few real users, including people who use assistive technology, to try booking or ordering

Combine automated checks with a manual walk-through of your most important journey, such as finding your hours and booking an appointment.

## A word on accessibility overlays

Some vendors sell widgets claiming to make a site accessible with one line of code. Accessibility professionals generally caution that overlays do not fix underlying problems and may not protect you legally. Fix the issues in your actual site or theme instead.

## Plan the work

You do not need to fix everything in one go. A sensible order is:

1. Run a WAVE or Lighthouse scan on your home page, booking page and contact page.
2. Fix contrast, alt text, headings and link text.
3. Test keyboard navigation and forms.
4. Add captions to videos.
5. Add an accessibility statement saying how to get help if something does not work.
6. Re-check whenever you redesign or add plugins.

## FAQ

**Q: Is website accessibility a legal requirement for small businesses?**
A: It depends on your country, region and business type. Laws such as the ADA in the US, the Equality Act in the UK and the European Accessibility Act in the EU can be relevant. Seek legal advice for your situation.

**Q: Will accessibility improve my SEO?**
A: It can help indirectly. Clear headings, alt text, descriptive links and a fast, well-structured site are good for users and for search engines, though accessibility is not a magic ranking switch.

**Q: Do I need an expensive audit?**
A: Not to begin. Free tools and a manual keyboard test will surface the biggest issues. A professional audit makes sense for complex sites or higher legal risk.

**Q: How do I make my website builder site accessible?**
A: Use the builder's heading and alt text features, choose accessible themes, check contrast, test keyboard navigation and avoid plugins that add inaccessible pop-ups.
