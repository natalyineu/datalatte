If you spend 6 hours a week on marketing and your time is worth $50 an hour, doing it yourself costs about $1,300 a month before you spend a cent on tools or ads. A consultant at $600 a month who takes 4 of those hours off your plate is cheaper than you, if the work is as good. That is the whole "is it worth it?" question in one line, and it has little to do with whether consultants are good or bad. The hours and the hourly value are assumptions, and we will work through them below.

## The Four-Gate Test

Answer four questions in order. If you fail a gate, fix it before paying anyone.

### Gate 1: Do you know what a customer is worth?

You cannot judge a consultant without two numbers: the profit from a typical new customer, and the share of enquiries that become customers. If you do not know them, the first job is not marketing, it is a tracking and bookkeeping fix. Our conversion tracking guide shows how to count calls and forms. A consultant who agrees to start with measurement is a good sign; one who starts by spending your ad budget is not.

### Gate 2: Is marketing actually your bottleneck?

A consultant can bring enquiries. They cannot answer your phone, add capacity or fix a poor product. Look at the last month honestly:

- Do enquiries go unanswered for hours? Fix that first.
- Are you booked solid for weeks? You may need price or capacity changes, not more demand.
- Do people enquire and then vanish? Look at your quote, your pricing page or your first reply.

If demand is the problem (few enquiries, quiet calendar), carry on.

### Gate 3: Does the maths survive a bad month?

Take the fee and divide it by your profit per new customer. A $600 fee at $300 profit per customer means two extra customers a month, every month, caused by the work, just to break even. The table below shows the time-based version. If two extra customers sound unrealistic for your size, a one-off audit or training session is a better fit than a retainer.

### Gate 4: Can you give access and a couple of hours a week?

Consultants need access to your Business Profile, ad account and analytics, plus fast answers about offers, photos and availability. Owner-controlled accounts and a weekly check-in are the minimum. If you want zero involvement, the work will drift. Our [remote consultant guide](/blog/remote-marketing-consultant-local-business-how-it-works) lists what to grant and what to keep.

## What a Consultant Can and Cannot Change

Be wary of anyone who promises a ranking. Google's own help page on improving local ranking says "There's no way to request or pay for a better local ranking on Google", and names three factors: relevance (how well a profile matches the search), distance (how far each business is from the searcher) and prominence (how well-known a business is). A consultant can improve relevance, by fixing categories, services and pages. They can help prominence over time through reviews and links. They cannot move your building closer to the searcher. So "guaranteed number one" is a red flag, and "we will fix the specific gaps and track what changes" is a better pitch.

For paid ads, the same logic holds. A consultant can fix structure, tracking and wasted clicks, which is real value. They cannot change what a click costs in your city, and they will not turn a weak offer into a strong one. That is why Gate 2 matters.

## Cheaper Options to Try Before You Hire

Before paying a retainer, use the free or low-cost advice that exists. None of this is marketing-specialist depth in every case, but it is a sensible second opinion.

- **US:** The Small Business Administration's counselling page lists SCORE as "free counseling and workshops from volunteer business mentors", and Small Business Development Centers as offering one-on-one advising and low-cost training ([SBA](https://www.sba.gov/counseling/)). Mentor expertise varies, so ask for someone with local-marketing experience.
- **England:** The Business Support Service offers free advice by phone, email or webchat on 0800 998 1098, Monday to Friday 9am to 6pm excluding bank holidays ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/business-support-helpline)). Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own helplines on the same page. Local Growth Hubs may offer workshops, but we could not confirm current schemes.
- **New South Wales, Australia:** Service NSW lists a Small Business Advisory program offering "free, independent advice" from experienced advisors ([Service NSW](https://www.service.nsw.gov.au/business/business-connect)). We did not confirm hours or eligibility; check the page. Other Australian states run their own services.
- **Canada:** support varies by province and we did not verify specific programmes; search your provincial small business agency.

Use these for a sanity check on your plan, not to run your ads.

## Consultant, Employee or You: The Cost Per Month

The honest comparison is not "consultant versus free". It is the cost of getting the work done.

For an employee, start with the US Bureau of Labor Statistics. The median pay for market research analysts and marketing specialists was $78,760 a year in May 2025 ([BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/business-and-financial/market-research-analysts.htm)). The BLS Employer Costs for Employee Compensation release for June 2026 reports that wages and salaries made up 70.0% of total compensation in private industry, with benefits the other 30.0% ([BLS ECEC](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/ecec.nr0.htm)). A rough loaded cost is $78,760 divided by 0.70, or about $112,500 a year, around $9,400 a month. That is a back-of-envelope estimate: it applies an industry-wide ratio to a median that covers many industries and roles, and a small business may pay more or less. UK, Australian and Canadian figures will differ.

## The Math

Every row is an **assumption** except the $78,760 median pay and the 70.0% wage share (BLS), which produce the employee estimate. Replace the rest with your own.

Assumptions: you value your time at $50 an hour, you work 4.33 weeks a month, and a consultant costs a flat $600 a month and needs 2 of your hours a week.

| | DIY | Consultant | Employee |
|---|---|---|---|
| Your marketing hours per week (assumption) | 6 | 2 | 1 |
| Your time per month, at 4.33 weeks | 26 h | 8.7 h | 4.3 h |
| Value of your time at $50 an hour | $1,300 | $433 | $217 |
| Fee or salary (assumption / BLS estimate) | $0 | $600 | $9,400 |
| Total monthly cost | $1,300 | $1,033 | $9,617 |

How to read it: the consultant is cheaper than DIY by about $267 a month on these numbers, with no change in results. That only counts if you use the freed hours for something that earns money. If your lost hours would otherwise be spent idle, the saving is fiction. An employee only makes sense when there is enough work for a full week.

Now test the sensitivity. How much time must the consultant save you for a $600 fee to pay for itself?

| Hours saved per week (assumption) | 2 | 4 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Value per month at $50 an hour | $433 | $866 | $1,299 |
| Minus the $600 fee | -$167 | +$266 | +$699 |

Break-even is 600 divided by (50 x 4.33), about 2.8 hours a week. Below that, you are paying for results, not time, and the earlier customer-based maths applies. Combine the two: if the consultant also finds wasted ad spend or fixes tracking, the benefit adds to the time saving, but do not count it until you have a baseline to compare against.

## What "Working" Looks Like at 90 Days

Without inventing benchmarks, here is a reasonable scorecard to agree before starting.

1. **Month 1:** an audit with specific findings, tracking that counts calls and forms, and a written plan with the changes in order of impact.
2. **Month 2:** the first changes live (profile fixes, ad structure, landing page) and a baseline number for cost per enquiry.
3. **Month 3:** a comparison against that baseline, shown in your own accounts, with an honest note on what did not work.

Whatever the consultant reports, you should be able to open the accounts and see the same numbers. Ask for month-to-month or a short first term, with success criteria in writing. If someone insists on a long contract before showing any work, that is a signal to ask more questions.

## Do This Today

1. Write down your profit per new customer and the share of enquiries that become customers. If you do not know them, set up call and form tracking first.
2. Log your marketing hours for one week, then multiply by your honest hourly value. Compare with the break-even of about 2.8 hours a week for a $600 fee.
3. Book one free session with a local advisor (SCORE, SBDC, Business Support Service or your state service) and ask them to review your plan.
4. Ask for two quotes from consultants who work with your type of business. Request a one-off audit as the first step.
5. Agree three success measures in writing before any retainer starts, and make sure you own the accounts.

## FAQ

**Q: Is a marketing consultant worth it for a small local business?**
A: It can be, if the consultant saves you more time or earns you more profit than the fee costs. Use the four gates and the break-even math to check, rather than taking anyone's word for it.

**Q: How much does a consultant cost?**
A: Quotes vary widely and no independent survey exists, which is why this guide uses your fee as a variable. Our [agency guide](/blog/local-business-google-ads-agency-how-to-choose) compares published ranges and explains why they disagree.

**Q: Should I hire an agency, a consultant or do it myself?**
A: Start with the small, defined jobs where expertise matters (tracking, ad setup, profile clean-up) and keep the work that needs your voice. See [agency or DIY](/blog/should-you-hire-a-marketing-agency-or-do-it-yourself).

**Q: Does my consultant need to be local?**
A: Not for most digital work. What matters is experience in your type of business and market, plus access to your accounts and clear reporting.

**Q: How do I know if it is working?**
A: By your own numbers: enquiries, bookings and cost per customer compared with a baseline. Reports that show only impressions or followers do not answer the question.
