Google Ads for a hair salon has no fixed price. You choose a daily or monthly budget and pay each time someone clicks your ad. The figure that matters is cost per booking: what you spend to win one paying appointment. ## How Competitive Is the Market for Salon Clients?

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics counts 670,800 barbers, hairstylists and cosmetologists in 2025 and projects employment to grow 7 percent through 2035, which it describes as much faster than the average for all occupations. [BLS, Barbers, Hairstylists, and Cosmetologists](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/personal-care-and-service/barbers-hairstylists-and-cosmetologists.htm). Growth in the trade means more salons competing for the same searches.

The median pay for the occupation was $17.25 an hour in 2025. The BLS also notes that salon owners handle hiring, supervising, inventory and business records. Those duties sit alongside marketing, so the ad budget has to earn its place. The BLS figures describe the occupation rather than ad performance, so use them as context, not as a cost input.

## What Drives Your Cost Per Click?

Google Ads runs an auction on each search. Google's help pages say Quality Score is calculated from three parts: expected click-through rate, ad relevance and landing page experience. Each is rated "Above average," "Average" or "Below average" against other advertisers showing for the same search over the last 90 days. [Google Ads Help: About Quality Score](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/6167118)

Quality Score is measured on a 1 to 10 scale at the keyword level. Google also states that it is a diagnostic tool, not an input in the ad auction, and that it should not be optimised as a key performance indicator. Use it to find weak spots. If a balayage keyword scores "Below average" on ad relevance, the ad text is probably too generic for that search.

Competition is the other driver. A dense city centre usually has more bidders than a small town, and broad searches attract more mixed intent than service-plus-area searches. Keep the location target tight around where clients actually live and work.

## How Do You Build a First Budget From Bookings?

Work backward from the bookings you want:

1. Set the number of new clients you want per month.
2. Estimate how many clicks produce one booking request, using your own tracking once you have it.
3. Multiply: budget = new clients wanted x clicks per request x cost per click.

Until you have real data, label every input as an assumption. Keyword Planner gives estimated bid ranges for your own area, which is a better starting point than any national average. Test the budget for at least two months, then move money toward the keywords that produce bookings.

## Worked Example: Does One New Client Pay Back?

Every input below is an assumption for illustration. None is a salon result or a market statistic.

Assumptions: $400 a month in ads. Cost per click of $4. 10% of clicks become a booking request. 50% of requests book a first visit. First visit is $90. Margin after product, rent share and stylist pay is 40%. Clients make 4 visits in the first year, one first visit plus three returns.

| Line | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Clicks | $400 / $4 | 100 |
| Booking requests | 100 x 10% | 10 |
| New clients | 10 x 50% | 5 |
| Cost per new client | $400 / 5 | $80 |
| Margin per visit | $90 x 40% | $36 |
| First visit only: total profit | ($36 x 5) - $400 | -$220 |
| Visits needed to break even | $80 / $36 | about 2.2, so 3 in practice |
| Four-visit total: profit | ($36 x 4 x 5) - $400 | +$320 |

Read the first-visit row against the four-visit row. On one visit the example loses $220. Break-even needs more than two visits per client, so the ad only pays if clients come back. Rebooking at the chair is worth as much as the ad itself.

Now test a weaker case. Suppose 3 of the 5 clients make all 4 visits and 2 make only the first. Margin totals $432 plus $72, or $504, against $400 spend: a profit of $104. Change one input at a time in your own version of this table to see which number moves the result most.

## Which Costs Do Salon Owners Miss?

Management fees are the most common surprise. A freelancer or agency may charge a flat monthly fee or a percentage of spend, and the scope varies. Ask what is included: setup, search-term reviews, reporting and landing pages.

Wasted clicks are the second. Searches for cosmetology school, DIY tutorials or wig supplies cost money and never book. Add negative keywords early. The guide on [negative keywords to stop wasting budget](/blog/google-ads-negative-keywords-stop-wasting-budget) walks through a starter list.

Tracking is the third. Without conversion tracking for calls and booking clicks, cost per booking cannot be calculated at all. Set it up before the first dollar is spent.

## When Is Google Ads the Wrong Channel?

Google Ads suits people who are ready to book now. It is a weak choice in several situations:

- The salon is already fully booked. Raising prices, adding a stylist or building referrals comes first.
- The mobile booking flow is slow or confusing. Fix the landing page before paying for traffic.
- The average ticket is small and clients rarely return, so even a modest cost per booking may not pay back.
- The budget cannot run for at least two months, because one month rarely gives enough data.

For discovery and before-and-after content, social ads can suit some salons better. See the guide on [using Meta ads for hair salon marketing](/blog/using-meta-ads-for-hair-salon-marketing).

## FAQ

### How much does Google Ads cost per month for a hair salon?

There is no fixed price. You choose the budget and pay per click. Start with a budget you can test for at least two months, and set it from the number of new clients you want. Management fees are extra if you hire help.

### What should I measure to know whether Google Ads is working?

Track cost per booking, not just clicks or impressions. Divide monthly spend by the number of new clients who booked through the ads. Then check how many of them rebooked, since repeat visits decide whether the spend pays back.

### Is Quality Score the same as my cost per click?

No. Google states that Quality Score is not an input in the ad auction. It is a 1 to 10 diagnostic rating for each keyword, built from expected click-through rate, ad relevance and landing page experience.

### Should I run Google Ads myself or hire someone?

Running it yourself works if you review search terms every week and keep tracking accurate. The most common beginner mistakes are broad keywords with no negative list and no conversion tracking. Hire help when your time is worth more in the salon.
