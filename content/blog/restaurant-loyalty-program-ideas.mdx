A free $12 dessert feels like a $12 gift to the guest and costs you about $3.60 in ingredients, if your food cost on that item is 30%. A "$12 off your bill" reward costs you the full $12. That gap, not the points system or the app, is the first design decision in a restaurant loyalty program. Most advice skips it.

This guide is for independent restaurants and bars with a dining room in the US, UK, Australia and Canada. It does not repeat the cafe punch card playbook; for that, read [coffee shop loyalty without an app](/blog/coffee-shop-loyalty-program-ideas-without-an-app). It covers what is different when the check is $30 to $80, visits are weekly or monthly rather than daily, and your real constraint is empty tables on slow nights.

## What Is Different About a Restaurant

Cafes win on frequency: someone buys a coffee four times a week. A restaurant guest might come twice a month at best. That changes three things.

- **Rewards should be earned on visits or spend, and fast.** If it takes ten visits to earn a reward, a monthly diner needs 10 months. Many will never get there.
- **Margin per item varies widely.** A dessert or starter has a very different food cost from a steak, so the reward you choose matters more than the rate you give it.
- **Capacity is the real cost.** A free appetiser on a Friday night at a full restaurant displaces a paying guest's order. The same reward on a quiet Tuesday costs only the ingredients.

## What the Evidence Does and Does Not Say

Square's loyalty page quotes its own 2022 global data: enrolled customers spend 53% more and visit 40% more often than non-members, and in food and drink, 46% more spend and 57% more frequent visits ([Square Loyalty](https://squareup.com/us/en/software/loyalty)). Treat that carefully. People who sign up for a loyalty program are usually already your most frequent guests, so the numbers describe who joins, not how many extra visits the program created. It is a vendor statistic with no published method.

What matters is the share of member visits that would not have happened without the program. Nobody can tell you your number. The math below shows how low it can be and still pay.

## Seven Loyalty Ideas, Sorted by What They Cost You

### 1. The free-item reward (cheapest per perceived dollar)

Give a starter, dessert or side after a set number of visits. Food cost for a typical restaurant ranges from about 28% to 35% of menu price, according to an Illinois State Board of Education lesson on food cost percentage, which adds that a steakhouse may run 40% ([ISBE](https://www.isbe.net/CTEDocuments/FCS-L700052.pdf)). Your own number comes from your recipes. Choose the item with the lowest cost relative to its price and highest perceived value.

### 2. The off-peak multiplier

Double the points or stamps on your slowest two days. The reward cost is the same, but the visit it earns is far more likely to be incremental because the table would otherwise be empty. Look at your covers by day for the last eight weeks and pick the weakest.

### 3. The "next visit" voucher

Instead of a reward on the third visit, give a voucher on a high spend: "Spend $60 tonight and get a free dessert on your next visit, valid 21 days." It brings the guest back sooner, and a time limit stops vouchers piling up.

### 4. Lapsed-guest message

When a member has not visited for twice their usual gap, send a short message with a small reward. This often works better than a general promotion because you are speaking to people who already like you. A simple spreadsheet or your POS export is enough to find them. See [email marketing for restaurants](/blog/email-marketing-for-restaurants).

### 5. Early access, not discounts

Top members get first pick of tables for a new menu launch, an invitation to a tasting night, or a booking window before the public opens. It costs almost nothing and gives regulars status. It works best for restaurants with limited seats.

### 6. Birthday table

Ask for day and month at sign-up and send a small treat for a party of four or more. The reward is cheap and the visit tends to include other paying guests.

### 7. Direct-order points

If a share of your orders comes through delivery apps, points for ordering through your own channel give guests a reason to skip the platform. This is a separate project with its own math, covered in [loyalty for delivery customers](/blog/restaurant-loyalty-program-delivery-customers).

## Pick the Tool Last

Check whether your POS already has loyalty built in before buying a separate product. Square describes points earned on visits, spend or specific items, redemption in store or online, tiered programmes, and reporting that compares loyalty and non-loyalty customer spend. Prices for loyalty add-ons vary by plan and change; we could not confirm a current Toast Loyalty price, so ask each vendor for an all-in quote including card fees and contract length.

Before you commit, ask:

- Can guests join with just a phone number at the table or till?
- Can you export the list if you leave?
- Can you set an expiry and exclusions?
- Does it let you run the off-peak multiplier and lapsed-guest message above?

**A note on texts.** Joining a points programme is not the same as agreeing to marketing texts. In the US, telemarketing texts to wireless numbers need prior express written consent under 47 CFR 64.1200, and STOP replies must be honoured ([Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/47/64.1200)). In the UK, PECR rules apply. Add a clear tick box and see [SMS marketing best practices](/blog/sms-marketing-best-practices).

## Make It Visible

A programme nobody mentions will not work. Train servers to say one line at the start of the meal, not at payment, when it can still change what guests order: "We have a points card, want me to add your number?" Add a table card with a QR code, a line on your website, and a mention in your [Google Business Profile](/blog/google-my-business-for-restaurants) and [Instagram](/blog/instagram-marketing-for-restaurants).

<Callout type="coffee" title="Count the Dessert">
If guests earn a free dessert on the 6th visit, track how many of those redemptions came with a full-price meal. The reward is cheap; the table it sits at is not. If redemptions cluster on your busiest night, move the reward to the slow ones.
</Callout>

## The Math

Assumptions only, except the 28 to 35% food cost range (ISBE) and the structure. Replace every number with yours.

Setup (assumptions): a free dessert menu-priced at $12 with 30% food cost ($3.60), earned every 6 visits. The average check per guest visit is $35 and contribution margin on an extra visit is 55% (revenue after food and other variable costs). Software and admin cost $50 a month and the programme runs about 50 reward cycles a month, so $1 per cycle.

| | Tough | Base | Strong |
|---|---|---|---|
| Share of member visits that are truly extra (assumption) | 3% | 8% | 15% |
| Extra visits per 6-visit cycle | 0.18 | 0.48 | 0.90 |
| Contribution from extra visits at $19.25 each | $3.47 | $9.24 | $17.33 |
| Cost per cycle: dessert $3.60 plus software $1.00 | $4.60 | $4.60 | $4.60 |
| Net per cycle | -$1.13 | +$4.64 | +$12.73 |
| Net per month at 50 cycles | -$57 | +$232 | +$636 |

How to read it: break-even is $4.60 divided by $115.50 (6 visits x $19.25), about 4%. The programme pays if at least 1 in 25 member visits would not otherwise have happened. A "$12 off" reward instead costs $13 per cycle and needs about 11% extra visits, nearly three times as many. That one choice, free item versus dollars off, decides whether many programmes make money.

The other lever is where visits land. Move the same reward to slow nights and the share of truly extra visits rises, because those tables were empty anyway.

## Do This Today

1. Pull three prices and food costs: your best dessert, starter and side. Choose the reward with the lowest cost relative to menu price.
2. Find your slowest two days in the last 8 weeks of sales and decide the off-peak multiplier.
3. Write the rules on one card: how a reward is earned, expiry, exclusions, and that points do not combine with other offers.
4. Brief servers on a one-line pitch at the start of the meal, and check how phone numbers are captured.
5. After 8 weeks, count how many members visited on slow nights versus before, and compare against the break-even in the table.

## FAQ

**Q: Do small restaurants need a loyalty programme?**
A: Not always. If repeat guests make up a large share of your covers and you have slow nights to fill, a light programme can pay. If you are always full, spend the effort on reviews and reservations instead.

**Q: Should I build an app?**
A: Usually not. A phone-number lookup in your POS or a QR sign-up is enough, and many guests will not install an app for a restaurant they visit twice a month.

**Q: How generous should the reward be?**
A: Pick the reward by cost to you, not menu price. A free item at roughly 30% food cost is cheaper than a $12 discount. Use the table above to check break-even.

**Q: How do I know it is working?**
A: Compare visit frequency of members before and after joining, plus the share of redemptions on slow nights. Do not rely on sign-ups, and do not compare members with non-members, because members were already your regulars.

**Q: Is points-based better than stamps?**
A: Points suit varied checks. Stamps are simpler. Both work if staff mention them. See the [cafe version](/blog/coffee-shop-loyalty-program-ideas-without-an-app) for a paper-card setup.
