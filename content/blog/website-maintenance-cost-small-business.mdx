Picture a salon site whose contact form quietly stopped sending emails in March. Nobody notices until May. If that site normally produces 20 enquiries a month, a quarter of them book, and a first booking is worth $60, six silent weeks cost about $450. Those numbers are assumptions, not data, but the shape is real: the expensive part of website maintenance is rarely the hosting bill. It is the thing that breaks while nobody is looking. A maintenance plan is partly insurance against that, and partly a way to avoid paying for features you do not need.

This guide is for owners of cafés, salons, studios and groomers in the US, UK, Australia and Canada. It covers what maintenance actually involves, what published guides say it costs, which of those costs you can drop, a 15-minute weekly routine, and the math to decide between doing it yourself and paying someone. Dollar figures are US-based; for pounds or Australian and Canadian dollars, use local quotes in the same formulas.

The same page prices the do-it-yourself components: shared hosting $5 to $15 a month, security tools $5 to $50 or more, automated backups $0 to $20 or more, and uptime monitoring $0 to $30 or more. Freelancer hourly rates are listed at $50 to $150 or more.

A second source, [UENI's 2026 maintenance cost article](https://ueni.com/blog/website-maintenance-cost/), cites that GoDaddy guide and agrees that most small business sites land at $50 to $500 a month. UENI sells done-for-you websites, so treat its comparisons as marketing. Its useful contribution is the list of five recurring jobs behind any maintenance bill: hosting with SSL, domain and email, software updates, security and backups, and content edits. When you compare quotes, check which of those five each one covers.

What to take from this:

- If your site is a simple brochure for a local shop, the realistic range is the low end: tens of dollars, not hundreds.
- If you take bookings, sell online or run custom features, you are in the upper bands, because more moving parts means more that can break.
- Published ranges are vendor content. Treat them as ballpark, and ask for a written scope.

## Where the Risk Actually Is

Maintenance sounds abstract until you see where sites fail. Patchstack's [State of WordPress Security in 2026](https://patchstack.com/whitepaper/state-of-wordpress-security-in-2026/) reports 11,334 new vulnerabilities in the WordPress ecosystem in 2025, up 42% on 2024. About 91% were in plugins and 9% in themes, with only 6 in WordPress core. It also reports that 46% had no developer fix at the time of public disclosure, and that for heavily exploited flaws the median time to first exploitation was 5 hours.

For a small business, three practical points follow:

1. **Plugins are the risk, not WordPress itself.** Every plugin you add is something to update and something that can be abandoned. A simple site with five plugins is cheaper to keep safe than one with thirty.
2. **Updates matter more on WordPress than anywhere else.** The WordPress developer docs on [upgrading](https://developer.wordpress.org/advanced-administration/upgrade/upgrading/) state that existing sites receive minor core updates automatically by default, that major releases are not automatic for existing sites, and that plugins and themes update automatically only in special cases such as critical security fixes. In plain terms, someone has to look at your plugins.
3. **Back up before you update.** The same docs recommend a backup of your database and files before upgrading, and checking that the backup is usable.

The security of a hosted builder works differently. [Squarespace's pricing page](https://www.squarespace.com/pricing) states that all plans include managed cloud hosting and free SSL certificates, and [Wix's plans page](https://www.wix.com/plans) lists hosting on every plan. Neither page describes plugin patching, because you do not manage plugins in the same way. That removes one category of work. It does not remove content, forms and accuracy.

## What You Actually Need (and What You Can Skip)

**Essential**
- **HTTPS.** Most hosts include it, and [Let's Encrypt](https://letsencrypt.org/about/) offers certificates at no cost, run by a nonprofit. If a host charges extra for a basic certificate, look elsewhere. Our guide to [SSL and HTTPS](/blog/website-ssl-https-why-it-matters) explains setup.
- **Backups you have tested.** Automated, stored off the server, and restored at least once as a drill.
- **Updates.** Core, theme and plugin updates on WordPress. For builders, check that your integrations (booking, forms, embeds) still work.
- **Uptime monitoring.** Free tools exist. You want an email within minutes if the site is down.
- **A form and booking test.** See below.

**Nice to have**
- Monthly speed check. Our [website speed guide](/blog/website-speed-optimization-small-business) and [Core Web Vitals article](/blog/core-web-vitals-local-business-website) explain what matters.
- Content refresh: opening hours, prices, photos, offers. An expired "Christmas special" on a July homepage looks careless.

**Often skippable at small scale**
- Daily malware scans on top of a good host's protection, if you run few plugins.
- Premium page builders and add-ons you do not use.
- Monthly SEO retainers bundled into a maintenance plan. SEO is a separate decision; see our [local SEO](/services/local-seo) service.

## The 15-Minute Weekly Routine

Do this every Monday and put it in the calendar:

1. Submit your own contact form and booking form. Confirm the email arrives in the inbox, not spam.
2. Open the site on your phone over mobile data. Tap the call button.
3. Check that opening hours, prices and holiday notices are right.
4. Glance at the uptime monitor and the backup log.
5. Look at last week's enquiries in your inbox against your analytics. If forms show 12 submissions and you received 8, something is eating leads.

## The Math

Every row is an **assumption** for illustration, except that the price ranges used sit inside the GoDaddy bands above and the Squarespace plan price matches its pricing page (Core on annual billing, as read there; that page does not clearly label billing periods, so check yours). Your own time is valued at $30 an hour, which is also an assumption.

| | Builder site | WordPress, DIY | WordPress, freelancer care plan |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Platform or hosting per month (assumption) | $29 | $10 | $10 |
| Security tool and backups per month (assumption) | included in plan | $15 | included in plan |
| Care plan per month (assumption) | none | none | $150 |
| Your time per month | 1 hour | 3 hours | 0.5 hour |
| Cost of your time at $30 per hour | $30 | $90 | $15 |
| Total per month | $59 | $115 | $175 |
| Total per year | $708 | $1,380 | $2,100 |

Domain registration is left out of all three; it is a similar cost for each.

Now put the failure on the other side of the ledger. With 20 enquiries a month, a 25% booking rate and a $60 first booking (all assumptions), the site produces about $300 a month in first bookings. A six-week outage costs about $450. A tested weekly routine costs about an hour a month and catches that outage in a week, limiting the loss to roughly $75.

Two things follow:

- **Your break-even for a paid plan.** The care plan costs $60 a month more than DIY in the table ($720 a year). It pays for itself only if it prevents roughly $720 of losses or saves more than the extra time. For a simple brochure site, it often does not. For a booking-driven site that makes $3,000 a month in first bookings, one outage of two weeks costs more than the whole year's plan.
- **The biggest saver is fewer moving parts.** Cutting plugins, ditching a homemade booking widget for a hosted one, and keeping content short lowers every row.

<Callout type="tip" title="Ask for a monthly report">
Any provider you pay should send a short monthly note: what was updated, when the last backup ran, uptime, and anything found. If they cannot produce one, you do not know what you are paying for.
</Callout>

## Do This Today

1. List every plugin, integration and embed on your site. Delete anything you do not use.
2. Check that you have an automated backup and restore it once to a test location, or ask your host or builder how restores work.
3. Submit your contact form and booking form and confirm the emails arrive. Do it again after any change.
4. Set up a free uptime monitor with an email alert to a real inbox.
5. Put the 15-minute Monday routine in your calendar, and write down the monthly total you are paying for hosting, domain, tools and help.

## FAQ

**Q: How much does website maintenance cost per month?**
A: GoDaddy's 2026 guide puts small to medium business sites at $50 to $500 a month, simple blogs at $5 to $75, and e-commerce at $300 to $1,000. A simple local brochure site usually belongs near the bottom of the small-business band. Always get the scope in writing.

**Q: Do Wix or Squarespace need maintenance?**
A: Less. Squarespace states that every plan includes managed cloud hosting and free SSL, and Wix lists hosting on every plan. You still need to keep content accurate, test forms and bookings, and renew your domain.

**Q: What happens if I never update a WordPress site?**
A: WordPress applies minor core updates automatically by default, but plugin and theme updates are mostly your responsibility, and Patchstack reports that 91% of new WordPress vulnerabilities in 2025 were in plugins. An unpatched plugin is the typical way in.

**Q: Is it cheaper to rebuild every few years than to maintain?**
A: Not reliably. Rebuilding costs a lump sum and can reset your rankings if URLs change, so plan redirects. See our guide to [when and why to redesign](/blog/website-redesign-when-and-why).

**Q: Do I need SSL if I do not take payments?**
A: Yes, and it should cost nothing. Let's Encrypt offers certificates at no cost, and Squarespace says it provides free SSL on every plan.
