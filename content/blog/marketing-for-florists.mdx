A florist's year is lumpy. A handful of holidays carry a large share of demand, and the shops that win those weeks are usually the ones that were already visible in the weeks before. This guide is built around that fact: spend your limited time on what a nearby buyer sees in the 10 seconds before they pick a shop.

## Why florists should plan around the calendar

The National Retail Federation's 2026 Mother's Day survey projects a record $38 billion in total spending, with flowers the most popular gift category: 75% of shoppers plan to buy them, worth a projected $3.2 billion (NRF, April 1-8, 2026, 7,877 US adults, margin of error 1.1 points). That is demand you do not have to create. You only have to be the shop that shows up, looks trustworthy and takes the order without friction.

The practical consequence is a sequence: build visibility (profile and reviews) in the quiet months, then switch on paid search a few weeks before a peak, then measure.

## Decision 1: Google Business Profile before ads

Google states that local ranking is based mainly on relevance, distance and prominence, and that there is no way to pay for a better local ranking. Its own advice is to verify the business, keep address, hours, category and attributes accurate, respond to reviews, and add photos and videos (see Google's [local ranking guidance](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091)).

Do this first, because it is free and it also improves every ad click that lands on your name later. Details: [Google Business Profile optimization checklist](/blog/google-business-profile-optimization-checklist).

## Decision 2: Reviews are your storefront window

BrightLocal's 2026 Local Consumer Review Survey (1,002 US adults) found:

- 68% require at least four stars to consider a business; 31% require 4.5 or higher (up from 17% the year before).
- 47% will not use a business with fewer than 20 reviews.
- 74% only consider reviews from the last three months.
- 89% expect owners to respond; 50% say generic, templated replies make them unlikely to choose the business.

Source: [BrightLocal Local Consumer Review Survey 2026](https://www.brightlocal.com/research/local-consumer-review-survey/).

For a florist, the recency finding matters most. A shop with 200 reviews, none newer than a year, can look weaker than one with 40 reviews from this season. Ask every delivery recipient and every buyer, and reply to each review with a specific detail (the arrangement, the occasion), not a template.

## Decision 3: Should you run Google Ads, and how much?

LocaliQ's 2026 search benchmarks do not publish a florist row. Its all-industry averages are a $5.42 cost per click, a 6.64% click-through rate and an 8.18% conversion rate; its closest retail category, Shopping, Collectibles and Gifts, shows a $4.14 CPC and a 4.01% conversion rate ([LocaliQ search advertising benchmarks](https://localiq.com/blog/search-advertising-benchmarks/)). Treat the gifts row as a rough proxy, not a florist benchmark.

Worked example (assumptions, not results): you plan a 3-week Mother's Day campaign with a $900 budget, a $4.14 CPC and a 4.01% conversion rate. That buys about 217 clicks and about 8.7 orders, a cost of roughly $103 per order. If your average order is $75 with 50% gross margin, each order earns about $37.50 before ad cost, so this campaign loses money on first orders. It only pays if the average order is higher (for example $120 with delivery and add-ons), if repeat buyers are captured by email, or if your real conversion rate beats the proxy. Run the same arithmetic with your own order value before you spend. See also [Google Ads for florists](/blog/google-ads-for-florists) and [best Google ad keywords for florists](/blog/best-google-ad-keywords-for-florists-in-2026).

Rule of thumb that follows from the math: if cost per order exceeds the gross margin on a typical first order, either lift order value (tiered bouquets, add-on vases, same-day fee) or put the budget into the profile and reviews first.

## Decision 4: One social channel, tied to orders

Pick one channel and link it to a page where a stranger can order. Instagram suits visual products; see [Instagram for florists](/blog/instagram-for-florists). Posting without a clear way to order sends people to a DM inbox where orders get lost.

## A 12-month plan (assumption-based template)

- Off-peak months: complete the profile, collect reviews, photograph arrangements by season, build an email list from every order.
- 4 to 6 weeks before Valentine's Day and Mother's Day: update profile photos and hours, publish pre-order pages, email past buyers first.
- Peak weeks: short paid-search campaign on high-intent terms, daily review replies, publish cut-off times clearly.
- After each peak: calculate cost per order by channel and keep only what beat your margin.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I need a website, or is Instagram enough?**
You need a page that can take an order and show delivery areas and cut-off times. Instagram can drive traffic to it, but it does not replace it.

**How many reviews should a florist aim for?**
BrightLocal found 47% of consumers avoid businesses with fewer than 20 reviews, so 20 is a floor, and recent reviews matter because 74% only consider the last three months.

**Is Google Ads worth it for a small florist?**
It depends on your cost per order versus margin. Use the worked example above with your own average order value and test a short campaign before a peak.

**Can I pay Google to rank higher in the map results?**
No. Google says there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking.

**What is the first thing to fix this week?**
Verify the profile, correct hours and categories, add current photos, and reply to every unanswered review.
