CPC charges you when someone clicks your ad. CPM charges you for every 1,000 impressions, whether or not anyone clicks. For a local business, the practical question is which model gets a customer for less money. The answer depends on one number: the click-through rate (CTR) your ad earns. Divide the CPM by 1,000 times the CTR to get an effective cost per click, then compare that figure with what a click costs you on search. Published programmatic figures give a starting point: AdPersonam recommends at least $1,000 for programmatic advertising, and Adwave quotes Netflix programmatic CPMs of $35 to $50.

## How does each model charge you?

With CPC, you pay each time someone clicks. With CPM, you pay per 1,000 impressions, so your cost is set by delivery rather than by response. Connected TV pricing uses several metrics. Amazon Ads notes that CTV programmatic pricing draws on marketing metrics such as video completion rate and [cost per completed view (CPCV)](https://advertising.amazon.com/library/guides/connected-tv-advertising), which measures the price of each completed ad.

Programmatic buying adds an auction. Strategus describes the process this way: [the highest bid wins, and the ad plays for the viewer](https://www.strategus.com/blog/ctv-platforms-a-guide-to-the-top-connected-tv-providers). A high CPM can therefore buy a slot, but it does not buy attention. That is why the click or completed view, not the impression, is where you check whether the money worked.

## Which formula settles CPM vs CPC?

Convert any CPM into an effective CPC:

**Effective CPC = CPM divided by (1,000 x CTR)**

A $10 CPM with a 0.2% CTR works out to $5 per click. The same $10 CPM at a 1% CTR is $1 per click. CPM buying is neither cheap nor expensive on its own. It depends on how many people respond.

The breakeven is easy to find. A $40 CPM against a $2 click needs a 2% CTR, which means roughly one viewer in 50 has to click. Measure your own CTR on a small test, because the result changes with format, audience and creative.

## What does a $300 test buy on each model?

Take a hair salon with $300 a month to test. The $2.00 click price, the 0.5% CTR and the 3% booking rate are assumptions for illustration. The $40 CPM is the programmatic Netflix figure Adwave quotes. Adwave's own $200 to $400 monthly test range delivers roughly 5,000 to 10,000 impressions at that CPM, and $300 sits inside it.

Under these inputs, CPC produces about four times the bookings for the same spend. Raise the CTR to 2% and the CPM option delivers 150 clicks, matching CPC exactly. For a salon that wants direct bookings this month, the search click is the safer test. For a salon launching a new service, the streaming option may be worth judging on a longer window. ## Where does CPM earn its place for local businesses?

CPM makes the most sense when you need reach before you need bookings. Programmatic CTV, which includes self-serve platforms, accounts for more than 75% of U.S. CTV ad transactions, up from roughly 60% two years earlier, according to eMarketer as cited by Adwave. That growth means more inventory is available to smaller buyers than it was.

Targeting also matters for a local business. Adwave says programmatic platforms let you target Netflix viewers by metro area, state, or zip code radius. A radius around your location cuts wasted impressions in areas you do not serve. Check current minimum spends with the platform before committing, since vendor pages quote different starting points. ## Which model fits your goal?

Work through these questions in order before choosing a bidding model:

1. Are people already searching for your service? If so, start with CPC on search, because intent is the thing you are paying for.
2. Do you need awareness for a new location, class or menu? Test a CPM format with a radius target and plan a longer measurement window.
3. Do you have a small list of past customers or site visitors? Retarget on either model, then compare effective CPC using the formula above.
4. Is the budget small? AdPersonam recommends at least $1,000 for programmatic, so a CPM test well below that buys few impressions to learn from. Start with CPC.

## What mistakes distort the comparison?

The most common error is comparing a CPM campaign's clicks with a CPC campaign's leads. Put both on the same measure, such as cost per booking or cost per call, before deciding anything. Clicks from a streaming campaign and clicks from search are not equivalent.

The second error is judging reach in a week. BidWaves says it reviews results and prioritizes next actions each month, and it optimizes bids, audiences, placements and creative on that same monthly cycle. Plan at least one full cycle before you call a CPM test a failure. Pair that with conversion tracking, because without it neither model can be judged. Google describes [Smart bidding as bidding that uses AI to optimize for conversions or conversion value in each auction](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/2404190?hl=en), which only works if the conversions are being recorded.

Finally, watch the cost of each booking, not the cost per thousand. The [programmatic cost benchmarks](/blog/how-much-does-programmatic-advertising-cost-real-cpm-benchmarks-2026) show how CPM figures translate into channel budgets, but your own booking numbers decide the winner.

## FAQ

### Is CPM or CPC cheaper for a local business?

Neither is cheaper by default. Convert CPM to effective CPC with the formula above, using your own measured CTR, and compare that with your search CPC.

### Is CPC better for small business search ads?

Usually, for people ready to buy, because you pay only when someone clicks. Reach-focused goals can justify CPM, especially for a new location or offer.

### What CTR breaks even between a $40 CPM and a $2 click?

A 2% CTR. At that rate, 1,000 impressions produce 20 clicks, so the $40 buys the same 20 clicks as $40 of search clicks would at $2 each.

### Can I run both models at the same time?

Yes, but give each its own budget and conversion goal so the results do not blur together.
