Here is the number that decides whether marketing help is affordable: about 5. If you spend $1,000 a month on ads and pay $600 a month for someone to run them, you need roughly five new customers a month, each worth $300 in profit, just to break even. Hire the wrong person and you pay $600 for a monthly PDF. Hire the right one and the same $600 is the cheapest line on your P&L. Those inputs are assumptions, and we will work through them below. The point is that "affordable" is not about the lowest fee. It is about what each new customer costs you all in.

This guide is for owners of local businesses in the US, UK, Australia and Canada who want professional help without agency prices. It covers how much to budget, what is worth paying for and what you can do yourself, how to vet a freelancer or agency, and how to protect yourself while you do it.

## How Much Should You Budget?

There is no single right answer, and the common rules of thumb are old. The US Small Business Administration's [marketing budget article](https://www.sba.gov/blog/how-get-most-your-marketing-budget) (published July 2019) says many businesses use a percentage of revenue as a guide, and quotes two outside figures:

The same article reports restaurants at 1.93% and retailers at about 4% of revenue on advertising, and notes that startups generally need larger budgets than established businesses. These are second-hand figures from about 2018, and "marketing" and "advertising" are different buckets, so use them as a sanity check, not a target. If your business earns $400,000 a year, 7.9% is about $31,600 a year, or roughly $2,630 a month. At 1.08% it is about $360 a month. Your answer sits somewhere in that wide band, and it depends on how fast you want to grow and how much of the work you do yourself.

SBA's practical advice is more useful than the percentages: track results, even by simply asking new customers how they heard about you, and use different codes for each ad to see which attracts the most customers. ## What Is Worth Paying For

Pay for the work where mistakes are expensive and expertise is technical. For most local businesses that means:

**Paid search management.** Match types, negative keywords, bidding and conversion tracking are easy to get wrong, and wrong settings cost real money. Google's own [match type guide](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/2497836) shows the difference between broad, phrase and exact match, and that broad match is designed to work with Smart Bidding. A small account on the wrong setting can spend a month's budget on irrelevant searches.

**A one-time local search clean-up.** Many local businesses have obvious gaps in their Google Business Profile: missing categories, wrong hours, no photos, no review routine. Google says complete and accurate information makes a business [more likely to show up in local search results](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091), and also that there is no way to pay for a better local ranking. So this is a fixable, one-off job, not an ongoing retainer. Start with our [Google Business Profile checklist](/blog/google-business-profile-optimization-checklist).

**Conversion fixes.** If you already get website visitors but few enquiries, an audit of your page and phone or form flow is usually a one-off project.

## What You Can Probably Do Yourself

- **Social posts.** The best content for a local business comes from the people in it. A consultant can supply a simple framework and templates; you or your team supply the photos and stories.
- **Email newsletters.** A monthly email to your own customer list is manageable with a free or entry-level email tool.
- **Review requests.** Asking happy customers is a habit, not a skill. Build it into checkout.
- **Blog posts.** Worth doing if you enjoy writing and know your customers' questions. A consultant can give you the topics.

The rule: outsource what needs expertise, keep what needs your voice.

## Freelancer, Agency or DIY?

| | DIY | Freelance consultant | Agency |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cost | Your time | Usually a flat monthly fee or a percentage of ad spend | Often a monthly retainer, sometimes a minimum ad spend |
| Best for | Social, email, reviews | One or two defined channels | Larger budgets and multi-channel work |
| Main risk | Slow, inconsistent | One person's bandwidth | Small accounts get junior attention |
| What to check | Your own weekly time | Their past work in your type of business | Who actually runs your account |

Published prices vary a lot. A 2026 rate guide from [BizToolKit](https://www.biztoolkit.co/post/freelance-google-ads-manager-rates-in-2026) lists $500 to $1,500 a month flat for accounts spending $1,000 to $5,000 a month on ads, or 10 to 20% of ad spend. That guide cites no data or method, so treat it as a conversation starter and get two or three real quotes. The same goes for any claim that one model "always" beats another: the right choice depends on your budget and how much the provider pays attention to you.

A Google Partner badge is a mixed signal for a small account. Google's [badge page](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/9702452?hl=en) requires a minimum optimization score of 70%, $10,000 in ad spend over 90 days across managed accounts, and certifications for at least half of account strategists. That shows a company handles meaningful volume and has trained staff. It does not tell you whether they will care about your $800-a-month account.

## How to Vet Help Without Getting Burned

**Ask for a look at your current situation first.** A reputable provider can review your Google Business Profile, your ads and your website before pitching. Compare what two providers notice. If they only list problems you could find yourself, keep looking.

**Ask for work in your type of business.** "We've run campaigns for salons like yours" is stronger than "10 years of experience". Ask what the results were in numbers and over what period, and note whether they offer a reference you can call.

**Keep ownership of your accounts.** Your Google Ads account, Business Profile, analytics and domain should be in your name, with the provider invited as a user. Google's help explains that a manager account sends a [link request](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/7459601?hl=en) and the owning account must accept, so you stay in control. If a provider insists on running your ads inside their own account, ask what happens to your data and history if you leave.

**Prefer short commitments.** A month-to-month arrangement, or a 90-day trial with an exit, tells you the provider expects to earn the next month. Be cautious of anything that locks you in before they have shown results.

**Agree what "working" means.** Before you start, write down 90-day targets: a cost per lead, a number of calls from your Business Profile, a number of booked jobs. Vague promises like "more visibility" cannot be checked.

**Ask what they will report.** You want leads, calls, cost per lead and booked jobs, not impressions. If they cannot connect spend to enquiries, you will not know whether to keep paying.

## The Math

Take a monthly plan of $1,000 in ad spend plus a $600 management fee, so $1,600 in total. Every row below is an **assumption** meant to show the logic, not predict your results. Replace the figures with your own after a month of data.

| | Tough | Base | Strong |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ad spend (assumption) | $1,000 | $1,000 | $1,000 |
| Management fee (assumption) | $600 | $600 | $600 |
| Cost per lead (assumption) | $80 | $50 | $35 |
| Leads | 12.5 | 20 | 28.6 |
| Lead to customer rate (assumption) | 20% | 30% | 40% |
| New customers | 2.5 | 6 | 11.4 |
| Profit per customer over first year (assumption) | $300 | $300 | $300 |
| Gross profit | $750 | $1,800 | $3,420 |
| Total cost per customer, including fee | $640 | $267 | $140 |
| **Net monthly result** | **-$850** | **+$200** | **+$1,820** |

How to read it. Break-even is total monthly cost divided by profit per customer: $1,600 / $300 = 5.3 new customers a month. The Tough column fails because the cost per lead is high and few leads close. Notice that a 10-point improvement in lead-to-customer rate matters about as much as a better ad account, and that part is your phone-answering and booking process, not the freelancer. Notice also that the fee is 37.5% of your total spend, so on a very small ad budget the fee can swamp the benefit. If your ad spend is $300 a month, a $600 fee needs a different arrangement, like a one-off set-up project, or you should do it yourself while it is small.

And customer profit must be real profit. Use revenue minus direct costs, and be honest about repeat visits rather than hoping.

With a small budget, one channel done well beats three done badly. Pick the one closest to a purchase decision, usually Google Business Profile and search ads, and add others only when that one is working and you have time to track it.
</Callout>

## Do This Today

1. Write down your monthly marketing budget as an amount, a percentage of revenue, and the profit you make per new customer. Compute your break-even customers a month.
2. Check that your Google Business Profile and ad accounts are in your name and that you know the logins. Fix any gaps first.
3. Decide which one channel to start with, and which jobs you will keep (posts, review requests) versus pay for.
4. Ask two providers for a short review of your current setup and a written quote, and compare what each noticed and how they would report results.
5. Agree on a 90-day target in numbers, and add a "how did you hear about us?" question to your booking or checkout.

## FAQ

**Q: How much should a small business spend on marketing?**
A: There is no official figure. The SBA article quotes a 2018 average of 7.9% of revenue for marketing spending across business types, and 1.08% for advertising only, from outside sources. Treat those as a range to sanity check against, then base your budget on what a new customer is worth.

**Q: Is a freelancer cheaper than an agency?**
A: Often yes for a single channel, but not always. Compare what you get for the fee, who works on your account, and what the exit terms are. Get at least two quotes in writing.

**Q: Can I just do it myself?**
A: For social, email and review requests, usually yes. For paid ads, you can, but only if you will check the account weekly and set up conversion tracking first. Mistakes are cheaper to avoid than to pay for.

**Q: Should I sign a long contract to get a lower price?**
A: Only if you have already seen results with that provider. A lower price on a contract you cannot leave is not a discount if the work does not perform.

**Q: How long before I know it is working?**
A: Calls and leads can start within days of ads going live. Give it at least four to eight weeks, my rule of thumb rather than a published figure, before judging, and judge on cost per customer rather than clicks.
