Here is the picture to aim for. On $900 a month, with a $4.50 click, 7 in 100 clicks turning into a booking and a $65 first visit, a nail salon gets about 14 bookings at roughly $64 each. Change the click price or the booking rate and that swings from about $35 to about $137 per booking. Those three inputs are my assumptions, not benchmarks, and the math section below shows how to swap in your own.

This guide is about the build: how to structure the campaign, how to split a small budget so it does not leak, and how to count bookings properly. It is for salon owners in the US, UK, Australia and Canada. Dollar figures are US-based; for pounds or Australian and Canadian dollars, run the same arithmetic with your own click prices. For the keyword list itself, use our [nail salon keyword guide](/blog/best-google-ad-keywords-for-nail-salons-in-2026) rather than building one from scratch, and for the budget question in general see [how much a nail salon should spend on Google Ads](/blog/how-much-should-a-nail-salon-spend-on-google-ads). This page is what sits between the two.

## Build Three Campaigns, Not One Pile

Most salon accounts fail because everything sits in one campaign with one budget, so the broad, expensive terms eat the cheap, high-intent ones. Split by intent:

| Campaign | What goes in it | Share of budget (my suggestion) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Services + location | Ad groups for gel, acrylic, dip powder, pedicure, plus "near me" and neighbourhood terms | 80% |
| 2. Brand | Your salon name, exact match only | 5% |
| 3. Seasonal | Prom, wedding, holiday, summer sandals offers, switched on a few weeks before | 15% held back |

Those shares are a starting point, not a Google rule. On $900 a month that is about $720, $45 and $135. If you have nothing seasonal coming up, leave the 15% in Campaign 1 until you do.

Within Campaign 1, one ad group per service keeps the ad matching the search. A person typing "dip powder nails [city]" should see "Dip Powder Nails in [City]" in the headline, not a generic salon ad. The full term lists, match type advice and negatives are in the [keyword guide](/blog/best-google-ad-keywords-for-nail-salons-in-2026) and the negative keywords guide.

### Match types in one paragraph

Google's [match type help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/2497836) says exact match gives the most control and reaches the fewest searches, phrase match reaches searches that carry your keyword's meaning, and broad match needs Smart Bidding ("It's critical to use Smart Bidding with broad match"). At a salon budget, start with phrase and exact. Add broad match later, only if you have enough conversion data to feed automated bidding.

## Set the Budget Mechanics Correctly

Google's [daily budget help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/6385083) explains three things that surprise owners:

- Divide the monthly figure by 30.4. A $304 month is a $10 average daily budget, so $900 is about $29.60 a day.
- Spend varies by day. On some days you spend less than the average, on others more.
- On any single day Google will not charge more than two times your average daily budget for most campaigns, and in a month no more than 30.4 times it.

So a "$30 a day" campaign can show a $55 day without anything being wrong. Judge it over the month, not the day.

### Bidding: tracking first, automation second

Google says [Maximize conversions](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/7381968) requires conversion tracking, will try to fully spend your daily budget, and ignores most manual bid adjustments. Its help page also says that if you have a specific cost goal, Target CPA is the option to use. The help page gives no minimum number of conversions.

My advice, not Google's rule: until you have a few weeks of tracked bookings, run Manual CPC on the exact and phrase keywords so a new account cannot overspend on expensive terms. Once bookings are coming in steadily, test Maximize conversions or Target CPA in one campaign and compare the cost per booking.

### Location setting

Google's [location targeting help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/6317) describes "presence or interest" as the default and recommended setting. For a salon I would check the setting in your own account and choose presence only, meaning people in or regularly in your area, so you do not pay for someone elsewhere who searched your city. That is my preference for a walk-in business. Google also says location targeting is best effort, not exact.

Draw the radius from your real clients: look at the last 50 bookings and note where they live or work.

## Ads and Assets That Get the Booking

Write headlines that repeat the search: service, area, and a real reason to book. For example `Gel Nails in [Neighbourhood]`, `Walk-Ins Welcome Until 6pm`, `Book Online in 60 Seconds`, `Nail Art and Dip Powder`. Only use "same-day appointments" if you honour it, and only say "top rated" if your reviews back it.

Add call and location assets. Per Google's [assets help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/7331111), call assets add a phone number or call button, can be scheduled to show only when you can answer, and are charged at the usual click rate. Location assets show your address, a call button and a link to your business details page. Google says assets show only when predicted to improve the ad and need a minimum Ad Rank, so they will not appear on every impression.

## Ad Schedule: Use It Lightly

Google's [ad schedule help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/2404248) says campaigns show at any hour by default, and that you can limit ads to certain days and hours or adjust bids for them. Scheduled campaigns still pace toward 30.4 times the daily budget. For a salon, the useful move is to run call assets only while someone answers the phone. Do not switch off whole days based on two weeks of data; bookings made in the evening for next-day slots are easy to miss.

## Count Bookings, Not Clicks

Salon enquiries arrive in three ways: online bookings, phone calls and forms. Set up all three before turning ads on. Our conversion tracking setup guide and call tracking guide cover the steps.

If you use a booking platform, check its help centre for a Google Ads link. As one example, Fresha's [help article on tracking Google Ads conversions](https://www.fresha.com/help-center/knowledge-base/online-presence/103391-track-google-ads-conversions) says it sends completed bookings to Google Ads as conversions, but only when the client clicked your ad and then booked, and the conversion action must be a website type. It also suggests making a test booking after clicking your own ad to confirm it works. Other platforms differ, so ask yours.

The ad landing page matters as much as the ad. Send clicks to a service page with prices, photos of your work, real reviews, a tap-to-call number and one booking button. See [landing page best practices](/blog/landing-page-best-practices).

## The Math

Take a $900 monthly budget. Every row marked assumption is mine, not a market figure. The Base CPC sits near the $4.62 beauty benchmark above; the others bracket it.

| | Tough | Base | Strong |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Monthly budget (assumption) | $900 | $900 | $900 |
| Cost per click (assumption) | $5.50 | $4.50 | $3.50 |
| Clicks | 164 | 200 | 257 |
| Click to booking rate (assumption) | 4% | 7% | 10% |
| Bookings | 6.5 | 14 | 25.7 |
| Ad cost per booking | $137 | $64 | $35 |
| First visit ticket (assumption) | $65 | $65 | $65 |
| Visits in first 12 months (assumption) | 3 | 3 | 3 |
| Revenue per new client in 12 months | $195 | $195 | $195 |
| Revenue left after ad cost | $58 | $131 | $160 |

Read it carefully. These are revenue figures, before wages, product and rent, so real profit per client is lower. In the Tough column the first visit alone loses money ($137 to win a $65 sale), and the account only breaks even if the client really comes back. In the Base column the first visit roughly covers its cost.

Your break-even click price: maximum CPC = the most you will pay per booking x your click-to-booking rate. If you will pay $60 per booking at a 7% rate, your ceiling is $60 x 0.07 = $4.20 a click. Above that, you lose money on average.

The lever that moves most is the booking rate, which is a landing page, phone-answering and availability problem. Going from 4% to 7% cuts cost per booking by almost half with no change to bids.

The Tough column only works if clients return. Pull your own numbers: of the new clients you got last quarter, how many came back within 8 weeks? Scale spend only once you know that figure.

## Optimise Weekly

1. Read the search terms report and add negatives. Our mistakes guide for nail salons lists the usual leaks.
2. Pause keywords that spent about twice your target cost per booking with no booking. That threshold is my rule of thumb.
3. Test one new headline every two weeks.
4. Compare call versus online booking volume, and make sure phone hours match call assets.
5. Raise budget by about 20% only on a campaign that is hitting your target cost per booking. Keep your Google Business Profile strong, since people check photos and reviews before they book.

## Do This Today

1. Pull your last 50 new clients and map where they live or work. That is your ad radius.
2. Decide the most you will pay for one new client, then work out your maximum click price with the formula above.
3. Create Campaign 1 (services and location) with one ad group per service, phrase and exact match, plus the negatives from the keyword guide.
4. Set up online booking, call and form conversions, and make a test booking from your own ad to confirm each is counted.
5. Add call and location assets, set the daily budget to your monthly figure divided by 30.4, and book 20 minutes every week to read the search terms report.

## FAQ

**Q: How much should a nail salon spend on Google Ads each month?**
A: There is no official figure. Work backwards: the most you will pay for a new client, divided by how many clicks it takes to win one, gives your break-even click price. Then see whether your area's click prices fit. Our [budget guide](/blog/how-much-should-a-nail-salon-spend-on-google-ads) goes deeper.

**Q: Should I use Performance Max for a nail salon?**
A: Not first. Start with Search so you control the keywords, then test other types when you have steady booking data. See [Performance Max vs standard Google Ads](/blog/performance-max-vs-standard-google-ads-which-should-you-run).

**Q: Why does Google sometimes spend more than my daily budget?**
A: Google says daily spend can reach up to twice the average daily budget on a given day for most campaigns, but not more than 30.4 times the average over a month.

**Q: Can I run ads if my only website is a booking-platform page?**
A: Yes, but check whether your platform can pass booking conversions back to Google Ads, as some do. Without that you can only count clicks and calls, which makes optimising harder.

**Q: How long until bookings come in?**
A: Clicks can start within hours, and bookings in the first weeks. Give it four to six weeks, my rule of thumb, before major changes.
