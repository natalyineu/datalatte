Yes, show prices where you can, or at least "from" prices and typical ranges. A visitor who cannot see a starting cost has to call or submit a form just to learn whether you fit their budget. A short price table or a few sample scenarios removes that step. Where a job is truly custom, a range still gives buyers enough to decide whether to get in touch. Benchmarks suggest most websites convert only a small share of visitors, so anything that answers a buyer's first question is worth testing.

## Why does showing a price help a local visitor?

Conversion rate is the share of visitors who take the action you want, such as booking or enquiring, as [Adwave defines it](https://adwave.com/resources/how-to-market-my-online-business). A visible price moves a visitor closer to that action because it answers a question they already have. They can judge fit before they commit any time.

Benchmarks give a sense of scale. [Outbrain reports](https://www.outbrain.com/blog/advanced-retargeting-tips/) that the average conversion rate across industries sits between 2 and 3%, citing a third-party source. Another [retargeting guide](https://www.benchmarkemail.com/blog/tools-for-retargeting-ads/) cites a 3% ceiling for first-time visitors, drawn from a 2016 report. Both are older or general figures, so test on your own site rather than treating them as targets.

## Which price format fits your service?

Not every service suits an exact price. Choose the format that matches how much the job varies, then test it against your current page.

| Format | Best for | Main risk | Example line |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Exact fixed price | Standard, repeatable services | Customers feel locked in if extras appear | "Standard 60-minute massage: $X" |
| From-price | Services with a clear starting point | Visitors assume the lowest price is typical | "Dog grooming from $X" |
| Typical range | Jobs with a few common size or scope bands | Range too wide to help | "Small bathroom repaint: $X to $Y" |
| Sample scenario | Trades and custom work | Scenarios read as quotes | "Example: 2 rooms, 1 coat, about $X" |
| Quote only | Large or unusual projects | Visitors leave without a number | Use only with a stated reply time |

## How should a bundle or package price be shown?

If you sell bundles, [Adwave's guidance](https://adwave.com/resources/creating-package-deals-and-bundles-that-increase-average-order-value) is to show the individual prices, the bundle price and the dollar savings clearly. Visitors then see the value without doing arithmetic.

For example, using assumed prices: a bath at $60 and a nail trim at $15 sell separately for $75. A bundle at $65 saves $10. Put all three numbers in one row so the saving is visible at a glance.

## What should a local pricing page include?

Keep the page short and make the next step obvious. Adwave's [trust and conversion guidance](https://adwave.com/resources/how-to-create-an-about-page-that-builds-trust) points to clear credibility signals, and a pricing page is a natural place for them.

1. A line stating what the price includes and excludes.
2. A table of services with from-prices or ranges.
3. A short note on what changes the price, such as size, time or materials.
4. Your location and contact details, shown on the same page.
5. One clear action such as "Book Now" or "Request a quote."

A [med spa keyword guide](https://www.max-conversion.com/best-keywords-to-use-for-med-spa-google-ads-campaign) makes the same point for searches like "cost near me": show pricing, a clear call to action, and location and contact details together. If the page has a booking form, [Adwave's mobile rule](https://adwave.com/resources/mobile-first-website-design) requires every field to show its label above the input in portrait orientation. Use the [local website checklist](/blog/local-business-website-checklist) to check the rest of the page.

## What does a price table change for a groomer?

All inputs in this example are assumptions, not measured results.

| Input (assumption) | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Monthly visitors to service pages | 600 |
| Enquiry rate with no prices | 4% |
| Enquiry rate with a price table | 5% |
| Share of enquiries that book | 50% |
| Average booking value | $70 |

Without prices: 600 x 4% = 24 enquiries, 12 bookings, and 12 x $70 = $840 a month. With prices: 600 x 5% = 30 enquiries, 15 bookings, and 15 x $70 = $1,050 a month. The difference is $210 a month. The one-point lift is an assumption you must replace with your own result. Some visitors may also drop out because the price is too high, which can reduce enquiries but save time spent on poor-fit calls.

## How do you measure whether prices help?

Run the change as a test, not a permanent verdict. Track enquiries and bookings per week for a few weeks before the change and a few weeks after. Compare the number of enquiries, the number that book, and whether the enquiries look like good-fit customers.

Measurement needs two pieces. [Adwave describes](https://adwave.com/resources/conversion-tracking-for-small-business) most website tracking as a base tag on every page plus an event snippet that fires when a conversion happens. Without both, you cannot tell whether the pricing table or something else changed the result. For a benchmark on what a good result looks like for your type of business, see the [conversion rate benchmarks guide](/blog/ctr-conversion-rate-benchmarks-local-business).

## When should a business avoid a single price?

Custom work, such as a full renovation or a wedding package, can make a single number misleading. Use a range or a sample scenario instead, and say plainly that the final figure needs a quote. State how fast you reply so visitors know what to expect.

Keep the wording consistent across the website, your listings and your phone script. A visitor who sees one figure online and hears another on the phone is more likely to leave. Check local consumer rules on how prices must be displayed, and see the [GDPR guide for local business websites](/blog/local-business-website-gdpr-compliance) if your form collects personal details.

## FAQ

### Will showing prices let competitors undercut me?

They can see your prices either way. Compete on service, reviews and response speed as well as on price, and make sure the price table tells visitors what they get for the money.

### What if my prices change often?

Show from-prices with a last-updated date, and keep one source of truth so the page does not go stale. Old prices damage trust faster than no prices.

### Do I need exact prices?

No. Ranges, from-prices and sample scenarios work when exact prices vary. Say what changes the price so visitors understand the range.

### Should I also write new copy around the prices?

Yes. Prices work better when the surrounding text explains what the customer receives. The [website copy examples](/blog/how-to-write-website-copy-local-business-examples) show how to do this for local services.
