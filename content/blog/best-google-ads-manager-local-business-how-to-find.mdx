To find a Google Ads manager, start with referrals from owners of similar businesses in other towns, shortlist two or three people, then test each one on your own account before you sign. Whether you need one at all is an arithmetic question. In the worked example below, a flat $500 monthly fee on $1,000 of ad spend needs about 1.25 extra customers a month to pay for itself, assuming each customer is worth $400 in gross profit (an assumption; use your own number).

This guide is for owners of local businesses in the US, UK, Australia and Canada, such as coffee shops, salons, studios, groomers and trades. It covers finding and testing the person who will run the account.

## Do You Need a Manager Before You Search?

A manager earns the fee in three ways: cutting wasted clicks, fixing tracking so you can see which ads produce customers, and saving your time. Before you hire, check which of those you actually need.

If your spend is small, there may not be enough traffic for a manager to change much. In that case, a tight Search campaign you run yourself, or a one-off audit, may be the better spend. If the account feels like a black box, or you simply have no hours to give it, a manager is worth interviewing, because you are buying time and clarity.

## Where Should You Look for Candidates?

Four sources are worth checking, in roughly this order:

1. Referrals from owners of similar businesses in a different town. They have no reason to hide who they use or what they pay.
2. Partner directories and agency listings. A badge shows that a company met certain criteria at one point. It does not show that the person has run accounts like yours, so check the person, not the logo.
3. Freelancers with visible case write-ups that explain the numbers, not just star ratings or testimonials.
4. Your web developer or SEO provider, but only if they pass the tests in the sections below.

## Which Three Tests Should Every Candidate Pass?

Anyone can sound convincing on a call, so run these tests in order.

Test 1 is the search terms report. Ask each candidate to look at a search terms report from your account, or a screenshot you provide, and say on the call which phrases they would block. A competent person will point to specific irrelevant searches, explain negative keywords and mention match types. If there is no account yet, ask them to describe the first week for a business like yours: which keywords, which locations and which negatives.

Test 2 is tracking. Ask, "What will you count as a conversion, and how will phone calls be tracked?" A strong answer names form submissions, calls from the ad, calls from the website and bookings, all set up before ads go live. If the answer stops at "clicks" or "we track the thank-you page," pass. For what complete measurement looks like, see the [Google Analytics 4 setup guide for local businesses](/blog/google-analytics-4-setup-local-business).

Test 3 is bidding. Ask, "When would you use automated bidding on my account, and what data would you want first?" Good answers tie automation to having enough tracked conversions. Be wary of anyone who switches every campaign to automation in week one on an account with very few conversions.

If you are close to choosing, offer a paid, fixed-scope audit or a first-month trial with a written scope and a clear exit. A capable person usually accepts a paid audit readily. One who insists on a long lock-in before showing any work is telling you something.

## How Do You Keep Ownership of the Account?

Whoever you hire, the account should be yours. Create it under your own Google login and payment card, make yourself the administrator, and then link the manager's access. Log in during the first week to confirm you can see every campaign, conversion setting and bill.

Ask what happens to your data, your conversion tracking and your history if you leave. Put in writing that you own the data, that a named person is responsible for your account, and that you can exit on notice. If a candidate wants to run your ads inside their own account and bill you for media, get a clear answer on how you would take the account back.

## Why Do Hiring Relationships Break Down?

A guide to overseeing freelance social media managers, which cites Twine's research, says misaligned expectations contribute to a 48% turnover rate ([Adwave, secondary](https://adwave.com/resources/how-to-hire-and-manage-a-social-media-intern-or-freelancer)). That figure comes from social media work, not paid search, so treat it as a warning about process rather than a precise forecast for Google Ads.

The cause still carries over. When scope, reporting cadence and decision rights are vague, the relationship tends to fall apart early. Before you sign, agree on what the manager will change in month one, how often you will receive a report, which numbers that report will show and who approves changes to budget.

## How Much Ad Spend Justifies a Manager?

The table compares the same $1,000 monthly budget with and without a manager who cuts wasted clicks. Every input is an assumption for illustration. Replace each one with your own figures after you pull a month of account data.

| | Unmanaged account | With a manager |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monthly ad spend (assumption) | $1,000 | $1,000 |
| Cost per click (assumption) | $3.00 | $3.00 |
| Clicks | 333 | 333 |
| Clicks from irrelevant searches (assumption) | 30% | 10% |
| Relevant clicks | 233 | 300 |
| Click to lead rate (assumption) | 8% | 8% |
| Leads | 18.7 | 24 |
| Lead to customer rate (assumption) | 25% | 25% |
| New customers | 4.7 | 6.0 |
| Gross profit per customer over time (assumption) | $400 | $400 |
| Gross profit | $1,867 | $2,400 |
| Ad spend | $1,000 | $1,000 |
| Manager fee (assumption, flat) | $0 | $500 |
| Net after ads and fee | $867 | $900 |

Cutting wasted clicks from 30% to 10% adds about 1.3 customers a month, worth roughly $533 at the assumed $400 each. That barely clears the $500 fee, leaving a net gain of about $33 in this scenario. At $1,000 of spend, a manager is only marginally ahead of doing nothing.

The same improvement on $3,000 of spend adds about 4 customers, worth roughly $1,600 against the same fee (assumption). That is the case for hiring at higher spend, and for doing it yourself or buying a one-off audit at lower spend. Compute your own gross profit per customer before the first call, because a small change in that number moves the answer more than any change in the manager's badge.

## What Red Flags Should End the Conversation?

Walk away if a candidate guarantees leads, rankings or cost per click, because auction prices move with competition and no one can promise your number. Be cautious if they ask nothing about your margin, average sale, service area or how calls are handled, since that usually means a template is being run.

Watch for three more signals. A candidate who never mentions the landing page is ignoring the most common leak, clicks that never become calls. A report built only on impressions and clicks, with no cost per lead, calls or bookings, is measuring activity instead of outcomes. A reluctance to give you administrator access, or a rushed contract with a long lock-in and a setup fee before any work is shown, should end the process.

## What Should You Do This Week?

1. Calculate gross profit per new customer over their time with you, and write down the most you could pay to acquire one.
2. Pull the last 90 days of ad data if you have it, or set a budget if you do not, and note whether conversions are being tracked at all.
3. Shortlist two or three candidates from referrals or directories and send each one the three tests in this guide.
4. Ask each candidate for a sample report showing cost per lead, and for the names of accounts in your sector they have run.
5. Before signing, put in writing that you are the account administrator, you own the data, a named person is responsible and you can exit on notice.

## FAQ

### How much should a Google Ads manager cost?

No single price applies. Start from your own numbers: the fee has to be covered by the extra customers the manager brings in. The worked example above shows how to test that before you commit.

### Is a Google Partner badge enough?

No. A badge shows that a company met certain criteria at a point in time. It does not show that the individual has run accounts like yours, or that they will use your conversion data well. Use the three tests to check the person.

### Should I hire a freelancer or an agency?

For a single location with a modest budget, a specialist freelancer or a small shop is often a better fit, because the person you meet is usually the person doing the work. Agencies tend to suit multi-location or larger budgets. Run the same tests either way.

### Can I just let automation run the account?

You can use automated bidding, but it needs tracked conversions to learn from. Without correct tracking and a clean negative keyword list, automation optimises toward whatever you counted, including low-quality enquiries.
