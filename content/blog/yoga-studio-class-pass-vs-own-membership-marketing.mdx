Take a member who pays $120 a month and comes six times: each visit earns you $20. Now suppose a marketplace visit pays $10 (an assumption, and we will test it). You would need 12 of those visits to match one member, and the person behind them may never come back to you directly. That one comparison explains most of the ClassPass debate for yoga studios.

This is for studio owners in the US, UK, Australia and Canada. It covers how ClassPass says it pays, what the evidence on payouts shows, how to run the numbers on your own timetable, and a way to use ClassPass as a lead source without letting it become your business. Dollar figures are US-based; swap in your own currency and prices.

## How ClassPass Pays Studios

ClassPass publishes some of its terms for partners, and its site blocks automated reading, so the details below come from a ClassPass executive's article on the Mindbody blog (last updated June 30, 2026) and from a competitor's summary of ClassPass's partner FAQ (second-hand; confirm in your own contract).

What those sources say:

- **Payment mechanics.** The Mindbody article says ClassPass pays studios through Tipalti, a third-party payment service, with deposits into your bank account.
- **A confidential rate floor.** Per the second-hand summary, each studio agrees a floor set as a percentage of its direct price, and ClassPass's dynamic pricing tool, SmartRate, adjusts credit prices by demand and timing without going below that floor.
- **A lower rate than direct.** The same summary quotes ClassPass's FAQ as saying it often pays a lower per-spot rate than a direct booking, because it sells spots that would otherwise be empty.
- **No upfront cost, 90-day opt-out.** The summary also reports no upfront fee and a 90-day opt-out window, plus rules that stop partners sending dedicated offers to ClassPass users or making comparative references to ClassPass. Read these clauses before signing, because they limit exactly the conversion tactics this article recommends.
- **Claimed upside.** The Mindbody article says 94% of ClassPass users were new to the venues they visited (as of January 2025) and that the average Mindbody business sees about a 29% revenue increase after six months. These are the vendor's own figures, from a promotional piece that lists no drawbacks.

## What the Evidence on Payouts Looks Like

Independent data on current payouts is thin, because partner terms are confidential. The best-known critical account is a [VICE investigation from February 2020](https://www.vice.com/en/article/classpass-is-squeezing-studios-to-the-point-of-death/) based on interviews with 18 current and former partners. It reported per-student payments as low as $7 under dynamic pricing, and individual yoga studios averaging around $10 to $12 a visit, against drop-in rates of $16 to $25. ClassPass disputed the account, calling the owners a vocal minority and saying it does not pay below predetermined floors.

Treat those figures as dated, not as today's rates, since the article is more than six years old and ClassPass has changed its pricing tools since. They do show the shape of the issue: payouts commonly land at roughly half of a direct price or less, and the same account reported ClassPass users making up anywhere from about 20% of one studio's visitors to much more at others. You cannot assume your payout. Ask for your floor in writing.

## The Math

Use one class type and one month. Every row below is an **assumption** for illustration, except that the $10 payout sits inside the range the VICE report described. Replace each with your contract and attendance data.

| | Assumption | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Class capacity | 20 spots | |
| Off-peak classes using ClassPass per week | 8 (assumed) | about 35 per month |
| Spots offered to ClassPass per class | 6 (assumed) | |
| Share of offered spots that fill | 60% (assumed) | 3.6 visits per class |
| ClassPass visits per month | 35 x 3.6 | 126 |
| Payout per visit | $10 (assumed) | $1,260 per month |
| Share of those visits who would have paid $20 direct anyway | 15% (assumed) | about 19 visits |
| Direct revenue displaced | 19 x $20 | about $380 |
| Net extra revenue | $1,260 - $380 | about $880 per month |
| Unique ClassPass people per month | 80 (assumed) | |
| Share who become direct members | 5% (assumed) | 4 members |
| New recurring revenue | 4 x $120 | $480 per month |

How to read it: ClassPass earned about $880 net because the classes were half empty and the instructor was already paid. If those classes were already near full, the displaced share would be far higher and the net could go negative. The $480 of new membership revenue is where the real value sits, and it is worth comparing with paid acquisition. If an ad campaign costs you $150 per new member (an assumption; see how much a yoga studio should spend on Google Ads to work out yours), four members cost $600 once. A conversion rate of 5% is a guess, so track your own.

Two formulas to keep:

- **Visits to match one member** = monthly fee / ClassPass payout. At $120 and $10, that is 12 visits.
- **Break-even displacement** = how many of your ClassPass visits can be cannibalised before the channel loses money. When the displaced share times your direct price exceeds your payout times total visits, stop.

## Where Your Own Membership Wins

- **Revenue per visit.** Your fee is not shared with a marketplace.
- **Retention you can influence.** Members get your emails, texts, challenges and referral perks. The Mindbody article quotes ClassPass saying neither company will market to your customers directly using your data and that marketing goes to opted-in consumers, so confirm in your agreement what contact data you actually receive.
- **Pricing control.** You choose intro offers, tiers and pauses. Our [email sequence for converting trial members](/blog/fitness-studio-email-sequence-trial-to-annual-member) shows how to move a first-timer into a paid plan.
- **Predictability.** Recurring billing makes next month's revenue easier to forecast than marketplace bookings.

The catch is acquisition. Your own membership needs a way to reach new people: local search, a good Business Profile, ads and referrals. ## Where ClassPass Can Earn Its Place

ClassPass is most defensible in three situations:

1. **A new studio** with empty classes and no list yet. The marginal cost of an empty mat is close to zero.
2. **Specific off-peak slots** that you cap tightly, such as Tuesday 1 pm, so peak classes stay for full-price members.
3. **Discovery.** If your numbers show 5% or more of ClassPass visitors becoming members, the channel is cheaper than ads. If it shows 1%, it is just discounting.

It is least defensible when most of your classes are already near capacity, or when you cannot track who converts.

## A Hybrid Plan That Protects Your Brand

1. **Cap ClassPass spots per class** and keep peak classes closed to it. Do this in the ClassPass partner tools or your scheduling software.
2. **Give every visitor a path.** A welcome note, a QR code to your intro offer, and a front-desk script. Check the contract before sending ClassPass users anything marketing-led.
3. **Make the direct price obviously better for regulars.** If a member comes six times for $120, their $20 per visit should feel clearly cheaper than a marketplace plan.
4. **Review quarterly.** If displacement rises or conversion falls, reduce ClassPass inventory.
5. **Spend on direct acquisition in parallel.** Pilates and yoga searchers behave similarly; our [Google Ads for Pilates studios](/blog/google-ads-for-pilates-studios) and [Google Ads for gyms](/blog/google-ads-for-gyms) guides show the funnel math in detail.

<Callout type="coffee" title="One Number to Track">
Measure ClassPass-to-member conversion monthly: new members who first visited through ClassPass divided by unique ClassPass people. It is the number that decides whether the channel is marketing or markdown.
</Callout>

## Do This Today

1. Pull last month's ClassPass reservations and payouts from your dashboard, and divide revenue by visits to get your true payout per visit.
2. Calculate your direct revenue per member visit (monthly fee divided by average visits per member).
3. List every class that was more than 70% full while ClassPass spots were open; cap ClassPass in those classes first.
4. Add a "How did you find us?" question and a ClassPass tick-box to your intro offer, so conversions can be counted.
5. Read your agreement for the five clauses in the warning box, and write down the opt-out date.

## FAQ

**Q: How much does ClassPass pay a yoga studio per visit?**
A: It varies by studio, class and contract, and rates are confidential. A 2020 VICE investigation reported yoga studio averages around $10 to $12 and lows of $7, but that is dated. Ask for your floor in writing and divide your actual payouts by visits.

**Q: Can I contact ClassPass users directly?**
A: Check your agreement. A competitor's summary of ClassPass's partner FAQ says partners cannot send dedicated offers to ClassPass users, and the Mindbody article says marketing goes only to consumers who opted in. Use in-studio signs and your own intro offer rather than assuming you have their contact details.

**Q: Will ClassPass hurt my membership sales?**
A: It can if it fills peak classes or teaches regulars to book through the marketplace. The cannibalisation row in the math above shows how to test it. Cap spots and track displaced visits.

**Q: What if I want to leave ClassPass?**
A: The second-hand summary mentions a 90-day opt-out window. Confirm your own notice period and whether leaving affects class listings or visitors with existing credits.

**Q: Should a new studio start with ClassPass or its own membership?**
A: Start with your own intro offer and a Business Profile, then consider ClassPass for off-peak classes if the empty seats cost you more than they earn. Do not make it your only source of visits.
