A Facebook lead for a gym is cheap. A new member is not. LocaliQ's 2026 benchmarks put a Health & Fitness lead-form lead at $27.11 on average. If 40% of those leads show up to a trial and a third of trial visitors join, that $27 lead becomes a member who cost you about $205 in ads. Move the show-up rate or the close rate and the same ad account looks brilliant or broken. This guide is about the whole chain from ad to signed member, because the ad is only the first link.

It is written for gym and studio owners in the US, UK, Australia and Canada. Dollar benchmarks are US data. For other currencies, run the same math with your own numbers. The Google side of the funnel is covered in our [Google Ads for gyms guide](/blog/google-ads-for-gyms).

| --- | --- |
| Traditional gym | $14 to $20 |
| CrossFit box | $18 to $26 |

Treat that as a second-hand ladder, not a quote. The direction is believable: the more specialised and expensive the offer, the more a lead costs. Webtonic also gives a fitness Facebook CPC of $0.46 to $1.10 (credited to SearchLab and FoundryCRO), well under LocaliQ's $1.84 for lead campaigns. The gap is a good illustration of why you should treat every benchmark as a range to test against, not a promise.

Many other figures in that roundup, such as trial show-up rates and January price rises, have no named source, so we do not use them as facts here.

## Who Is on the Platform

Pew Research's [2025 social media fact sheet](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/fact-sheet/social-media/) (5,022 US adults, February to June 2025) says Facebook and YouTube are the most widely used platforms in the US and that half of US adults use Instagram. It also shows a split that matters for studio owners:

- Facebook: used by 78% of women and 63% of men.
- Instagram: used by 80% of adults aged 18 to 29 but only 19% of those 65 and over.

If your core members are women in their 30s to 50s, Facebook placements deserve real budget. If you sell to 20-somethings, the Instagram placements matter more. Let Meta deliver across placements at first, then read the breakdown report to see where your leads actually came from.

## Step 1: Pick the Lead Form That Matches Your Phone Capacity

For a local gym the cheapest way to collect leads is usually an instant form, which keeps people inside Facebook. Jon Loomer's [guide to instant forms](https://www.jonloomer.com/instant-forms/) lists three form types:

- **More Volume** (the default): quick to fill in and submit.
- **Higher Intent**: adds a review step before submission, which exists to improve lead quality.
- **Rich Creative**: replaces the standard intro with a longer pitch that can include how-it-works steps, social proof cards and an incentive.

The guide says custom questions may be your best tool for managing lead quality, and that conditional logic can route people to an ending where their details are not submitted. Third-party guides say Higher Intent cuts raw lead volume, but we found no verified figure, so test it yourself: run the same ad with both form types for two weeks and compare cost per attended trial, not cost per lead.

Good gym form questions: name, mobile number, email, a preferred class time (multiple choice), and one qualifier such as "Have you trained before?" Skip long essays. A phone number you can text in minutes is worth more than ten fields.

## Step 2: Build the Audience Simply

Set a radius around your door based on where your last 50 members live. Pull their zip or post codes and look at the map. Dense city: a few miles. Suburbs: further. Add a broad age range that fits your classes and let Meta find the likely joiners. Add interest layers only if lead quality is poor. If you keep a list of members, upload it as an exclusion so you stop paying to advertise to people who already joined. Our [fitness studio Facebook targeting guide](/blog/fitness-studio-facebook-ads-targeting) goes deeper on audiences.

## Step 3: Creative That Looks Like Your Studio

Film short vertical clips on a phone: a warm-up, a coach correcting form, members finishing. Ask members for permission and for one sentence about what they were nervous about before they joined. Put the offer and neighbourhood in the headline ("Free first class in [neighbourhood]") and use captions, since many people watch with sound off. See our [Facebook ads creative guide](/blog/facebook-ads-creative-guide) for hook ideas.

### Check Meta's rules before you write the claim

Meta's [health and wellness policy](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/health-wellness) says weight loss and dietary products must be targeted to people 18 and over, and it prohibits close-ups of body areas being pinched for fat. It also bans statements of inferiority about physical appearance. The policy exempts products and services marketed for general wellbeing, such as fitness services and health clubs, from the age restrictions. In practice:

- Say "beginner-friendly classes with a coach", not "fix your belly fat".
- Do not target insecurities in the copy, even implicitly.
- Skip dramatic transformation photos until you have checked the current policy text. Third-party blogs disagree on whether Meta changed its before-and-after rules in July 2026, and Meta's own page does not settle it for fitness classes.

Policies move, so read the live page before you launch.

## Step 4: Choose an Offer People Will Say Yes To

| Offer | Why it may work | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Free trial class or week | Low risk for newcomers | Some leads never show up |
| Discounted intro month | Commits people sooner | Can attract price-only shoppers |
| Bring a friend | Social, supports retention | Needs a clear way to claim |
| Free assessment or consultation | Suits personal training | More effort for the lead |
| Class pack | Fits drop-in styles | Make expiry terms clear |

Test offers one at a time with similar creative and audience. Judge each on cost per attended trial.

## Step 5: Tracking and Follow-Up

Install the Meta Pixel on your sign-up or booking page. Meta's [Conversions API page](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/marketing-api/conversions-api/) says server events are processed like events sent with the Pixel, and mentions deduplication when you confirm events are received, so if you use both, check in Events Manager that events are not counted twice. For instant forms, tracking is simpler: the lead arrives in Meta or your CRM, and your front desk marks who attended and who joined.

Then fix speed. Text or call every new lead within minutes. It is the cheapest improvement available and costs nothing in ad spend. Our [landing page best practices](/blog/landing-page-best-practices) help if you send traffic to a page instead of a form.

Most gym ad accounts do not fail at the click. They fail at the phone: slow replies, no reminder text before the trial, and no follow-up with people who did not attend. Fix those before you raise the budget.
</Callout>

## Retargeting: Follow Up With Interested People

Build audiences of website visitors who viewed pricing or the timetable, people who opened a lead form but did not submit, and video viewers. Show them a different message from the first ad, such as a member story or a reminder of the free class, and exclude people who already joined. A small retargeting budget, around a fifth of spend, is a reasonable starting assumption (our rule of thumb, not a benchmark).

## The Math

A $1,000 monthly budget under three scenarios. The CPL in the Base column is set to the LocaliQ Health & Fitness figure; every other input is an **assumption** to replace with your own numbers after a month of data.

| | Tough | Base | Strong |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Monthly budget (assumption) | $1,000 | $1,000 | $1,000 |
| Cost per lead | $45 (assumption) | $27 (LocaliQ: $27.11) | $20 (assumption) |
| Leads | 22 | 37 | 50 |
| Lead to attended trial (assumption) | 30% | 40% | 50% |
| Attended trials | 6.7 | 14.8 | 25 |
| Cost per attended trial | $150 | $68 | $40 |
| Trial to member (assumption) | 25% | 33% | 40% |
| New members | 1.7 | 4.9 | 10 |
| Ad cost per member | $600 | $205 | $100 |
| Monthly fee (assumption) | $55 | $55 | $55 |
| Months of fees to repay ad cost | 10.9 | 3.7 | 1.8 |

Payback uses revenue, not profit, so real payback is slower once staff and rent are counted. If your average member leaves after six months, the Tough column loses money and the Base column survives comfortably.

Two things follow.

- **Your break-even lead price.** Maximum cost per lead equals the most you will pay for a member, multiplied by show-up rate and close rate. If you will pay $200 per member: $200 x 0.40 x 0.33 = $26. A $27 lead is right at the line, so your show-up and close rates decide whether the ads make money.
- **Show-up and close rates are the biggest levers.** Moving show-up from 30% to 50% cuts cost per member by 40% without changing the ad at all. Reminder texts and a warm welcome do that, not new creative.

## A 30-Day Plan

**Week 1: Foundation.** Choose one offer, one radius, one lead form. Film 5 to 8 short clips.
**Week 2: Launch.** Run one ad set with 3 creatives. Do not edit for the first few days. Check that leads reach you.
**Week 3: Retarget and refine.** Add retargeting. Pause ads that spend without leads.
**Week 4: Review.** Work out cost per attended trial and per member. Decide whether to scale, test a new offer or fix follow-up first.

Pair this with search. [Google vs Facebook ads for local business](/blog/google-ads-vs-facebook-ads-local-business) explains how the two channels split the work, and our [Facebook ads for fitness studios](/blog/facebook-ads-for-fitness-studios) piece covers further creative angles.

## Do This Today

1. Pull your last 50 new members, note their zip or post codes, and draw your ad radius from the map.
2. Work out your monthly fee, average months retained and the most you would pay for one new member. Use the break-even formula above.
3. Build one instant form (More Volume or Higher Intent) with name, mobile, preferred class time and one qualifying question.
4. Film five phone clips of real classes, then check your claims against Meta's health and wellness policy page.
5. Set a 10-minute reply rule for every lead and a reminder text 24 hours before each trial.

## FAQ

**Q: How much does it cost to run Facebook ads for a gym?**
A: LocaliQ's 2026 Health & Fitness lead average is $27.11 per lead and $1.84 per click. Webtonic's compiled figures run lower, around $14 to $20 per lead for traditional gyms, but are second-hand. Use $20 to $45 as a test range, then substitute your own data.

**Q: What budget should a single studio start with?**
A: There is no official figure. Start with an amount you can sustain for four to six weeks and that buys at least a few dozen leads at the Base CPL. At $27 a lead, $1,000 buys about 37.

**Q: Is Higher Intent better than More Volume?**
A: Higher Intent adds a review step to improve lead quality. Whether it improves cost per attended trial for you is a test, not a rule. Compare both on attended trials.

**Q: Can I show before-and-after photos?**
A: Check Meta's current health and wellness policy first. The policy restricts close-ups of pinched body areas and statements of inferiority, but coverage of transformation photos for fitness classes is unclear, and secondary sources conflict.

**Q: Facebook or Instagram for a gym?**
A: Pew's 2025 data shows Facebook skews to women (78% vs 63% of men) and Instagram to younger adults (80% of 18 to 29s). Let Meta run both, then read the placement report.
