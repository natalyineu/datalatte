A hair salon Facebook ads strategy rests on three things: tracking that shows which ads lead to bookings, one clear offer, and a cost ceiling worked out from what a new client is worth. The ceiling is gross profit per client multiplied by the share of leads who become attended clients.

In the worked example below, every input is an assumption. That gives about $51 per lead, and $600 a month roughly breaks even over six months. Rebooking and fast replies move the result more than any ad setting.

No published cost benchmark is quoted in this update, because none could be verified against a source. Use the sums below with your own numbers and your own currency.

## How much can a salon afford to pay for a new client?

The affordable cost comes from the salon's own books, not from an industry average. Start with gross profit per client over a fixed period, then multiply by the share of leads who turn up and pay.

The inputs: an $85 average ticket, 3 visits in six months and a 50% gross margin. That gives $85 x 3 x 0.50 = $127.50 gross profit per client. With a 40% lead-to-client rate, the maximum cost per lead is $127.50 x 0.40 = $51.

Now a $600 monthly budget at an assumed $50 per lead. The table shows how the lead-to-client rate alone changes the outcome.

| Assumption set | Weak follow-up | Baseline | Strong follow-up |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Monthly ad spend | $600 | $600 | $600 |
| Cost per lead | $50 | $50 | $50 |
| Leads | 12 | 12 | 12 |
| Lead-to-client rate | 25% | 40% | 50% |
| New clients | 3 | 4.8 | 6 |
| Ad cost per new client | $200 | $125 | $100 |
| Gross profit after six months | $382.50 | $612 | $765 |
| Gross profit minus ad spend | -$217.50 | +$12 | +$165 |

The ad account is identical in all three columns. Only the share of leads who book and attend changes, which is why replies and rebooking matter as much as the creative.

## What tracking should be in place before spending?

Without tracking, platform reports show leads but not which ones became paying clients. Set this up before the first dollar goes out.

- Install the Meta Pixel on the booking website, using the site builder's built-in integration if there is one.
- Add an event for a completed booking, or for a click on the booking button if the booking happens on another platform.
- Ask the website platform or developer about the Conversions API as a second route for the same events. If both are used, the setup needs deduplication so one booking is not counted twice.
- Make a test booking, open Events Manager and confirm the event appears once.

Platform data only goes so far, so add an offline layer. Put "How did you hear about us?" on the booking form and give each ad its own offer code. The front desk then knows which leads sat in the chair.

Some booking platforms support coupon codes directly. Trafft, for example, [lists coupons among its features](https://trafft.com/salon-ads/), which makes code-based attribution simpler. Check whatever system the salon already uses before adding another tool.

## How should a salon choose its ad radius and audience?

Most salon clients live or work a short trip away. Pull the postcodes or zip codes of recent new clients and look at the spread. Set the ad radius in Ads Manager around the address to match that spread, not the whole county.

Meta changes its location options over time, so check what the chosen objective currently offers. Keep age and interest targeting broad and let the creative do the filtering. A photo of balayage attracts people who want balayage.

With a client list and permission to use it, build a custom audience from it. Exclude current clients from new-client offers and give them a separate message, such as a rebooking reminder.

## Which offers are worth testing first?

The offer usually carries more weight than the audience. Test one at a time, so a result can be traced to a cause.

| Offer idea | Why it can work | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| New-client consultation with a stylist | Low commitment, builds trust | Needs a quick-to-book calendar |
| Free add-on (gloss, scalp treatment) with a first colour | Protects the service price | Costs product and time |
| Service-led ad (balayage, extensions, bridal trial) | Reaches higher-ticket buyers | Needs real photos of that exact service |
| Quiet-slot offer (weekday mornings) | Fills empty chairs | Regulars may learn to wait for deals |

For creative, use the salon's own work: before-and-after photos, a short video of a finish, a stylist introducing herself. Get client consent first. State what the offer includes, the price and the end date. One idea and one button per ad.

Local creators can supply extra content too. The guide to [influencer marketing for hair salons](/blog/influencer-marketing-for-hair-salons-how-to-get-free-bookings-from-local-creator) explains how to approach them.

## Which objective and budget suit a small salon?

If tracking works, choose a leads or sales (conversions) objective so Meta can optimise for bookings. If tracking is not ready, send traffic to the booking page and fix tracking in the first week. Traffic campaigns do not report leads in the same way, so their results cannot be compared directly with lead campaigns.

For budget, work backwards from the ceiling above. If gross profit per client is $127.50 and 40% of leads convert, paying more than about $51 per lead loses money over six months. The [Facebook ads budget guide](/blog/facebook-ads-budget-local-business-how-much-to-spend) goes through spend levels for local businesses.

Small budgets generate few conversion events, so spreading them across many ad sets starves each one. Run one or two ad sets and judge on cost per booking rather than on likes, comments or reach.

AI tools can speed up drafting ad copy variations. The [ChatGPT prompts for local business marketing](/blog/chatgpt-prompts-for-local-business-marketing) article has starting points, but a person should still check every claim and price.

## How should enquiries and near-bookers be followed up?

An ad that generates leads can still lose money if nobody replies. Answer every enquiry the same day, and faster during opening hours. If the booking page is online, make the first available slot visible. If bookings come by phone, make sure someone answers the number in the ad.

Online booking can also shorten the path. Trafft's page lists [Reserve with Google](https://trafft.com/salon-ads/), described as accepting bookings via Google Search and Maps. That is one way to capture people who see the ad and then search the salon's name.

Most people do not book the first time they see an ad. With the pixel running, build an audience of people who visited the booking page or engaged with the page. Show them a different message: a client review, a before-and-after of the service they viewed, or a note about limited slots.

Rotate the creative so the same ad does not follow them around.

## How often should results be reviewed?

Check Ads Manager on a fixed day each week and look at three things:

1. Cost per lead or booking, which is the number that matters.
2. Which ad and audience produced the most bookings.
3. Click-through rate, as a sign of whether the creative gets attention.

Pause the weakest ad, keep the best and test one new thing. Changing everything at once makes the result impossible to read.

Set the test window and a written continue-or-stop number before launch, for example a maximum cost per booking taken from the ceiling above. Giving an ad a fair run before judging it avoids cutting a campaign after one quiet week.

## What mistakes waste a salon's ad budget?

- Targeting a whole county, when clients will not travel that far for a haircut.
- Spending first and arguing about results later, because no tracking was set up.
- Sending traffic to a slow or confusing booking page. Test it on a phone using mobile data.
- Judging a creative on reach or likes instead of bookings.
- Discounting too deeply. A free add-on usually protects pricing better than a large percentage cut.
- Skipping rebooking at checkout, even though the second and third visits produce most of the profit in the example above.

A practical order for this week: decide the radius from recent client postcodes, work out gross profit per client and the cost-per-lead ceiling, test the booking link on a phone, install the pixel and a booking event, then launch one offer with real photos and an offer code.

## FAQ

### How much does a lead cost for a hair salon on Facebook?

It depends on the offer, area, creative and booking page, so the reliable figure is the salon's own result from a short test. This article uses an assumed $50 per lead for the worked example. Compare any real result with the ceiling of gross profit per client multiplied by the lead-to-client rate.

### Do I really need the Meta Pixel?

Ads can run without it, but Meta cannot optimise for bookings and there is no way to see which ads produce them. A booking event on the pixel, plus a "how did you hear about us" question, gives the clearest picture. Ask the website developer about the Conversions API as an addition.

### How long should an ad run before a decision is made?

Long enough to collect a meaningful number of leads, with the window and the stop number written down before launch. Small salons generate few events, so judge on cost per booking rather than on early clicks.

### Should I boost posts or use Ads Manager?

Ads Manager suits booking campaigns, because it gives more control over objective and tracking than the boost button.

### Is Facebook or Instagram better for a salon?

Meta ads can run on both from one account. Test placements together first, then compare results by placement once there are enough bookings to read.
