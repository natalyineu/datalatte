## What should be settled before opening marketing starts?

TGP International states that a significant percentage of cafés fail within their first 12–24 months worldwide, and that the most common reason is poor pre-opening preparation rather than bad coffee ([TGP International](https://www.tgpinternational.com/blog/the-ultimate-caf%C3%A9-opening-checklist-a-complete-pre-launch-guide)). The source gives no exact percentage, so none is quoted here. The point for marketing is that promotion cannot fix an unclear concept.

Before any message goes out, the concept needs a clear answer to three questions from the same checklist: who the core customer is, when they visit, and why they would choose this café over competitors. The checklist also recommends mapping the full customer journey, from discovery to repeat visits.

Those answers decide the message. A grab-and-go espresso bar promotes speed and a morning route. A community café promotes a place to stay. Mixing both in one announcement usually weakens each.

The same source says every touchpoint should reinforce one narrative: menus, uniforms, signage, social media and staff behaviour. For marketing, that means the opening posts, the window sign and the counter script should sound like the same business.

## What does the 30-day plan look like?

The schedule below is a suggested structure, not a sourced benchmark. It places the soft opening, which TGP International treats as a budget item, ahead of the public launch so that problems surface before a promotion sends crowds in.

| Days | Focus | Actions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 to 10 | Foundations | Fix the concept and customer, claim and complete the Google Business Profile, prepare photos, menu and hours, set the marketing budget |
| 11 to 17 | Soft opening | Invite neighbours, nearby workplaces and friends, test service speed and menu, ask for honest feedback and first reviews |
| 18 to 24 | Grand opening | Announce the date and one clear offer, post daily, ask every guest how they heard about the shop |
| 25 to 30 | Repeat visits | Collect contact details with consent, offer a reason to return, reply to every review, review the numbers |

The order matters because awareness spent before the listing and menu are ready sends people to an unfinished page. Build the base first, then push people toward it. ## How should the opening budget be set?

TGP International's pre-opening budget list names four items: fit-out overruns, training wages, soft opening costs and initial marketing spend. It also says to always keep a contingency buffer ([TGP International](https://www.tgpinternational.com/blog/the-ultimate-caf%C3%A9-opening-checklist-a-complete-pre-launch-guide)).

Two practical points follow. Marketing is the line most often squeezed when the fit-out runs over, so it helps to set the figure early and treat it as committed. The contingency buffer should not be quietly spent on promotion either.

The soft opening is also a cost, because staff are paid and stock is used while revenue is limited. Its value is information: how long drinks take, which menu items slow the bar, and where queues form. A grand opening that exposes those problems to a crowd costs more than the soft opening would have.

A sensible split is to decide the total, then divide it between what is spent on the soft opening, what goes to paid promotion and what is held back for the first two weeks after launch. The example further down uses assumed figures to show how that total can be tested against the shop's own numbers.

## Which numbers should be known before spending on promotion?

The same checklist advises understanding the numbers before opening: average transaction value, daily customer counts, and monthly fixed against variable costs. Together these define the break-even point and pricing strategy.

Promotion spend only makes sense against those figures. If the break-even point needs a certain number of transactions a day, the opening campaign has a job: bring in enough first-time visitors, and enough of them returning, to close the gap faster.

The source also warns that uncontrolled cost of goods sold is one of the fastest ways cafés lose margin. An opening offer that discounts the highest-cost drinks can attract visitors who cost more than they bring in. Discount the items with the best margin, or bundle a drink with a pastry, rather than cutting the price of the most expensive item.

Without a real sales history, the first month's figures are estimates. Treat the opening campaign as a way to learn the actual average transaction and visit pattern, then update the break-even calculation after the first month.

## What might a small opening budget need to achieve?

The inputs below are all **assumptions**, chosen to show the method. They are not benchmarks for any café.

| Input | Value | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Opening marketing budget | $600 | Assumption |
| Monthly fixed costs | $9,000 | Assumption |
| Average transaction value | $8.00 | Assumption |
| Variable cost per transaction | $2.50 | Assumption |
| Contribution per transaction | $5.50 | $8.00 minus $2.50 |
| Break-even transactions per month | about 1,637 | $9,000 divided by $5.50 |
| Break-even transactions per day | about 55 | 1,637 divided by 30 days |
| Transactions to recover the marketing spend | about 110 | $600 divided by $5.50 |
| Visits per new customer in the first month | 4 | Assumption |
| New customers needed to recover the spend | about 28 | 110 divided by 4 |
| Maximum spend per new customer | about $21 | $600 divided by 28 |

On these assumptions, the café needs about 55 transactions a day to cover fixed costs, and the opening campaign must bring roughly 28 customers who return four times to pay for itself within the month. Any channel that costs more than about $21 per new customer would not break even in that window.

If the real figures differ, the method still holds. Swap in actual rent, wages, ticket size and cost of goods, and the ceiling on spend per new customer changes accordingly.

## How do you get early reviews and word of mouth?

Early reviews come from asking in person while the guest is still in the shop. Staff should be trained during the soft opening to make the request naturally, and the review link should be easy to open on a phone. The [Google Business Profile tips for coffee shops](/blog/google-business-profile-coffee-shop-tips) go into the profile side of this.

Check the review platform's current policy before offering any reward for reviews. Many platforms restrict incentives, and a promotion built around paid or rewarded reviews can put the listing at risk. The safe approach is to ask, make it easy, and reply to every review that arrives.

Word of mouth also comes from the neighbourhood. Soft opening invitations to nearby offices, shops and residents give those people a reason to mention the café, and the feedback from that session can be fixed before the public launch.

A short script helps staff. One line thanking the guest, one line asking for a review, and one line pointing to the link at the counter is enough. If guests ask what the café is about, the concept statement from the first ten days gives staff a consistent answer.

## Which channels suit a coffee shop opening?

Channel choice should follow the customer profile from the first step. A commuter-focused bar needs visibility on maps and in local searches around the morning route. A lifestyle café may do better with photo-led posts and a clear opening date on social media.

Free channels come first: the business listing, a window or sidewalk announcement, posts with the opening date and offer, and direct invitations to neighbours. Paid promotion can follow once the listing, menu and photos are complete, and it should stay small until visit data shows what works.

## How do you turn opening visitors into regulars?

TGP International's advice to map the journey from discovery to repeat visits applies directly here. The grand opening gets the first visit; the days after it decide whether there is a second.

Collect an email address or phone number at the counter with clear consent, and give a reason to return soon, such as a small offer valid for the following two weeks. Keep the offer on items with healthy margins, as covered above.

At the end of the 30 days, review what happened: reviews gained, profile views, calls and directions, visits by source, and cost per new customer. Compare the cost per new customer with the ceiling calculated in the worked example, and drop whatever did not clear it.

Then update the break-even figures with real transactions and ticket sizes. From that point, marketing becomes a monthly routine rather than a launch event.

## FAQ

### When should a coffee shop start opening marketing?

Foundations such as the business listing, photos and menu can start as soon as the concept and customer are clear. The 30-day plan above begins with them and reaches the public launch in the second half of the month.

### Is a soft opening necessary?

TGP International lists soft opening costs as a pre-opening budget item, which suggests it is a standard step. It lets staff and menu be tested before a promotion sends larger crowds.

### How much should a new coffee shop spend on opening marketing?

No source gives a standard figure. Set the amount as a budget line before opening, keep a contingency buffer, and test it against your own break-even numbers, as in the worked example.

### Should the opening offer be a discount?

Only if the offer targets items with healthy margins. TGP International warns that uncontrolled cost of goods sold is one of the fastest ways cafés lose margin, so a deep discount on high-cost drinks can hurt more than it helps.
