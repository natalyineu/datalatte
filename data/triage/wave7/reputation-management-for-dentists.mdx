Reputation management for dentists means keeping a steady flow of recent reviews, answering each one in public, and keeping the details patients see on your listing accurate. Patients read reviews before they book, so your profile often works as the first step of your front desk. A 2026 BrightLocal survey, cited by [Adwave](https://adwave.com/resources/reputation-management-for-service-businesses) (secondary), found that 74% of consumers prioritize reviews from the last three months. Review timing therefore matters almost as much as your star average. This guide covers response habits, review requests, platform pricing and a worked budget example.

## Why do patients read reviews before booking?

Review platforms have grown large enough that patients expect to find you there. Trustpilot reports more than 60 million monthly active users and says more than 200,000 reviews are written daily, according to [Adwave's summary of Trustpilot's figures](https://adwave.com/resources/reputation-management-for-service-businesses) (secondary). Trustpilot also says 7.8 million fake reviews were detected and removed in 2025, so platforms do screen for manipulation.

Recency matters as much as volume. The BrightLocal figure shows most consumers weigh recent feedback heavily. A practice with many older reviews and few new ones can look less current than one with steady new feedback, so requests should run continuously rather than in a single push.

Patients also notice when reviews look staged. Moz's local review survey found that 50% of consumers lose trust if owners or employees appear to be reviewing their own business. Staff should never post reviews for the practice, even with good intentions.

## How should a dental practice respond to negative reviews?

Respond publicly, briefly, and without arguing about clinical details. Future patients read the reply as well as the review, so the reply shows how the office handles problems. Acknowledge the experience, name the next step and keep the tone calm.

A simple template works for most cases: "Thank you for the feedback, [Name]. Your concern about [issue] has been passed to the office manager. Please contact [email] directly so the team can look into your visit."

Move detailed discussion of treatment or billing off the public thread. Do not confirm patient information in a public reply. Before you flag any review, check the platform's live policy page, because removal rules differ between sites and change over time.

## Does Google Q&A count as reputation management?

Google's Q&A section sits apart from the review tools many dental offices use. A [Google Business Profile automation guide](https://dmly.io/google-business-profile-automation-guide/) (secondary) notes that the Q&A section under a listing is not one of the five Reputation tabs. Answering a customer's question therefore happens on the listing itself, not inside the profile management tool.

Add Q&A to your weekly routine alongside review replies. Questions about parking, new-patient intake or emergency appointments are quick to answer, and an unanswered question is a visible gap on the listing. Check Google's current help pages for how questions display, because the interface changes.

## What does a worked budget look like for a two-location practice?

- Plan: GatherUp Multiple Locations at $60 per location per month, billed annually (published price)

- First-year value of one new patient: $500 (assumption)

The calculations follow from those inputs:

- Monthly tool cost: 2 × $60 = $120
- Annual tool cost: $120 × 12 = $1,440
- Patients from reviews per month: 2 × 4 = 8 (assumption-based)

Under these assumptions, the tool pays for itself with fewer than one new patient a month. If your value per patient is lower or your review flow is weaker, the result changes. ## How do you build a steady review flow?

Ask at the right moment, then keep asking. Request a review at checkout or as the patient leaves, and send a text with a direct link the same day. [AI agents for Google reviews management](/blog/ai-agent-for-google-reviews-management) can automate the follow-up, but the request still needs to sound like your office.

Adwave's comparison suggests reviews take 1-3 months to show results, so judge the process over a full quarter rather than a few weeks. Use this checklist to keep the routine consistent:

1. Confirm the business name, address and phone number match across your website and listing.
2. Ask for a review at checkout and send a text with a direct link.
3. Reply to each new review, positive or negative, as soon as you can.
4. Log review dates so you can see whether recent feedback is steady.
5. Keep staff from posting reviews about the practice.
6. Check the Q&A section every week.

Practices with several sites face the same steps at larger scale. The guide on [multi-location social media management](/blog/multi-location-social-media-management) covers how to keep location details consistent across channels.

## FAQ

### How long before reviews change new patient numbers?

Adwave's channel comparison lists reviews and reputation management as taking 1-3 months to show results. Set expectations for about a quarter rather than a few weeks. After that window, track new patients by source and compare the trend against your review dates.

### Should I flag fake or unfair reviews?

Yes, if a review breaks the platform's rules. Trustpilot says it detected and removed 7.8 million fake reviews in 2025, which shows platforms do filter suspicious content. Use each platform's live reporting page and name the specific rule. A review that is negative but truthful should get a professional reply, not a flag.

### Which tool should a single-location practice start with?

The published GatherUp Local Businesses plan is $99 per month, billed annually, for one location. Reputation.com starts at $80 per month with no free tier, according to CostBench. Compare the features each plan covers, then check live prices, since listings change.

### Can I ask for reviews on several platforms at once?

Start with the platform your patients use most when they search. Requests across several sites can work, but check each platform's terms first, because the rules on soliciting reviews vary. Whatever you choose, keep the requests fair and consistent.
