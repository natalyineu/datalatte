Content marketing for a local business means publishing useful material, such as photos, short videos, customer reviews and Google Business Profile updates, that helps nearby people choose you. It works when each piece answers a question a local customer is already asking and gives them a reason to book, visit or call. Start with one clear offer, publish in formats you can sustain, reuse each post across channels, and measure bookings rather than likes. The sections below cover strategy, a repurposing routine, a worked cost example and where paid streaming TV ads fit.

## What should a local business publish first?

Start with the questions customers ask before they buy. For a salon, that might be which treatment suits fine hair, how long a colour lasts, or whether there is parking nearby. Write the answers down, then turn each one into a post, a photo caption or a short video. Your unique selling point belongs in every piece: a specialty, a neighbourhood connection, or a service that other businesses in your area do not offer.

Keep the scope narrow. A bakery with one location does not need a content calendar covering ten topics. Pick two or three themes tied to what you sell and what people search for near you, then build from there. Many customers type "near me" queries, so place your neighbourhood and town names in titles, captions and profile descriptions where they are accurate. Avoid stuffing place names into every line, because the text should still read naturally to a person standing at your counter.

Your first month is a test. Publish the same kind of post on the same theme several times, then look at which ones bring people in. Keep the themes that produce bookings or store visits and drop the ones that only collect views.

## Which content formats work for a local shop?

Five formats cover most local needs. Photos of finished work and the team build trust fastest. Short video shows process and atmosphere in a way a static image cannot. Customer reviews and user-generated content show other people choosing you. Profile posts keep your Google Business Profile current. Email reaches people who already know you and want to hear from you again. Pick the formats your current customers already use and skip the rest until those are working.

Salon-specific guidance is covered in the [salon marketing guide](/blog/best-marketing-for-salons-in-2026-what-actually-works-with-real-examples), which compares channels using examples from that trade. The same logic applies to most service businesses: the format matters less than whether the post answers a real question and points to a next step.

Ask for permission before reposting a customer's photo or quote. Reply to reviews the way you would answer a question at the counter: thank the person, address anything specific they raised, and keep the reply short. A visible, polite reply often reads as more trustworthy than a polished slogan.

## How do you repurpose one post into many?

A single post can become several pieces with little extra work. Batch them in one sitting rather than writing each one from scratch. Use a blog post or a filmed video as the anchor, then build the rest from it. The routine below keeps the anchor piece central and makes each derived item link back to it.

1. Write or film the anchor piece with one clear call to action, such as booking, calling or visiting.
2. Pull three or four short quotes from it and turn each into a simple image.
3. Cut a 30 to 60 second vertical clip that covers one point from the anchor.
4. Write a short social post that links back to the anchor.
5. Add a paragraph to your next email to your list, with a link.
6. Publish a Google Business Profile update that names your town or neighbourhood and links to the anchor.
7. Print a one-page checklist or a card with a QR code for the counter.
8. Ask one regular customer for a comment or photo, with their permission, and reshare it.

Batching is what makes this manageable. Writing the anchor and all its derivatives in one session usually takes less time than producing the same number of unrelated posts across the week.

## What does a repurposing week cost in practice?

The example below uses a hair salon. Every input is an assumption, so replace each one with your own figures before relying on the result. The purpose is to show the arithmetic, not to predict results for your business.

| Input | Value | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Anchor posts per month | 4 | assumption |
| Labour per post, including repurposing | 3 hours | assumption |
| Labour rate | $40 per hour | assumption |
| Visits per anchor post | 600 | assumption |
| Share of visitors who book | 3% | assumption |
| Average appointment value | $75 | assumption |

Labour comes to 4 posts multiplied by 3 hours, which is 12 hours, costing $480 a month at $40 an hour. The four posts together draw 2,400 visits. At a 3% booking rate, that is 72 bookings. At $75 each, the bookings represent $5,400 in revenue. Labour cost per booking is $480 divided by 72, or about $6.67, and booked revenue is about 11.25 times the labour cost.

These are gross figures. They ignore product costs, staff time beyond your own labour, cancellations and no-shows. Treat them as a template: change one input at a time and see which one moves the result most. In most salons, the booking rate and the average appointment value will matter more than the number of posts.

## Should a local business buy streaming TV ads?

Most local businesses should not start with streaming TV ads. Amazon describes streaming TV (STV) as the digital distribution of video content across devices with an internet connection, rather than through a cable signal. A secondary agency summary of Amazon's pitch reports that self-service buying has no minimum spend, but that agencies typically recommend a starting budget of around $10,000 a month to generate enough impressions for meaningful optimization. The same summary says managed-service DSP starts at $50K.

## Which metrics show content is working?

Track booked appointments, not likes. Give each anchor post its own promo code or a separate landing link, and ask customers at checkout how they found you. Log the answers in a spreadsheet. A post that brought in six bookings in a month is more useful to know about than a post that collected a hundred likes and no visits.

Check Google Search Console for the local queries you are targeting. Its Performance report shows which searches display your pages and which ones earn clicks. If a query sits at the bottom of page one or just past it, a targeted update to an existing page is often faster than a new post. Compare your labour cost per booking month to month, using the same method as the worked example.

## FAQ

### How often should a local business publish?

Choose a schedule you can keep for several months. One solid post a week plus regular profile updates usually beats a burst of posts that stops after two weeks. No benchmark from the sources here sets a required frequency, so test your own response rate and adjust from there.

### Do Google Business Profile posts help local content marketing?

They keep the profile current and give you a place to link to an anchor post. Check Google's current Business Profile help pages for which post types and features are supported, because those change over time.

### Are streaming TV ads realistic for a small shop?

Usually not at the start. A secondary agency summary puts the typical self-service starting budget at around $10,000 a month, and self-service access requires Brand Registry eligibility. Build owned channels and local search first, then reconsider video advertising once those are producing bookings.

### What video length should I make for repurposed clips?

If you later adapt a clip for Amazon Streaming TV, a secondary summary cites MP4 files with durations between 6 and 45 seconds and a 15 Mbps minimum bitrate. Confirm these specifications on Amazon's live help pages before you produce the file.

### How do I know a post brought customers in?

Give each anchor post its own promo code or landing link, and ask at checkout how the customer heard about you. Compare the bookings each post generated against the labour you spent making it, using the same cost-per-booking method as the worked example above.
