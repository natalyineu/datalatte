For most hair salons, podcast ads are a second-step test, not a first channel. Spots are typically 15 to 60 seconds long and run inside episodes, according to [Tinuiti's guide to Spotify ads](https://tinuiti.com/blog/tv-audio/spotify-ads/). The audience is real: 37% of Americans listen to podcasts monthly, per a podcast statistics source cited by [StackAdapt](https://www.stackadapt.com/resources/blog/audio-advertising). A salon, though, draws clients from a few miles, so most national reach is wasted. Start with a local show, track with a unique offer code, and keep the first test small.

## Are Podcast Ads Worth Testing for a Hair Salon?

They are worth testing when listeners can become nearby clients. StackAdapt notes that podcast and music streaming listeners opt in to the content, which gives it an inherent trust. A host reading an offer carries more weight than a banner that scrolls past.

Compare that with search. Salon Guru says it has created and managed hair salon advertising campaigns across the UK, Ireland and the USA, and its Google Ads packages include monthly management and analysis costs for 3 months. People searching now for a stylist are already close to booking. Booksy states that over 90% of consumers check reviews before trying a new stylist, a claim it makes without a third-party source, so treat it as directional. A complete profile, covered in the [Google Business Profile posts guide for hair salons](/blog/hair-salon-marketing-google-my-business-posts), catches those searchers first. Podcast ads work best as an add-on that builds familiarity in the same local area.

## What Format and Length Should the Ad Use?

Tinuiti describes podcast ad spots as typically 15 to 60 seconds. Voices, cited by StackAdapt, says a fully-produced podcast ad can run 15 to 60 seconds and is often more conversational and casual than a radio ad. That suits a short, friendly invitation from the salon, not a price list.

Spotify's free tier uses shorter spots. ClicksGeek describes 15 to 30 second audio ads that play between songs and podcast episodes on the free tier. Shorter spots leave less room for an offer, so state the code early and repeat it at the end.

Short video works differently. Booksy recommends clips of about 15 seconds to explain at-home maintenance or how to use a styling tool. Those clips can point listeners to the same offer on social channels, as covered in the [Instagram marketing guide for hair salons](/blog/instagram-marketing-for-hair-salons).

## Who Should the Ad Reach?

Podcast audiences are not salon audiences, so targeting matters. Bookeo advises targeting women aged 21 to 55 who live nearby and show interest in hair care and beauty. That guidance is written for salon social ads, but it gives a starting profile for judging whether a show's listeners match it. Bookeo also says consumers aged 45 to 54 make up 21.9% of the salon market, and consumers aged 55 and older account for 30% of the market share while spending the most on hair care services.

Booksy's example shows the local precision that social ads can offer: a "Bridal Styling" Meta ad aimed at newly engaged women within a 10-mile radius of the salon. Confirm whether a podcast buy offers radius or location targeting before assuming it does. For a local creator option, see [influencer marketing for hair salons](/blog/influencer-marketing-for-hair-salons-how-to-get-free-bookings-from-local-creator).

The figures cited here do not include verified podcast sponsorship rates, so this article does not quote a price per thousand downloads. Ask each show for its current rate card. ## How Much Could a $300 Local Test Return?

All inputs below are assumptions for illustration. They are not benchmarks for salons or for any podcast.

| Item | Input or calculation | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sponsorship price (assumption) | Chosen for the test | $300 |
| Episode downloads in month one (assumption) | Assumed audience | 8,000 |
| Implied cost per 1,000 downloads | $300 / 8 | $37.50 |
| Listeners who live nearby (assumption) | 25% of 8,000 | 2,000 |
| Redemption of unique code (assumption) | 0.5% of 2,000 | 10 new clients |
| Cost per new client | $300 / 10 | $30 |
| First-visit revenue (assumption) | Average first ticket | $90 |
| Revenue from new clients | 10 x $90 | $900 |

At a 0.5% redemption rate, the test brings $900 in first-visit revenue against $300 spent. At 0.2%, the same spend yields 4 new clients at $75 each and $360 in first-visit revenue, barely above cost. The redemption rate, not the download count, decides the result.

Repeat visits raise the return further. Booksy states, citing its own data, that engaged loyalty members visit salons 2.4x more often. A new client who returns is worth more than the single $90 visit shown above.

## How Should a Salon Track and Judge the Test?

Give each show its own code or landing page. Booksy's example referral offer gives an existing client $20 off their next service and a new friend 20% off their first visit. A paired offer like that can be tracked by code, so record which show each new client came from at booking.

Judge the test by outcomes, not reach. RŌZ advises judging a search test by search terms, qualified calls or forms, completed consultations and booked appointments, not by clicks alone. The same logic applies here: count booked appointments tied to each code, not downloads. ARC notes that its salon figures come from one salon in one market and that yours will differ, so treat any single result as a starting point.

If one show produces clear bookings, test a second show with a new code. If it does not, stop and move the budget to the channels already working.
