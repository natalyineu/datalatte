Customer lifetime value (CLV) is the total revenue a single customer is expected to bring over the whole time they do business with you. The common formula multiplies average purchase value by purchase frequency and by average customer lifespan. [AdWave's CLV guide](https://adwave.com/resources/customer-lifetime-value-small-business) uses a coffee shop example: $5 per visit, four visits a week for three years, which gives $3,120 in total revenue from one person. To judge that figure, compare it with what you spend to win the customer, and use profit rather than revenue for the decision.

## What Is the Formula for Customer Lifetime Value?

AdWave defines the simple version as average purchase value x purchase frequency x average customer lifespan. Its illustrations show how the same formula fits different trades. A hair salon with a $75 average ticket, 6 visits a year and 4 years of retention reaches a CLV of $1,800. An HVAC example with a $350 average service call, 1.5 calls a year and an 8-year lifespan reaches $4,200.

Use one time period throughout. If purchases are counted per month, the lifespan must also be in months. Mixing a monthly frequency with a yearly lifespan is the most common error in home-grown CLV sheets.

Rejoiner's RFM guide describes frequency and monetary value in the same way, as totals across a customer's relationship with the business. That framing is useful when you rank customers, because it separates people who buy often from people who spend a lot.

## How Do You Test CLV Against Acquisition Cost?

CLV on its own does not tell you whether to spend more. You need to compare it with customer acquisition cost (CAC), which is what you spend to win one new customer. AdWave notes that a CLV:CAC ratio above 5:1 can signal that a business is underinvesting in growth. A ratio that high suggests room to spend more on acquisition.

Other rules of thumb exist, but they come from different businesses. LivePlan's guidance for SaaS companies sets lifetime value above three times acquisition cost, which is a software benchmark, not a local-trade one. AdWave also cites American Eagle's view that keeping CAC below 20% of CLV is a successful approach. Treat these as starting points and check them against your own margins.

AdWave's gym example makes the logic concrete. A member paying $50 a month for 24 months is worth $1,200, so spending $100 to $200 to acquire that member makes sense in its example.

## Worked Example: A Salon With Assumed Inputs

Every input below is an **assumption** chosen for illustration. Replace them with your own figures before using the result.

| Input | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Average ticket (assumption) | $80 |
| Visits per year (assumption) | 6 |
| Relationship length (assumption) | 3 years |
| Revenue CLV | $80 x 6 x 3 = $1,440 |
| Gross margin (assumption) | 55% |
| Profit CLV | $1,440 x 55% = $792 |
| Cost to win one client (assumption) | $60 |
| Profit CLV to CAC | 13.2 to 1 |

At 13.2 to 1, this salon sits well above the 5:1 level that AdWave links to underinvestment. That suggests paid acquisition could go further, but only if the extra clients also keep their visit pattern.

Now test a retention change. Suppose reminders add one visit a year. Revenue CLV becomes $80 x 7 x 3 = $1,680, and profit CLV at the same margin is $924, a gain of $132 per client. If reminders cost $20 a month (assumption) and 40 clients (assumption) benefit, the lifetime gain is $5,280 against about $240 a year in software cost. The gain only holds if the extra visit really happens, so track it.

## Which Local Businesses Have the Highest Lifetime Value?

Lifetime value varies widely by trade. AdWave estimates that a loyal pet owner is often worth $500 to $1,000 or more per year, and that a loyal med spa client who returns quarterly can reach $5,000 to $10,000 or more. These are illustrations drawn from that vendor's industry pages, not averages from a survey.

For mobile trades, the vendor Get Ryze says HVAC and landscaping businesses often see 2 to 3 times higher CLV than their initial project value. Its guidance also tracks Local Services Ads customer value over 12 to 24 months. That window is useful: a one-year view can undercount repeat service work.

Your own numbers matter more than any industry figure. Pull the last 12 to 24 months of invoices for past customers, group them by first service, and compute CLV for each group.

## How Can You Raise Customer Lifetime Value?

Each lever maps to one input of the formula. Order value, visit frequency and lifespan are the three places to look. Referrals are one route, since AdWave, citing Wharton research, says referred customers have 16% higher lifetime value than non-referred customers. Ask happy customers for introductions at the moment they are most satisfied.

Email follow-up is another. AdWave's Columbia-based figures show personalizing promotional-email frequency increased average CLV by 8.3% over baseline under an optimized policy. Its comparison table estimates a 25 to 50% CLV increase from purchase-history and lifecycle personalization. Treat these as directional, because they come from a vendor summary and a specific experiment.

Post-purchase messages matter too. AdWave says customers who convert through Google ads and enter an email nurture sequence deliver 2 to 5 times more lifetime value than those left without follow-up. For seasonal repeat visits, the [holiday marketing strategy for local business](/blog/holiday-marketing-strategy-local-business) shows how to time those prompts.

Engagement can feed retention as well. AdWave cites Fuze32 benchmarks suggesting brands that keep engagement above 1% see a 15% to 25% CLV uplift, though that is a third-party claim. For a local audience built from scratch, the [Instagram growth strategy for local businesses](/blog/instagram-growth-strategy-local-business-0-to-1000-followers) covers the early steps.

## Which Mistakes Distort Lifetime Value?

- Using revenue when the decision needs profit.
- Mixing time periods, such as monthly frequency with a yearly lifespan.
- Averaging regulars and one-time visitors together, which hides how each group behaves.
- Leaving out discounts and the cost of acquisition.
- Assuming a lifespan you have not checked against real customer records.
- Copying an industry figure from a different trade or a different country without adjustment.

## FAQ

### What is customer lifetime value in simple terms?

It is the total value a customer brings across all their purchases with your business over the time they stay with you.

### What is a good CLV for a small business?

No single number applies to every business. Compare your profit-based CLV with acquisition cost. AdWave flags a CLV:CAC ratio above 5:1 as a sign that growth spending may be too low.

### Should I use revenue or profit in the calculation?

Use profit for decisions. Revenue CLV is a useful first step, but margin determines how much you can afford to spend to acquire a customer.

### How do I estimate lifespan with little data?

Check what share of customers from one or two years ago still buy, then estimate a typical relationship length. Revise the estimate as more records come in.

### Is retention cheaper than acquisition?

Often it is. Harvard Business Review, as summarized by AdWave, cites a 5% retention increase lifting profits by 25% to 95%, although that figure dates from 2014 and is secondary.
