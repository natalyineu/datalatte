Choose website colors by the job each one does, not by mood. Keep the palette to three to five colors, following the range that [Adwave recommends](https://adwave.com/resources/how-to-choose-brand-colors-that-resonate-with-your-audience), and give each color one role: text and background, brand, and a single accent for the main button. People form an initial judgment about a brand in about 90 seconds, according to the same article, so the palette needs to be clear before a visitor scrolls. Then check that every text and background pair is readable on a phone, because only 31% of mobile sites meet minimum color-contrast requirements in the 2025 Web Almanac.

This guide is for owners picking or fixing a site palette for a cafe, salon, studio or trade business in the US, UK, Australia or Canada. It covers how many colors to use, how to split them across a page, how to check readability and a worked palette audit you can repeat each year.

## Does Color Psychology Decide Whether Visitors Trust a Site?

Less than many guides suggest, and the evidence is mixed. Adwave's brand color article cites, through Canva's summary of Institute for Color Research work, a figure that 62% to 90% of the initial assessment can be based on color alone. Those are secondary figures, and they describe first impressions, not conversions or trust in a specific service.

The same article notes that people form an initial judgment in about 90 seconds. That matters more for a local site than the choice of hue. A visitor deciding whether a salon or repair shop looks legitimate is reacting to the whole page: the contrast, the spacing, the photos and whether the booking button is easy to find. Pick colors that fit your premises and products, then test them with real customers rather than trusting a rule like "blue builds trust." Consistency across the site, the shopfront and the social profiles is what helps people recognise you.

## How Many Colors Should a Local Site Use?

Three to five, and fewer is usually easier to maintain. [Adwave's brand color guidance](https://adwave.com/resources/how-to-choose-brand-colors-that-resonate-with-your-audience) recommends keeping a brand palette to three to five colors. Its budget identity guidance is tighter: limit the palette to two to three primary colors plus one or two neutrals, as [explained in its budget visual identity article](https://adwave.com/resources/creating-a-visual-identity-for-your-business-on-a-budget).

A small business can start even leaner. Adwave's guide to building a website on a small budget recommends picking one logo lockup, one primary color, one accent color and one heading and body font pair, as set out in [its 500-budget website guide](https://adwave.com/resources/how-to-build-a-business-website-on-a-500-budget). Neutrals for borders and secondary text still need to be readable, so count them as part of the palette check rather than as decoration.

## How Should You Split Colors Across a Page?

Adwave recommends a 60/30/10 split, with 60% primary, 30% secondary and 10% accent, as the basis for the cleanest brand palettes, according to its [brand color article](https://adwave.com/resources/how-to-choose-brand-colors-that-resonate-with-your-audience). In practice, the primary covers most of the page background, the secondary covers headers and large blocks, and the accent appears only on the main call to action such as Book, Call or Order.

The same source recommends revisiting the palette on a quarterly cadence as the brand grows. For a local business, that review can happen alongside the menu, price or service list update you already do. Check whether the accent is still used only for actions, and whether new photos or signage have pulled in a color that is not in the palette.

## How Do You Check Text Readability?

Test every pair that carries text: body copy on the background, headings, button labels, footer links and any text on a photo. Use a free contrast checker and enter the exact hex codes from your builder or theme. Some brand color tools also let you enter an exact brand hex code, which avoids guessing from a screenshot.

Light brand colors are the usual problem. A pale accent can look elegant in a design file yet become hard to read as a button label. Underline text links so the link is not identified by color alone. The 2025 Web Almanac figure that only 31% of mobile sites meet minimum color-contrast requirements suggests most local sites have room to improve. Check on a phone, outdoors if you can, because screens and lighting change how contrast feels. ## Worked Palette Audit for a Cafe (Assumptions)

The inputs below are assumptions for illustration, not measured results. A cafe has seven colors on its current site and wants to reduce them to four.

| Step | What to check | Example (assumption) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. List current colors | Every hex code in use, including old promo colors | 7 colors found on the site |
| 2. Cut to 3 to 5 | Keep one job per color | Keep dark brown text, brand brown, cream background and caramel accent |
| 3. Apply 60/30/10 | Map primary, secondary and accent to page areas | Cream 60% background, brown 30% headings and header, caramel 10% for the Book button |
| 4. Check readability | Contrast checker on every text pair | Caramel label on the button retested with dark text |
| 5. Compare with rivals | Audit three to five competitors the audience compares you against | Three nearby cafes checked (assumption) |
| 6. Schedule review | Quarterly check against new signage and photos | Next review set for the first week of each quarter |

The 60/30/10 mapping here is one way to apply the split, not the only valid one. The key result is that the caramel is now confined to the single action button, which is what the one-job rule requires. Try the same six steps for a salon or trade business by swapping in your own colors and competitors.

## How Do You Test Colors With Real Visitors?

Run the audit, then watch what people do. Heatmap tools record clicks, mouse movements and scroll behavior, then color the page by frequency to show what gets attention and what gets ignored, as described in [Adwave's heatmap guide](https://adwave.com/resources/heatmaps-for-small-business-understanding-how-visitors-use-your-site). If the main button gets few clicks, check its contrast and position before changing its color again.

Ask five customers or staff members to find the booking or call button on a phone and time how long it takes. If they struggle, change the layout or contrast first, not the hue. Test one change at a time so you know what worked. For structured tests of headlines or button colors, read our [A/B testing guide for local businesses](/blog/a-b-testing-for-local-business). For the first screen, see [above-the-fold design](/blog/above-the-fold-design-local-business), since the accent button often sits there.

## FAQ

### What are the best colors for a local business website?

No single color is verified as best. Choose three to five colors that suit your premises and audience, give each one job, and check that all text pairs are readable.

### Does blue build trust?

There is no reliable rule that blue builds trust. Treat any claim about a specific color and emotion as a design habit, and test your palette with real visitors.

### Can I use a light brand color for buttons?

Only if the label stays readable. A light accent can fail as a button background, so darken it or switch the label to a dark color, then retest.

### How often should I review the palette?

Adwave recommends revisiting the palette on a quarterly cadence as the brand grows. Pair the review with menu, price or signage updates so it is easy to remember.

### How do I test my colors?

Enter the exact hex codes into a contrast checker, review the site on a phone, and ask a few customers to complete one task, such as booking. Change one thing at a time.
