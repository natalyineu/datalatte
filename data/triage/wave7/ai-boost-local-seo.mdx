To do AI local keyword research without invented numbers, use an AI assistant to brainstorm phrases and group them by intent, then take every demand figure from a data source. Google Keyword Planner, Search Console and your Business Profile data are the places to check. [Zapier](https://zapier.com/blog/best-keyword-research-tool/) says Keyword Planner is free even if you never buy Google Ads, and [Ahrefs](https://ahrefs.com/seo/local-seo) notes it shows broad search volume ranges rather than exact counts. Treat AI output as a list of ideas to test, not as search volumes.

This workflow suits single-location businesses such as cafes, salons, groomers and studios in the US, UK, Australia and Canada.

## What does local keyword research need to answer?

Local keyword research means finding the words people near you use for what you sell, such as "dog groomer near me" or "balayage Leeds". Three questions matter. Intent asks whether the searcher is ready to book, comparing options or just learning. Location asks which suburbs and neighbourhoods you genuinely serve. Competition asks who already ranks and whether your page can appear beside them.

Answer those three before you choose any phrase. A phrase can look promising and still be wrong if nobody in your area types it. Local visibility also depends on your profile, so the same service names should appear on your website and in your Business Profile details. The [local keyword research guide](/blog/local-keyword-research-guide) covers the fundamentals in more depth.

## Where does AI help, and where should data take over?

AI is good at speed and sorting. It can produce a wide list of phrasings, group them and draft an outline in minutes. It is poor at anything that needs measured data, such as how many people search a phrase or how often a page appears for it today. Those figures need a tool that measures them.

| Task | AI useful? | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Brainstorm phrases and questions | Yes | Fast and wide |
| Group keywords by intent or page | Yes | Saves spreadsheet time |
| Draft outlines and FAQs | Yes | Edit for accuracy |
| Search volume | No | Use Keyword Planner |
| Current rankings | No | Check Search Console or a rank tool |
| Knowing your neighbourhoods | Partly | You know them better than any model |

Use the table as a rule of thumb for your workflow. If a number did not come from a tool that measured it, it does not belong in your plan.

## How do you seed the list without asking AI for volumes?

Start with your own knowledge. Write down each service, each area you serve and ten questions customers ask on the phone or at the counter. Then give those notes to an assistant with a prompt that asks for phrasing only:

> I run a dog grooming salon in [suburb, city]. Services: [list]. Generate 50 phrases a local customer might type into Google, grouped as ready to book, comparing options, and learning. Include nearby suburb variations. Do not give search volumes or difficulty scores.

The last sentence does the most work. Assistants will attach confident-looking volumes to every phrase if you let them, and those numbers have no source. Keep the best 30 to 60 phrases and move on to real data.

## Which free tools show real demand?

Several free tools are enough for one location. [Wildheart Media](https://wildheartmedia.com/keyword-research-yoga-retreats/) lists Google Search, Google Trends and Google Keyword Planner as free options for keyword research without paid SEO software. [BoostFloral](https://www.boostfloral.com/blogs/using-google-ads-to-market-your-flower-shop-website) advises starting with Keyword Planner to find keywords related to your business. Expect ranges, not exact counts. Keyword Planner shows figures such as 1K–10K, so use them to compare phrases against each other rather than to forecast precise traffic. Search Console's Performance report shows which queries already bring your site impressions, and your Business Profile data shows the searches that surfaced your listing. Paid suites exist too. ## How do you cluster keywords into pages without making doorway pages?

Paste your cleaned list into the assistant and ask it to group phrases so each group can be targeted by one page. Ask it to name each group and suggest a page title. Then check the result yourself. Typical groups include a service page (puppy grooming), a question page (how often to groom a dog), a price or comparison page (mobile versus salon grooming) and an area page only where you have something specific to say.

Do not create near-identical town pages with only the place name swapped. A single strong service page with real local detail, such as parking, nearby landmarks and the suburbs you actually cover, usually does more work than a set of thin copies. If you run paid search later, [Google Ads](https://business.google.com/en-all/ad-tools/keyword-planner/) lets you create multiple campaigns in the same account without opening a new account, so each cluster can sit in its own campaign.

## How should you score keywords and choose five to ten?

Score each phrase from 0 to 2 on four questions. Relevance asks whether you sell exactly this. Intent asks whether a searcher would book soon. Competition asks what page one looks like when you search the phrase on your phone; if it is all national brands and directories, a narrower phrase will be easier. Effort asks whether you already have a page you could improve.

Add the scores and take the top five to ten phrases. Scoring is a judgement tool, not a published metric, so keep the scale simple and apply it the same way each time. Improve existing pages before writing new ones, because an existing page already has history that a new one lacks. ## How do you place the phrase on the page?

Put the main phrase where a reader would expect it: the page title, the main heading, the opening paragraph, a few subheadings, image alt text and internal links. [Adwave](https://adwave.com/resources/how-to-update-old-blog-posts-to-boost-search-rankings) cites Faith Hanan's guidance on blog updates, which suggests the main keyword appear in at least one-third of H2 headings but no more than 60%. Treat that as a secondary, practitioner-level guideline rather than a rule, and check your own headings for natural wording.

Then add what AI cannot know: parking, the nearest landmark, the suburbs you cover and what a first visit involves. Read every AI draft before it goes live, correct it and remove any claim you cannot back up. Repeating the phrase in every sentence does not help a reader, and it makes the page harder to trust.

## What does the math look like for one phrase?

Is a keyword worth a page? Here is a worked example for a hypothetical groomer. Every input below is an assumption, included only to show how the numbers multiply. Replace them with your Keyword Planner, Search Console and booking data.

| Input or result | Weak phrase | Good phrase |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monthly searches (assumption) | 40 | 90 |
| Share of searches that click through to you (assumption) | 5% | 10% |
| Visits a month | 2 | 9 |
| Visit-to-booking rate (assumption) | 5% | 10% |
| Bookings a month | 0.1 | 0.9 |
| Value of a new client over a year (assumption) | $250 | $250 |
| Value per month | $25 | $225 |

Demand, click share and booking rate multiply, so a smaller phrase with strong intent can beat a larger one with weak intent. If the page costs three hours at $30 an hour (assumption), that is $90. At these inputs the good phrase repays it in under half a month, and the weak phrase takes about 3.6 months.

Never paste AI-estimated volumes into a plan. If a number did not come from Keyword Planner, Search Console, your Business Profile or a paid tool, label it as an assumption.
</Callout>

## What should you do this week?

1. List your services, the areas you genuinely serve and ten questions customers ask you.
2. Run the seeding prompt and keep the best 30 to 60 phrases, excluding any numbers the assistant offered.
3. Check Keyword Planner for those phrases and note the ranges. Open Search Console and add any queries you already appear for that look relevant.
4. Cluster the list, score each phrase on the four questions and pick five to ten priorities.
5. Update one existing page's title, main heading and opening paragraph, then check that your Business Profile lists the same service names. The [local citation guide](/blog/local-citation-building-small-business-guide) explains how consistent listings support that work.

## How do you measure whether it worked?

Track four things monthly: Search Console impressions, clicks and average position for your target queries; Business Profile calls, direction requests and website clicks; form fills or call taps on your site; and a search from your own area on a phone to see what appears. Write down the date of every change, so you can match results to edits later.

Judge progress over several months rather than a few days. Local rankings move for many reasons, including competitor changes and new reviews, so one fortnight of data rarely proves a single edit worked. ## FAQ

### Can ChatGPT replace a keyword research tool?

No. Use an assistant for ideas, grouping and outlines. It cannot give reliable search volumes or current rankings, so pair it with Keyword Planner, Search Console and your Business Profile data.

### How many keywords should a small local business target?

Start with five to ten priority phrases tied to your main services and areas. Expand once those pages are solid and generating enquiries.

### Do I need a paid SEO tool at the start?

Usually not. Keyword Planner, Search Console and autocomplete cover most single-location needs. Paid suites such as SEMrush are more useful once you are acting on the free data consistently.

### How long does local keyword work take to show results?

There is no fixed timeline. Profile and page fixes can show earlier than new content, so track changes across several months and keep a dated log.
