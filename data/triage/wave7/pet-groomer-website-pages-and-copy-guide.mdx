A pet groomer website needs six core pages: Home, Services and Prices, Booking, About the Groomers, Gallery and Reviews, and Contact and Location. Each page answers one question an owner asks before calling. Home shows who you are and where you are. Services shows a starting price. Booking takes the appointment. The other pages build trust and remove doubt. Add an FAQ and a policies page once the six are live. Booking software is the cheap part; the words on these pages decide whether a visitor books.

This guide is for groomers and mobile groomers in the US, UK, Australia and Canada.

## Which pages does a pet groomer website need?

Six pages cover the questions owners ask before they book. Each page should have one job and one next step.

- Home: who you are, where you are, and a Book button.
- Services and prices: what each groom includes and a "from" price.
- Booking: a short form, the details you need, and what happens next.
- About: the people handling the animals, their training and their safety routine.
- Gallery and reviews: before-and-after photos with consent, plus recent reviews.
- Contact and location: address, phone, hours, parking and service area.

After launch, add an FAQ page and a policies page. Add area pages only when each one holds local detail that is genuinely different, such as your travel fee or the neighbourhoods you cover. Area pages that only swap a town name read as thin content and add little for a visitor.

## What should the home page say in the first few seconds?

A visitor should know who you are, where you are and what to do next without scrolling. Use a headline that names the service and the place, such as "Calm, careful dog grooming in [Town]." Follow it with one differentiator you can prove: small-breed work, cat-friendly handling, a mobile van, or one dog at a time. Only claim what you actually do.

Competitors show how plain copy works. Groombuggy's Anaheim page opens its mobile pitch with "Forget the 4-hour trips to the groomer." Zooming Room In lists Mobile Spa & Grooming, Nail Trimming, Bathing & Washing and Deshedding as separate services. Clear service names are easier to scan than slogans.

Put your Book button, address and phone number as live text, not inside an image. Skip generic lines such as "where every pet is family," because any competitor could use them.

## How should services and prices appear?

Owners want a price range before they call. Show "from" prices and explain what changes them. Replace the placeholders with your real figures.

| Service | Includes | Price guide |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bath and brush | Bath, blow dry, brush out, ears, nails | From $X by size and coat |
| Full groom | Bath plus haircut, face and feet tidy | From $X by breed and condition |
| Nail trim | Short appointment or walk-in | $X |
| Add-ons | De-shedding, teeth brushing, flea treatment | $X each |

Explain the factors in one line each: size, coat type, matting and temperament. If every groom is quoted individually, say how you quote, for example from a photo or a short consultation. One benefit-led sentence per service works better than a list of adjectives, such as: "Nails are trimmed with a grinder or clippers, whichever your dog finds calmer."

## Which booking details should the form ask for?

A booking form should be short. A widget or a few fields work better than a questionnaire. Ask for the pet's name, breed, size, last groom date, and any health or behaviour notes.

State your vaccination rule exactly as you enforce it. Show how long each appointment takes, and send a confirmation message so owners know the request went through. If you take bookings by phone or message, state your reply time, and only include a time you can actually keep.

Booking tools differ in cost and features, and many groomers start with a form on their own site. Whichever you choose, make the path from the home page to the booking step a single click.

## How do you show trust on the about and gallery pages?

Trust is the product, so the About page should show who handles the animals. Use a real photo of you and your team. List training and certifications you genuinely hold, and describe how you handle nervous or senior pets. Explain your safety routine for drying, supervision and cleaning between animals. A short personal note about why you became a groomer helps owners feel they know you.

Before-and-after photos show skill faster than paragraphs. Get written permission from owners, show different breeds and coat types, and caption each photo with the breed and the service. Quote recent reviews word for word and link to your Google Business Profile. Do not edit a review so it says more than the client wrote.

## Which policies and extra pages are worth adding?

Policies prevent awkward conversations at the front desk. Cover cancellation, late arrivals, vaccination proof, your behaviour policy, matting fees and accepted payment methods. Put each one in plain sentences a client can read in under a minute.

An FAQ page should answer what you hear every week: whether you groom cats, how long a visit takes, what happens with heavy matting, whether owners can stay, and how cancellations work. Short tips posts on shedding, nail care and coat care also build trust and give people a reason to visit the site beyond booking.

## What copy works, and which claims need a check?

Plain copy converts better. Adwave's summary of a benchmark roundup, credited to Genesys Growth, reports that landing pages written at a 5th to 7th grade reading level convert at 11.1%, compared with 5.3% for copy at a college reading level (secondary). Write short sentences and use "your dog" rather than "canines."

Wording of calls to action matters too. Adwave's copy guidance, citing Rob Palmer, reports tests where first-person CTAs outperformed second-person CTAs by 25% to 90% (secondary). A separate CTA-copy experiment, cited by Adwave from Invesp, attributed a 48% increase in form fills to a copy change at a 98.5% confidence level (secondary). Test "Book my dog's visit" against "Book your dog's visit" on your own page before you rely on either result. Tools like [AI marketing tools for small business](/blog/ai-marketing-tools-for-small-business) can draft variants, but a person should check them.

Health and outcome claims need a human check. Adwave advises assigning a human reviewer to final copy and scrutinising any personal or outcome claim in regulated fields (secondary). For grooming, treat claims such as "calms anxious dogs" or "gentle on sensitive skin" as statements you must be able to back up. Only promise a practice, such as cage-free drying, if you follow it every day.

Sample copy to adapt:

Home headline: "Calm, careful dog grooming in [Town]."

Sub-headline: "One groomer per dog and a quiet room, if that is true for your shop."

Policy line: "Vaccination records are required before the first visit. Upload them when you book or bring them to drop-off."

Replace every line with what is true in your business. The same testing logic applies to CTA copy as it does to email, which is covered in [A/B testing emails for small business](/blog/ab-testing-emails-small-business-guide).

## How do you track which pages bring in bookings?

Tracking shows which page earns the booking, so you can spend time on the right one. Google's guidance, as summarised by ChiroCandy (secondary), notes that some low-volume queries are omitted from the search terms report for privacy, so the report is not a complete list of every search. A keyword is part of your targeting setup, while a search term is what someone typed.

Conversion events also differ in value. A click on a phone number, a connected call, a submitted form and an attended appointment represent different stages. Google's conversion tools measure only the actions you configure, so the report is only as useful as the actions you choose to track. Start with one action, such as a submitted booking form, and add call tracking once that is working.

## What should you do first?

1. Write your real "from" prices for your four most-booked services, plus the factors that change them.
2. Write your vaccination, cancellation and matting policies as plain sentences you could say to a client's face.
3. Photograph five finished dogs or cats with owner permission, plus one photo of you and your setup.
4. Draft your home page headline, one differentiator and a Book button, using the sample copy above as a base.
5. Check every page on your phone, then confirm your address, phone and hours match your Google Business Profile exactly.

## FAQ

### Do I need a website if I already have social profiles?

Yes. Social profiles help with reach, but a website gives you a stable place for prices, policies and booking that you control. Platforms change their features; your own site does not disappear with them.

### Should I list my prices on the website?

Show "from" prices and the factors that change them. This lets visitors judge whether you suit them before they call. If every groom is quoted individually, explain how you quote.

### How many pages does a pet groomer website need?

Six core pages are enough to launch: Home, Services and Prices, Booking, About, Gallery and Reviews, and Contact and Location. Add an FAQ and policies page next, then tips posts and area pages as time allows.

### Can I rely on booking software's page instead of a website?

It can work as a start, but a site you own keeps your reviews, policies and search presence in one place. Link the booking page from your site so visitors can move between them easily.
