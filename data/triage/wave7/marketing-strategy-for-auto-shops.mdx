## What Monthly Budget Does a Repair Shop Need?

Adwave sets three tiers. A solo mechanic or 1-2 bay shop should plan $1,200-$3,000 a month. An established 3-6 bay shop should plan $3,000-$8,000. Within the smaller tier, Adwave allocates 60% to Google Ads and LSAs, which works out to $720-$1,800 a month. The rest is for other channels.

Adwave's separate guide for local shops gives a reasonable starting range for multi-channel advertising of $500-1,000 a month. That is a floor for testing rather than a target for a repair business, which tends to need more spend to fill a bay schedule.

Start at the lower end of your tier for two to three months. Track which campaigns produce calls that become booked jobs, then move money toward those. A [marketing dashboard](/blog/how-to-create-marketing-dashboard-small-business) that links ad spend to booked repairs makes this easier than reading ad platform reports alone.

## Which Google Searches Should a Repair Shop Target?

Near-me searches carry the most volume, but they are also expensive. The serpwars.com keyword table lists the following for US searches. These are third-party estimates, so check current figures in your own keyword tool before setting bids.

| car repair shops | 40,500 | $9.20 |

A secondary retelling of WordStream benchmarks, published on Adwave's mobile mechanic page, puts average auto repair cost per click on Google Search at about $3.90. The same retelling says auto repair has the lowest cost per lead of 23 industries studied, at about $28.50. Use these as directional checks against your own account rather than as targets.

Broad terms like "auto repair shop" can bring in shoppers far from your area. Use location settings and an ad schedule so you do not pay for calls you cannot answer.

## Are Local Services Ads Worth the Lead Cost?

Adwave's auto body guide gives a collision-repair benchmark: a well-run Google Ads campaign should generate leads at $30-$75 each, with close rates of 25-40%. Cost per click for collision keywords runs $5-$15, depending on the market. Those figures are for collision work, so a general repair shop may see different numbers.

Local Services Ads charge for leads rather than clicks, which makes lead cost easier to compare. Compare the cost per lead against what a booked job earns, not against what a click costs. A lead that books a $350 brake job is a different proposition from one that only asks about pricing.

For body shops, the same guide cites a 2024 Collision Industry Conference survey, retold by Adwave, showing that shops on direct repair programs reported labour rates 15-20% below retail. Price that into any insurer-network decision before you bid on insurer-linked terms.

## How Do You Turn One-Time Jobs into Repeat Customers?

Adwave advises following up 3-5 days after pickup with a text or email that links directly to your Google review page. A short message at that point asks for the review while the repair is fresh, and it also reminds the customer that you are available for the next service.

Adwave also suggests a $25-$50 gift card for customers who refer someone who completes a repair. Make the referral terms clear and keep the reward fixed, so it reads as a thank-you for the referral rather than a payment for a review.

Adwave's example puts the scale in context. A 10% response rate on a 1,000-customer base produces 100 appointments a year at zero acquisition cost, according to the page. Your own response rate will differ, so treat that as a model to test. ## What Is a Customer Worth, and What Can You Spend to Win One?

Adwave's lifetime value example is a driver who spends $800 a year over five years, which the vendor says makes that customer worth $4,000. In a separate calculation, Adwave says that with a $4,200 customer lifetime value and a 3:1 ratio, an auto repair shop could spend up to $1,400 to acquire one new customer.

Adwave also says spending $80-$200 to acquire a customer produces strong ROI, while most shops cap acquisition cost at $30-$50. Those two positions suggest different thresholds. The practical rule is to set your cap from your own repeat rate and average ticket, then check that the cap leaves room for the lifetime value you expect.

Lifetime value depends on how many repairs a customer returns for. Track that for a full year before you rely on any figure here.

## Worked Example: What Does $720 a Month Need to Return?

Every input below is an **assumption** or is labelled with its source. Replace them with your own numbers.

| Step | Input | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monthly ad budget | $1,200 (assumption, low end of Adwave's solo tier) | $1,200 |
| Search share | 60% (Adwave allocation) | $720 |
| New customers from search per month | 4 (assumption) | $180 per customer |
| Lifetime value | $4,000 (Adwave example: $800 a year for 5 years) | About 22 times the $180 cost (assumption-based) |
| Customers needed to meet a $50 cap | $720 divided by $50 | 15 per month |
| Customers needed to meet a $30 cap | $720 divided by $30 | 24 per month |

At 4 new customers a month, the shop pays $180 each. That clears the $1,400 ceiling Adwave's 3:1 calculation allows, but it misses the $30-$50 cap most shops use. Closing that gap means either raising customer count to 15-24 a month from the same budget or cutting the cost per customer through better landing pages and call handling. A shop that misses calls at busy hours is paying for leads it never converts.
