Facebook ads can bring new customers to a coffee shop when they reach people near the store, make one specific offer, and track what each new customer costs. Meta owns Facebook and Instagram, and both let advertisers set a budget for each ad and target detailed customer profiles. Most of the work is in three choices: the radius, the offer, and the budget. This guide covers each choice and includes a worked budget calculation with every input labelled.

## How do you set up Facebook ads for a coffee shop?

Start with the account. Restaurant HQ notes that Facebook and Instagram are both owned by Meta, which allows you to identify detailed customer profiles and set a budget for each ad ([Restaurant HQ](https://www.therestauranthq.com/operations/coffee-shop-marketing/), secondary). Keep the ad budget separate from your page's organic posting, so you can see which spend is producing visits.

Time matters as much as money. Bellwether Coffee lists Facebook at about 15 minutes per day of time investment for a coffee shop, as of 2026-05-03 ([Bellwether Coffee](https://bellwethercoffee.com/blog/social-media-marketing-for-coffee-shops)). That covers checking the page and replying to messages, not building campaigns. Campaign setup is a one-off task, so plan a block of time for it, then check the account on a regular schedule.

Before launching, confirm the ad points to a page where the offer is easy to find and redeem. A broken link or an unclear offer wastes the spend no matter how well the audience is chosen.

## Which people should the ads reach?

Geography is the strongest lever for a coffee shop. Adwave recommends setting a 1-3 mile radius around the shop for Reels promotions ([Adwave](https://adwave.com/resources/best-advertising-coffee-shops-cafes)). Its example for a new fall latte targets everyone within a five-mile radius of the store ([Adwave](https://adwave.com/resources/multi-channel-marketing-approach)). A tighter radius suits a shop with a dense neighbourhood around it; a wider one may suit a shop on a commuter route.

Age and location also matter. Pew Research, as cited by Restaurant HQ, reports that 75% of 30- to 49-year-olds use Facebook, 69% of 50- to 64-year-olds do, and 70% of rural social media users prefer Facebook ([Restaurant HQ](https://www.therestauranthq.com/operations/coffee-shop-marketing/), secondary). Check your own customer mix before assuming the same split. Use the platform's audience insights to see who is nearby.

Start with one radius and one audience, then widen only after the first results are clear.

## What offer should the ad make?

An offer gives the ad a reason to act now. Fresh Cup describes Partners Coffee pairing newsletter sign-ups with access to brewing resources and a 10% off coupon ([Fresh Cup](https://freshcup.com/a-breakdown-of-6-coffee-ads-and-why-they-work/), secondary). The pattern to copy is the pairing: a clear reward for a clear action, with a code that lets you trace who responded.

Name the product and the deadline in the ad. A vague line such as "save on coffee" gives the customer no reason to visit this week. Use a unique code or a note for staff to record each redemption, so the cost per new customer can be calculated later.

Change one thing at a time. Test the offer, then the image, then the radius, so you know which change moved the result.

## How much should a coffee shop budget for Facebook ads?

No source here sets a required budget, so the calculation below uses assumptions you should replace with your own figures. The aim is to show how the numbers connect, not to suggest a benchmark. Adwave's guide does give a planning range for a loyalty platform at $50-$300 per month, plus $200-$1,000 in incentive payouts such as free drinks ([Adwave](https://adwave.com/resources/best-advertising-coffee-shops-cafes)), which is useful for setting aside money for rewards alongside ads.

| Input or result | Value | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Facebook spend over 14 days | $400 | assumption |
| Offer redemptions | 40 | assumption |
| Average spend per visit | $6.50 | assumption |
| Cost per acquisition | $10 | $400 divided by 40 |
| First-visit revenue | $260 | 40 times $6.50 |
| First-visit ROAS | 0.65x | $260 divided by $400 |
| Revenue from regulars | $1,690 | assumption: 10 customers visiting weekly for 26 weeks at $6.50 |
| Total ROAS including regulars | 4.9x | ($260 plus $1,690) divided by $400 |

On the first visit alone, the campaign does not pay back. The result depends on repeat visits. Adwave's customer lifetime value example puts a customer spending $5 per visit who comes in four times a week for three years at $3,120 in total revenue ([Adwave](https://adwave.com/resources/customer-lifetime-value-small-business)). Its benchmark for coffee shops is $1,200-$7,000 in lifetime value.

## Should a coffee shop run ads on other platforms too?

Facebook is one option among several, and each takes different time. Bellwether Coffee lists daily time investments of about 15 minutes for Facebook, 30 minutes for Instagram, 1 hour for TikTok, and 5 minutes for Google Business, as of 2026-05-03 ([Bellwether Coffee](https://bellwethercoffee.com/blog/social-media-marketing-for-coffee-shops)).

| Platform | Listed daily time | Listed best use |
|---|---|---|
| Facebook | 15 minutes | Local community and events, skewing older |
| Instagram | 30 minutes | Visual brand and local discovery |
| TikTok | 1 hour | Growth and a younger audience, mostly 18-34 |
| Google Business | 5 minutes | Local search and reviews |

For a small team, the time column often decides the mix. Facebook ads plus a maintained Google Business Profile is a common starting pair, since one targets people by place and the other answers people searching for a coffee shop nearby.

## How should a coffee shop track whether the ads pay off?

Track two numbers. Cost per acquisition is ad spend divided by new customers. Return on ad spend, or ROAS, is revenue from those customers divided by ad spend. Adwave's connected TV example gives a simple illustration: $500 spent with $2,000 in attributed revenue yields a 4:1 ROAS ([Adwave](https://adwave.com/resources/ctv-measurement-attribution-guide)). That is a hypothetical example, not a benchmark for Facebook.

Count only customers you can tie to the campaign: a redeemed code, a tagged order, or a staff note at the till. Visits that cannot be linked to a campaign should be reported separately, so they don't inflate the result.

Look at repeat visits, not just the first one. A campaign that brings in a few regulars can outperform one that brings in many single visits.

## What about Google and local search?

Google Business Profile is where many people check a coffee shop before they walk in. Per Diem advises choosing the "Coffee shop" category or a related term rather than something broad such as "Restaurant" ([Per Diem](https://www.tryperdiem.com/blog/google-business-profile-coffee-shop-guide), secondary). It also notes that Google usually verifies a listing by postcard with a code, and that without verification your updates will not show publicly ([Per Diem](https://www.tryperdiem.com/blog/google-business-profile-coffee-shop-guide), secondary).

Search phrasing matters too. Salesforce UK gives examples such as "best coffee shop in your city" as long-tail, location-specific keywords ([Salesforce UK](https://www.salesforce.com/uk/marketing/local-marketing/?bc=OTH)). Seoplus, a vendor, states that a localised phrase like "coffee shop downtown Phoenix" is likely to have less competition than "coffee shop", which can lead to a lower cost per click ([seoplus+](https://seoplus.com/paid-ads/the-ultimate-guide-to-multi-location-ppc-advertising-on-google-ads/)). For a search-led approach, see the [Germany Google Ads guide](/blog/local-marketing-germany-small-business-google-ads).

## What should Canadian coffee shops consider?

Canada has a dense coffee market. Square, citing IBISWorld, says the country is home to over 8,000 coffee shops as of 2025 ([Square](https://squareup.com/ca/en/the-bottom-line/reaching-customers/marketing-plan-for-a-coffee-shop), secondary). Statista, as cited by Square, says seven in 10 Canadians use smartphones to access the internet, so a mobile-first offer is a reasonable default.

Square's 2025 Future of Commerce report, as cited there, found that 65% of Canadians like businesses to communicate with them by email, and 39% appreciate text message updates ([Square](https://squareup.com/ca/en/the-bottom-line/reaching-customers/marketing-plan-for-a-coffee-shop), secondary). Ads that send people to a sign-up for a message list can combine with those channels. ## FAQ

### Is Facebook still worth it for a coffee shop?

It can be, depending on who lives nearby. Pew data cited by Restaurant HQ shows 75% of 30- to 49-year-olds use Facebook ([Restaurant HQ](https://www.therestauranthq.com/operations/coffee-shop-marketing/), secondary). Check your own customer age mix before deciding.

### How long should an ad run before judging the results?

The sources here do not set a required test length. The worked example above uses 14 days as an assumption. Keep the test long enough to collect a clear count of redemptions, and check the platform's current help pages for guidance on learning periods.

### Do I need a Google Business Profile as well as Facebook ads?

Yes, if you want to be found in local search. Per Diem notes that without verification, updates will not show publicly and customers may not find the business ([Per Diem](https://www.tryperdiem.com/blog/google-business-profile-coffee-shop-guide), secondary).

### How much is a regular customer worth to a coffee shop?

Adwave's illustration of a $5 visit, four times a week for three years, totals $3,120 in revenue ([Adwave](https://adwave.com/resources/customer-lifetime-value-small-business)). Its benchmark for coffee shops is $1,200-$7,000. Use your own average spend and visit frequency to calculate your figure.
