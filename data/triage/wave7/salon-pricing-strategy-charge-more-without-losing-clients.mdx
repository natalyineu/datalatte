A salon price rise pays off as long as the revenue from the clients you keep is at least what you earned before. If revenue equals price times clients, an 8% rise breaks even at about 7.4% client loss. The formula is the rise divided by one plus the rise. Costs that change with each client move that threshold, so the real test uses your own numbers. The worked example below shows how to run that test for one chair and plan the announcement.

## What does break-even mean for a salon price rise?

[Adwave](https://adwave.com/resources/pricing-strategies-for-small-business-a-complete-guide) puts the idea simply: "Break-even tells you when revenue covers total costs." For a price rise, the question is narrower. At what point does the higher price, multiplied by the clients you still have, match what you earned before?

Take a rise of r. If you lose a share L of clients, revenue is unchanged when (1 + r) × (1 − L) = 1. Rearranged, L = r ÷ (1 + r). For an 8% rise, that is 0.08 ÷ 1.08, or about 7.4%. Lose more than that and total revenue falls below today's level.

Revenue is not profit. If a larger share of each ticket goes on variable costs such as product, card fees or commission, the rise is worth more per client, so the break-even loss is higher. Run that version with your own figures before deciding.

## How much client loss can an 8% rise absorb?

The table shows the break-even loss for common rises. The first column is revenue only. The second assumes variable costs are 10% of the current price, which is an assumption for illustration and should be replaced with your own.

| Price rise | Break-even client loss (revenue only) | Break-even client loss (variable cost 10% of price, assumption) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5% | 4.8% | 5.3% |
| 8% | 7.4% | 8.2% |
| 10% | 9.1% | 10.0% |
| 15% | 13.0% | 14.3% |

Read the 8% row as a limit, not a forecast. It tells you how many clients you can lose before the raise makes you worse off. It does not tell you how many clients will leave. That is why the tracking step later in this guide matters as much as the sum.

## Which clients are worth protecting during a raise?

Client loss is rarely spread evenly. Kitomba data, as cited on Lutily, says regular clients make up about 40% of the typical client base but account for 70% of all appointments. Losing a few regulars costs far more than losing a few occasional visitors.

## What does an 8% rise do to one chair?

Every input below is an assumption for illustration: 25 appointments a week, an average ticket of $90, 48 working weeks, and an 8% rise to $97.20. Replace each with your own figures. Current annual revenue is $108,000.

| | Lose 0% | Lose 5% | Lose 7.4% (break-even) | Lose 10% |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Appointments per week | 25 | 23.75 | 23.15 | 22.5 |
| Weekly revenue | $2,430 | $2,309 | $2,250 | $2,187 |
| Annual revenue (48 weeks) | $116,640 | $110,808 | $108,000 | $104,976 |
| Change vs. $108,000 today | +$8,640 | +$2,808 | $0 | −$3,024 |
| Chair time freed per week | 0 | 1.25 slots | 1.85 slots | 2.5 slots |

At the 7.4% row, revenue is exactly where it was. The freed slots only help if you can refill them. If the chair is not full, the constraint is demand, and a higher price will not solve it. Check that before announcing anything.

## How should you track client loss after the raise?

A basic weekly scorecard can track advertising spend, qualified inquiries, consultations booked, new-client appointments completed, revenue from those appointments and second appointments booked, according to [RŌZ's 2026 salon advertising guide](https://rozhair.com/blogs/education/hair-salon-advertising-ideas). Add a count of regulars who have not rebooked, because that is where a rise shows up first.

For lapsed clients, reactivation timing varies by vendor. [BonusQR](https://bonusqr.com/article/10-actionable-hair-salon-marketing-ideas-to-keep-your-chairs-full) suggests a "We miss you!" offer for clients who have not visited in 90 days. [Booksy](https://biz.booksy.com/blog/salon-marketing) suggests a CRM message at 12 weeks. Pick one window and test it.

The value of rebooking is easy to estimate. The [WAJ rebooking calculator](https://waj.ai/tools/rebooking-rate-calculator) example works out USD 2,700 from a 10-point rebooking increase: 600 clients × 10% × USD 45. With an assumed $90 ticket and 600 clients (both assumptions), the same formula gives $5,400.

## Which services should rise first?

Start with services that have a waiting list, because demand already exceeds supply there. Time-heavy services such as colour and long appointments use more chair time, so they can carry a larger rise than quick ones. Keep one or two entry-level services at today's price so new clients have a way in.

Fold mandatory extras into the menu price where every client pays them anyway. This is a recommendation, not a rule, and it avoids the surprise of an add-on at checkout.

If you were going to offer a discount to soften the rise, a package for regulars may do the job better. See [grooming package pricing strategy](/blog/grooming-package-pricing-strategy-increase-average-ticket) for how bundles can lift the average ticket. Loyalty perks have costs of their own. [Loopy Loyalty](https://blog.loopyloyalty.com/?p=1241) advises adding software, rewards, staff time, setup, promotion and tools, then working out the break-even point, before you launch one.

## How should you announce the new price?

Tell clients early, plainly, and in the channels you already use for bookings. A workable sequence:

1. Email or text your client list, and add a note to your booking page, several weeks before the change. The exact lead time is your choice; four to six weeks is an assumption.
2. Mention the change in person at each appointment before the date, and let clients rebook at today's price within the notice window.
3. On the day, update your online menu, Google Business Profile and social bios together.

A short message you can adapt:

> Hi [name], a quick note from [salon]. From [date], [service] moves from [old price] to [new price]. Prices have not changed since [month, year], while product, rent and staff costs have risen. Appointments already booked stay at today's price. To book your next visit before [date], reply here and [salon] will hold your usual slot.

For the same-day listing update, the [Google Business Profile post guide](/blog/hair-salon-marketing-google-my-business-posts) explains how to publish a change, and the [hair salon local SEO guide](/blog/hair-salon-local-seo-guide) covers the listing details to check.

## FAQ

### How much can I raise prices before clients leave?

No reliable public figure exists for how many salon clients leave after a rise, so treat any quoted number with caution. Use the break-even math instead: an 8% rise breaks even at about 7.4% revenue-based client loss. Then measure your own loss over a period you choose.

### Should I raise prices for existing clients or only new ones?

Most salons raise for everyone on a set date with a notice period. A two-tier list is harder to explain and harder to run. This is a recommendation, not a rule.

### Should I offer a discount to soften the rise?

A notice period and a chance to rebook at the old price is usually enough. Ongoing discounts erode the rise you just made. Cost any offer first, including staff time and setup, before deciding.

### Do I have to give notice of a price rise?

Advance notice is good practice and protects client relationships. Check your local consumer protection rules before changing prices on pre-paid packages or memberships, since those can carry their own terms.
