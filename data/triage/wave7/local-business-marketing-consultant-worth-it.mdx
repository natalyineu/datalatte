A marketing consultant is worth hiring when the fee costs less than the time or profit they free up, and when you can measure the result. Take a worked example with assumed inputs: if you spend 6 hours a week on marketing and value your time at $50 an hour (both assumptions), that is about $1,300 a month before any tools or ads. A $600 consultant who takes 4 of those hours off your plate would save about $267 a month, if the work is as good.

DataLatte sells marketing consulting, so it has an interest in this answer. This guide cites public sources where it uses a figure and labels everything else as an assumption. It is written for owners of local businesses in the US, UK, Australia and Canada.

## What Does the Four-Gate Test Check?

The test asks four questions in order. If you fail a gate, fix that problem before paying anyone. The gates cover what a customer is worth, whether marketing is the real bottleneck, whether the maths holds in a weak month, and whether you can give access and time.

Each gate is a filter, not a score. A consultant with a strong pitch cannot compensate for a failed gate, because the problem sits in your business rather than in their skill. Work through the gates on paper before the first call.

## Do You Know What a New Customer Is Worth?

You cannot judge a consultant without two numbers: the profit from a typical new customer, and the share of enquiries that become customers. If you do not know them, the first job is tracking, not marketing.

A roofing example in a [UK Facebook ads guide](https://bonsandfrazer.com/insights/facebook-ads-on-a-budget/) shows the arithmetic: one in four enquiries becomes a job, and the average job is worth £2,000. Those are that business's figures, not a benchmark for yours, but they show what the two numbers look like in practice.

Count calls as well as forms. According to Google Ads Help, as cited by [Biznex](https://biznex.app/blogs/blogs-performance-max-for-local-business/) (secondary), Google's channel performance report includes offline conversions such as local actions, store visits and store sales. A consultant who starts by setting that up is doing useful work.

## Is Marketing Really the Bottleneck?

A consultant can bring enquiries. They cannot answer your phone, add capacity or fix a weak offer. Look honestly at the last month:

- Do enquiries sit unanswered for hours? Fix response time first.
- Are you booked solid for weeks? Price or capacity may be the constraint, not demand.
- Do people enquire and then disappear? Check your quote, pricing page or first reply.

If demand is genuinely the problem, with few enquiries and a quiet calendar, move on to the next gate. If not, a consultant will mostly add cost.

## Does the Maths Survive a Bad Month?

Divide the fee by your profit per new customer. Assume a $600 monthly fee and $300 profit per new customer (both assumptions). The work then has to produce two extra customers every month just to break even. Those customers must be caused by the work, not by seasonal demand you would have had anyway.

If two extra customers sound unrealistic for your size, a one-off audit is a better first step than a retainer. [Affordable marketing help options](/blog/affordable-marketing-help-small-business-2026) are worth comparing before you commit to a monthly fee.

## Can You Give Access Without Losing Control?

Consultants need access to your Business Profile, ad account and analytics, along with quick answers about offers, photos and availability. Without that, the work drifts. Agree a weekly check-in as a minimum.

Adwave describes a single prepaid budget that covers audience, creative, budget and reporting. Use that as a checklist: ask any provider which of those four they control and which you keep. You should be able to log into every account yourself. For a fuller hiring checklist, see [how to hire a digital marketing consultant](/blog/how-to-hire-digital-marketing-consultant-local-business).

## What Can a Consultant Actually Change?

Treat any guaranteed local ranking as a red flag. A consultant can fix categories, services, pages and reviews, which improve how clearly your business matches a search. They cannot promise a position on a results page.

Profile changes need time to read. Adwave recommends running a Google Business Profile A/B test for at least two weeks, and preferably about four, before comparing call volume. In paid search, a consultant can fix structure, tracking and wasted clicks. They cannot change what a click costs in your city, and they cannot turn a weak offer into a strong one.

## How Much Is the Time Saving Really Worth?

This is the core calculation. Every input is an assumption, so replace them with your own numbers.

Assumptions: you spend 6 hours a week on marketing, value your time at $50 an hour, work 4.33 weeks a month, pay a flat $600 a month, and still spend 2 hours a week with the consultant.

| | DIY | Consultant |
|---|---|---|
| Your marketing hours per week (assumption) | 6 | 2 |
| Your time per month, at 4.33 weeks (assumption) | 26 h | 8.7 h |
| Value of your time at $50 an hour | $1,300 | $433 |
| Consultant fee (assumption) | $0 | $600 |
| Total monthly cost | $1,300 | $1,033 |

On these numbers the consultant is about $267 a month cheaper, but only if the freed hours earn money. If they would go unused, the saving is not real.

Now test the sensitivity. Break-even is $600 divided by ($50 x 4.33), about 2.8 hours a week.

| Hours saved per week (assumption) | 2 | 4 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Value per month at $50 an hour | $433 | $866 | $1,299 |
| Minus the $600 fee | -$167 | +$266 | +$699 |

Below about 2.8 hours saved, you are paying for results rather than time, so the customer-based maths from Gate 3 applies.

## What Should a 90-Day Test Look Like?

A 90-day window (assumption) is long enough to see whether a change works, but only if the test starts with a baseline. Adwave's small business TV guide sets out a four-week plan: define the KPI, offer, geography and budget in week one; produce creative and install tracking in week two; then launch a controlled test in week three.

Measurement needs time. For a CTV test, Adwave says four weeks of pre-launch data is the minimum, with six weeks being better. For brand effects from TV, it says two to four weeks of consistent exposure is needed before measuring. For a web form test, it suggests reserving two to four weeks, with the sample size and your business cycle deciding whether that is enough.

For a worked example of paid search in another market, see the [Germany Google Ads guide](/blog/local-marketing-germany-small-business-google-ads).

## Which Small Test Budgets Make Sense First?

Start small before scaling. Adwave advises a test budget of $50 to $100 before scaling a TV approach, and for an initial CTV test it suggests $200 to $500 over two to four weeks. Its TikTok guide recommends splitting a $500 budget into phases rather than spending it all on one campaign.

For lead follow-up and simple automations, Make.com has a free-forever tier plus four paid tiers, priced by monthly operations, according to [InclusiveAIHub](https://inclusiveaihub.org/2026/10/01/make-com-for-small-business-is-it-worth-using-in-2026/) (secondary, as of 2026). Check current pricing on the vendor's site before relying on it. ## How Do You Know the Consultant Is Working?

Compare results with your baseline, not with a report full of impressions. Enquiries, bookings and cost per customer are the numbers that answer the question. For content work, Adwave suggests tracking blogging spend against four factors: production cost, distribution value, assisted conversion impact and refresh efficiency.

Ask for a written scorecard before any retainer starts. A simple version: month one is an audit with specific findings and tracking that counts calls and forms; month two is the first changes live with a baseline cost per enquiry; month three is a comparison against that baseline, shown in your own accounts, including what did not work.

## What Should You Do This Week?

1. Write down your profit per new customer and the share of enquiries that become customers. If you do not know them, set up call and form tracking first.
2. Log your marketing hours for one week and multiply by your honest hourly value. Compare that with the break-even of about 2.8 hours a week for a $600 fee.
3. Ask a consultant for a one-off audit as the first step, not a retainer.
4. Agree three success measures in writing and confirm that you own the accounts.
5. Start any paid test small, with the budget sizes above, and set the measurement window before launch.

## FAQ

### Is a marketing consultant worth it for a small local business?

It can be, if the time they save or the profit they add is greater than the fee. Run the four gates and the break-even calculation with your own numbers before deciding.

### How do I know if a consultant is working?

Compare enquiries, bookings and cost per customer against a baseline you recorded before the work started. Impressions and follower counts do not answer whether the business is growing.

### Should I hire a consultant or do it myself?

Start with defined jobs where expertise matters most, such as tracking, ad setup and profile clean-up, and keep the work that needs your own voice. Our [hiring checklist](/blog/how-to-hire-digital-marketing-consultant-local-business) covers what to ask.

### Does my consultant need to be local?

Not for most digital work. Experience in your type of business, access to your accounts and clear reporting matter more than location.
