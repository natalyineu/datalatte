A/B testing shows two versions of one page or ad to similar visitors and compares the results. For small business websites, AdWave gives 2% to 5% as a common conversion range, though it varies by industry and channel ([AdWave](https://adwave.com/resources/sales-funnel-small-business-guide), secondary). At that rate, a local site needs a lot of visitors before small edits show a clear result. Test big changes on your website, or test ads, where you control spend and impressions. A low-traffic framework from Conversion Works suggests aiming for effects of roughly 30% or more when you only have 100 to 500 conversions ([AdWave](https://adwave.com/resources/a-b-testing-low-traffic), secondary).

This guide covers how much traffic a test needs, what a local business should test first, how to measure results, and how to test ads on Meta.

## How Much Traffic Does an A/B Test Need?

Usually more than a small local site gets. The number of visitors you need depends on your current conversion rate and the size of the change you want to detect. Small lifts need large samples, and large lifts need smaller ones.

The low-traffic framework from Conversion Works recommends aiming for effects of about 30% or greater when only 100 to 500 conversions are available ([AdWave](https://adwave.com/resources/a-b-testing-low-traffic), secondary). That threshold matters more than any calculator result. If a test cannot produce 100 conversions in a reasonable time, the page is too quiet for a fair test of a minor edit.

For a page that gets a few hundred visits a month, the realistic options are a bigger change, a test on a page with more traffic, or a test on ads.

## What Should a Local Business Test First?

Test things that change the decision a visitor makes, not decoration. Good candidates include:

- The offer, such as a free estimate versus a fixed price, or a trial class versus an intro pass
- The main action, such as a phone call, an online booking or a contact form
- The headline, such as service plus area versus a slogan
- Proof near the button, such as review count or years in business, only if the claim is true

Testing value propositions or conversion factors is a better use of a small sample than testing colours or images. StatRow-style ideas aside, StackAdapt recommends testing value propositions or conversion factors rather than elements such as CTA colour or images when gathering insight from creatives ([StackAdapt](https://www.stackadapt.com/resources/blog/audience-insights-with-creatives), secondary, as of 2020).

Change one main thing at a time. If the offer and the headline both change, you cannot tell which one moved the result. For a fuller list of page elements to review first, see the [website conversion rate optimization guide](/blog/website-conversion-rate-optimization).

## Which Metrics Should You Compare?

Pick one primary metric before the test starts. For a contact or booking page, AdWave recommends starting with one measurement: completed form submissions, tracked as a conversion event in your analytics ([AdWave](https://adwave.com/resources/contact-page-optimization-getting-more-leads-from-a-single-page), secondary). Phone calls and bookings can be added later, but a second metric makes it harder to call a winner.

For ads that aim at response, AdWave suggests CTR and conversion rate as the main pair ([AdWave](https://adwave.com/resources/dynamic-creative-optimization), secondary). A higher click-through rate with no extra sign-ups means the ad is attracting clicks but not the right people, so the landing page or offer needs a look. For CTR benchmarks by channel, see [CTR and conversion rate benchmarks for local business](/blog/ctr-conversion-rate-benchmarks-local-business).

Mobile traffic also affects results. Google's mobile-site analysis, cited by AdWave, found conversion probability fell 95% as page elements rose from 400 to 6,000 ([AdWave](https://adwave.com/resources/form-optimization-conversions), secondary). If a test page loads slowly or has many elements, fix that before testing copy. See [mobile optimization for local business](/blog/mobile-optimization-local-business).

## How Do You Test Ads on Meta?

Meta's A/B testing feature measures each strategy's performance on a cost per result basis or a cost per conversion lift basis ([Meta Business Help Center](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/1738164643098669), primary). Choose the metric that matches your goal before you launch, and do not switch it halfway through.

Start with one variable, such as the image, the headline or the audience. Keep the budget the same for both versions so the comparison is fair. If you are testing how often people see an ad, AdWave suggests comparing engagement, recall and conversion metrics after two weeks when testing frequency caps of four versus eight on similar audience segments ([AdWave](https://adwave.com/resources/ad-fatigue-frequency-capping-political-campaigns), secondary).

For ads that cost more than the test is worth, stop and check the offer. A local service that wins clicks but loses sales may have a pricing or trust problem. The Google Local Services Ads reporting guide from one vendor says contractors with estimate-to-sale conversion rates below 20% typically have pricing or trust issues that LSA optimization alone cannot solve ([Get Ryze](https://www.get-ryze.ai/blog/google-local-services-ads-reporting-tool), secondary).

## Worked Example: A Plumber's Low-Traffic Test

All inputs below are assumptions for illustration, not data from a real business.

Assume a plumber's booking page gets 400 visits a month and converts 3% of them into booking requests. That is 12 conversions a month. Assume the plumber wants to test a headline that might lift conversions by 30%, which is the effect size the low-traffic framework aims for.

Calculation:

- Monthly conversions: 400 visits × 3% = 12
- Conversions needed for the low end of the framework: 100
- Months to reach 100 conversions with all traffic on one page: 100 ÷ 12 ≈ 8.3 months
- Target conversion rate if the headline works: 3% × 1.30 = 3.9%

Eight months is too slow for a headline test. The plumber has three practical choices: test a larger change such as a new offer, move the test to a page with more traffic, or run the headline as an ad test where impressions and clicks come faster. The calculation shows why small sites should check traffic before they choose what to test.

## How Do You Read the Result?

Wait until the test reaches its planned end date or sample size. Do not stop when one version leads early. A lead that appears on day three often disappears after the weekend traffic arrives.

Record the test: what changed, the dates, the primary metric, the numbers and the decision. This record stops you from running the same test again in a year.

If the difference is small and the sample is small, treat the result as a hint, not a rule. Run a second test with a bigger change. For tracking setup, see the [email testing guide](/blog/ab-testing-emails-small-business-guide) for the same logic applied to newsletters, and check your analytics configuration on each platform's live help page.

## FAQ

### How long should an A/B test run?

Run it until you reach the sample size you planned, not until one version pulls ahead. For Meta ads, check Meta's best-practices page for current guidance on test length, because the platform changes its recommendations.

### Can I A/B test a small local website?

Only for large changes. The low-traffic framework points to effects of about 30% or greater when conversions are limited to 100 to 500 ([AdWave](https://adwave.com/resources/a-b-testing-low-traffic), secondary). Small edits will rarely show a clear result on a few hundred visits a month.

### What is a normal conversion rate for a local business website?

AdWave gives 2% to 5% as a common range for small business websites, but notes it varies by industry and channel ([AdWave](https://adwave.com/resources/sales-funnel-small-business-guide), secondary). Use it as a starting point, then compare against your own earlier months.

### Should I change two things at once?

You can, but then you cannot tell which change caused the result. For a small site, one large change is easier to read than several small ones.
