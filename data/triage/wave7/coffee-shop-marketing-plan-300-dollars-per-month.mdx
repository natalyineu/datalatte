A $300 monthly budget can cover the basics for a coffee shop if the money goes to one paid channel, a free Google Business Profile, and a low-cost email or text tool. The profile costs nothing to create, so it should be set up first. The worked example below assumes about two-thirds of the budget goes to Google Ads, and every number is either sourced or labelled as an assumption.

## What should a $300 coffee shop plan fund first?

Madsun Media recommends getting four fundamentals right on a tight budget before adding more marketing: a Google Business Profile, reviews, a mobile-friendly website, and one paid channel ([Madsun Media](https://www.madsunmedia.com/blog/small-business-marketing-australia), secondary). Only the paid channel needs a clear line in the budget. The profile and reviews run without a media spend, which leaves most of the $300 for one channel that can be measured.

Start with the profile because it is the free piece and the one most shops set up late. The plan in this article assigns $0 to it. If the website is already mobile-friendly, the budget does not need to cover it. If it is not, fixing the site is a separate cost that this plan does not include.

For a wider view of automation options, see [marketing automation for local coffee shops](/blog/marketing-automation-for-local-coffee-shops). That guide covers the tools; this one covers how to fit them inside $300.

## How do you set up a free Google Business Profile?

Creating a listing is free, and it requires a Google account, which can also be registered for free ([BrewRound](https://brewround.com/blog/coffee-shop-marketing/google-my-business-for-coffee-shops/), secondary, 2020). Per Diem recommends choosing "Coffee shop" or a related term rather than a broad category such as "Restaurant" ([Per Diem](https://www.tryperdiem.com/blog/google-business-profile-coffee-shop-guide), secondary). BrewRound notes that only one category can be selected at setup, with more added later.

Verification is the step that slows people down. Google usually mails a postcard with a code to the business address, and according to BrewRound, the profile does not appear in results until it is verified. Expect the postcard to take time, and start the setup before the budget starts running.

Once the listing is live, Per Diem suggests answering common questions proactively in the Q&A section, such as whether the shop offers free Wi-Fi, dairy-free options, or dogs on the patio. Google also lets people message a business directly through the listing. Per Diem reports that Think Coffee placed its app download link in its Google profile and now sees thousands of downloads. That is one brand's reported result, not a benchmark for your shop.

## How should a $300 budget be split for Google Ads?

Savoir Management recommends a targeting radius of 5 to 15 miles for shops and clinics, with a wider radius for mobile services ([Savoir Management](https://www.savoirmanagement.com/google-ads-for-local-businesses), secondary). A tighter radius is a choice to test against your own results, not a fixed rule. Square's citation of IBISWorld puts Canada at over 8,000 coffee shops as of 2025, so a broad radius in a dense city means competing against many nearby shops.

Localized phrases are a useful starting point. The vendor seoplus notes that a phrase such as "coffee shop downtown Phoenix" is likely to have less competition than a generic term like "coffee shop," which can lead to a lower cost per click ([seoplus+](https://seoplus.com/paid-ads/the-ultimate-guide-to-multi-location-ppc-advertising-on-google-ads/), secondary). The [keyword list for coffee shops](/blog/best-google-ad-keywords-for-coffee-shops-in-2026) is a practical next step once the radius is set.

The starting budget matters. One guide states that $500 to $1,500 per month gives enough data to evaluate cost per lead and foot traffic impact ([Wizerunek](https://wizerunekwsieci.com/local-advertising-on-google-for-small-businesses-ready-to-grow/), secondary). At $200 of search spend, a shop gets fewer clicks to learn from, so the review window should be longer, and decisions should wait for more data.

## What does a worked $300 budget look like?

The table below is a worked example. Inputs marked as assumptions are planning choices, not sourced prices. The only sourced figures are the free listing and the five minutes a day.

| Line item | Monthly amount | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Google Ads, local search | $200 | Assumption |
| Email or text tool | $50 | Assumption; loyalty app plans are listed higher, see the automation section |
| Reserve for one social boost | $50 | Assumption; accumulates to $200 over four months for one campaign |
| Google Business Profile | $0 | Listing is free per BrewRound |
| Staff time on the profile | About 2.5 hours | 5 minutes a day across 30 days, per Bellwether Coffee |

Assume 25 new customers a month come from the ads, and each visit averages $6 in sales. Cost per new customer is $200 divided by 25, which is $8. At $6 per visit, a new customer needs two visits to cover that $8 before cost of goods. Repeat visits, not the first sale, decide whether the ad spend pays back, so the monthly review should track return visits as well as new ones.

Social posting is the other place time goes. The Restaurant HQ recommends posting on social media at least once per week, and ideally one to two times per day ([The Restaurant HQ](https://www.therestauranthq.com/operations/coffee-shop-marketing/), secondary). Posting in a fixed weekly slot takes less time than daily posting, and that is the more realistic cadence at this budget.

## Where do email, text and social fit?

Square's Future of Commerce 2025 report, as cited on Square's page, found that 65% of Canadians like businesses to communicate with them by email, and 39% appreciate text message updates ([Square](https://squareup.com/ca/en/the-bottom-line/reaching-customers/marketing-plan-for-a-coffee-shop)). These are Canadian figures. For shops in the US, UK, or Australia, test the mix with your own customers before committing a budget. Email first and text second is a reasonable default for a $50 line.

Loyalty and messaging tools vary in price. Stamp Me lists its "The Full Works" plan at £49 per month in the UK, €49 per month, $79 AUD per month excluding GST, and $79 CAD per month ([Stamp Me](https://www.stampme.com/pricing)). Plans like these can take most of a $50 line, so check the currency and tax before choosing one. The [comparison of automation tools for small business](/blog/best-marketing-automation-tools-small-business-compared) lists more options.

Meta owns both Instagram and Facebook, which lets advertisers identify detailed customer profiles and set a budget for each ad ([The Restaurant HQ](https://www.therestauranthq.com/operations/coffee-shop-marketing/), secondary). A location-based social campaign can cost a couple of hundred dollars, so the reserve line only buys one campaign every few months.

## How do you review the plan each month?

A monthly review keeps the $300 from drifting. Use the steps below in order, and change one thing at a time so the results are readable.

1. Compare actual ad spend with the budget line and note any overspend before adjusting anything.
2. Count new customers who mention the ad, ask for the offer, or say they found the shop online.
3. Divide ad spend by new customers and compare the result with the $8 assumption in the worked example.
4. Check the Google Business Profile for unanswered reviews, empty Q&A entries, and missed weekly posts.
5. Answer every profile message within a set window, and use [AI-driven customer service](/blog/ai-driven-customer-service-for-local-coffee-shops) only if the volume justifies it.
6. Write down one change for the next month, such as a new keyword group or a different offer.

Keep the review short. Thirty minutes a month is enough if the spend, counts, and profile checks are already recorded in one place.

## FAQ

### Is $300 a month enough for Google Ads?

It can run a test, but the sourced starting range is $500 to $1,500 per month for enough data to judge cost per lead and foot traffic ([Wizerunek](https://wizerunekwsieci.com/local-advertising-on-google-for-small-businesses-ready-to-grow/), secondary). At $300, plan for fewer clicks, a longer review window, and no major changes until the data supports them.

### Does a Google Business Profile cost anything?

Creating a listing is free, according to BrewRound, which notes that a Google account is needed and can be registered for free ([BrewRound](https://brewround.com/blog/coffee-shop-marketing/google-my-business-for-coffee-shops/), secondary, 2020). Verification by postcard takes time, so start early. Check Google's live Business Profile help page for current verification options.

### Which category should a coffee shop choose?

Per Diem recommends "Coffee shop" or a related term rather than a broad category such as "Restaurant" ([Per Diem](https://www.tryperdiem.com/blog/google-business-profile-coffee-shop-guide), secondary). Add more categories later if the setup allows it.

### Should the budget go to Facebook instead of Google?

The sources do not show which channel is cheaper for a given shop, so this plan keeps search as the main line. Local search reaches people already looking for coffee nearby. Social can run as a reserve line for events and offers, and the monthly review will show which channel brings in more customers for your shop.
