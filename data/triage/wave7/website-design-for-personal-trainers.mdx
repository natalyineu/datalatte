A personal trainer website needs five core pages: a home page with a clear offer, a services page with prices or a starting price, client proof, an about page with qualifications, and a contact or booking page that works on a phone. Use the same main button on every page, usually a free consultation. Speed matters as well. Adwave's booking page guide cites a SOASTA study in which pages loading one second faster achieved up to a 27% increase in conversion rates.

## Which Pages Does a Personal Trainer Website Need?

Start with a short list and build each page well. A trainer with one location can cover the essentials on five pages, and the table below sets out what each one must answer.

| Page | What it must answer | Main button |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Home | Who you train, where, and the result you aim for | Book a free consultation |
| Services | Formats, session length, price or starting price | Book or enquire |
| About | Qualifications and who you work best with | Book a consultation |
| Results | Client quotes, used with permission | Book a consultation |
| Contact | Location, hours, form or calendar | Submit or book |

Keep the menu short. Adwave's navigation guide flags a top menu with more than seven items as a sign that pages should be grouped under hub pages. Most trainer sites stay well under that limit, so put package types and session formats under Services rather than giving each one its own menu item.

## What Should the First Screen Say?

A visitor should understand the offer before scrolling. Name the client you work with, the result you help them reach, and where you train. [Above-the-fold design for local businesses](/blog/above-the-fold-design-local-business) covers how to lay out that first screen so the booking button stays visible on a phone.

A FLiiP sales script example shows how a membership can be described in one sentence. Its illustrated membership includes access to all classes, 24/7 gym use, and one personal training session each month. A solo trainer can apply the same idea: state what is included, how often, and what it costs, in plain words.

## How Do New Clients Find a Trainer Site?

Local search is the main route. The Association of Fitness Studios explains that the Google Map Pack lists relevant local businesses for queries such as "personal training studios near me." Your Business Profile and a site with clear location details give that listing something to link to.

Paid search can fill gaps. Adwave's dog trainer guide suggests a monthly Google Ads budget of $300 to $800, and says that range gives most local trainers enough data to see what is working. That is a dog-training benchmark, so test your own range. [Google Ads for personal trainers](/blog/google-ads-for-personal-trainers) and [the best keywords for personal trainers](/blog/best-google-ad-keywords-for-personal-trainers-in-2026) cover the setup.

## Do Reviews and Testimonials Change Bookings?

Adwave's testimonial guide cites Caseleap's review that 88% of consumers trust online reviews as much as personal recommendations. For a trainer, that means a visitor who reads three real client quotes is closer to a booking than one who sees only your own description.

Use quotes only with the client's permission, and keep them specific. "Lost 8 kg in 12 weeks" is a personal outcome claim, so check it is accurate and that you can back it up. Adwave's microcopy guide recommends a human reviewer for personal or outcome claims in regulated fields such as healthcare, and the same caution suits fitness results.

## How Fast Should the Site Load on a Phone?

Most trainers view their own site on a laptop, but many clients will open it on a phone from a gym or a bus stop. Adwave's booking page guide cites the SOASTA study, referenced in Google's mobile site speed playbook, which found up to a 27% conversion increase from pages loading one second faster.

Resize photos before uploading them, remove unused plugins, and test the booking path on a real phone. Google's current Core Web Vitals targets are published on web.dev, so check the live page before setting a performance goal.

## How Should You Collect Emails and Send Offers?

Never buy an email list. Adwave's local marketing guide advises collecting emails ethically at the point of sale, through a sign-up form on your website, or at local events. Tell people what they will receive and how often.

Direct mail still has a place. A 2023 McKinsey report, cited by Universal Mail Works, found that 70% of consumers find direct mail more personal than online interactions. Adwave recommends two to four mailings per year tied to seasonal peaks. For a trainer, that could mean January and spring mailings to past enquiries rather than a constant stream.

## What Credentials Belong on the About Page?

The Bureau of Labor Statistics notes that personal trainers usually must be certified before they begin working with clients or with members of a gym or health club. Some fitness instructor credentials require 200 to 500 hours of training, depending on the credential.

List the certifications you hold, the body that issued them, and the year you earned them. Visitors cannot judge your qualifications from a logo alone. [Marketing strategy for personal trainers](/blog/marketing-strategy-for-personal-trainers) explains how the About page fits into wider marketing.

## How Do You Know Which Page Brings Bookings?

Google Analytics assigns a sign-up to the last campaign that brought the user to the website, according to LivePlan's marketing ROI guide. If a client read your services page, then clicked an ad and booked, the ad may get all the credit. Track your booking form separately and ask new clients how they found you.

Adwave's TV ad guide describes a unique promo code, such as "TV25," used to link visits and sign-ups to one ad. A trainer can use a simple variant, such as a booking form field asking "Where did you hear about us?" or a code printed on a flyer.

## Worked Example: What Does One Ad Client Earn?

Every input below is an **assumption** for illustration. Replace them with your own figures. The starting ad budget sits at the low end of the dog-trainer range cited above.

| Input or result | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Monthly ad spend (assumption) | $300 |
| New clients per month from ads (assumption) | 2 |
| Price per session (assumption) | $60 |
| Sessions per client per month, one a week (assumption) | 4 |
| Months a client stays (assumption) | 4 |
| Revenue per client ($60 × 4 × 4) | $960 |
| Ad cost per client ($300 ÷ 2) | $150 |
| Gross revenue per client ÷ ad cost per client | 6.4x |

This is gross revenue, not profit. It excludes your time, equipment, insurance and the costs of running sessions. The ratio shows why tracking matters: if fewer clients stay, or fewer clients come from ads, the result changes quickly.

## FAQ

### Do I need a website if I already get clients from Instagram?

A website gives you a page you control and a booking path that does not depend on a social feed's rules. Social profiles can still help. Use them to send people to a page that explains your offer and books the session.

### How many pages does a trainer website need?

Five covers most solo trainers: home, services, about, results and contact. Add pages only when a visitor keeps asking a question that no existing page answers.

### Should I list my prices?

Show at least a starting price. It filters out people who cannot afford you and saves time on intro calls. Test a published range for a month and compare the number of enquiries.

### How do I know if the website brings in clients?

Track booking form submissions and ask new clients how they found you. Analytics tools credit the last campaign a visitor used, so a simple question at booking gives a fuller picture.
