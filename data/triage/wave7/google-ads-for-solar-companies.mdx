## What do tax-credit rules change in solar ad copy?

A window-replacement vendor cites the IRS as saying that the Energy Efficient Home Improvement Credit under section 25C does not apply to property placed in service after December 31, 2025 ([Netembark, secondary](https://www.netembark.com/ppc/home-improvement/window-companies/)). That is a different section from the residential solar credit, so do not copy that date into solar copy. Check the IRS's current residential energy guidance directly before writing any credit claim.

Financing copy carries its own rules. The same vendor page says that under Regulation Z §1026.24, terms such as a payment amount, number of payments, repayment period or down payment trigger additional disclosures, including the APR. If a solar ad quotes a monthly payment or a deposit, check those disclosure obligations with a compliance adviser before launch.

The practical rule is simple. Remove any headline that promises a percentage back unless a current official source supports it and a tax adviser has checked it.

## Who should solar ads target?

Google's small business guide gives a home improvement example in which the target audience is local homeowners between 35 and 65 years old ([Google](https://business.google.com/us/resources/articles/small-business-marketing/)). That is an illustration of audience thinking, not a rule for solar.

A construction marketing vendor suggests age targeting of 30 to 55 for homeowners ([Ruban KT, secondary](https://www.rubankt.com/google-ads-for-construction-companies/)). Test both ranges in separate ad groups and compare cost per held appointment, not clicks, before committing to one. The same logic applies to [window companies](/blog/google-ads-for-window-companies), which also sell a large ticket to homeowners.

## How much solar demand is actually in search?

A window-replacement vendor states that Google captures about 8% of homeowners actively searching for windows, while about 92% need windows but have not started looking, and it suggests Facebook for that second group ([Home Service Direct, secondary](https://www.homeservicedirect.net/google-ads-ppc-guide-window-replacement/)). Treat that split as one vendor's view, not a measured market share.

Solar will likely differ, so measure it. Compare the leads that come from search with the leads your sales team hears about from other sources. Geo-fencing is one non-search option. One vendor says a geo-fenced user can receive targeted messages for 30 days after entering the area ([West Coast Media, secondary](https://www.westcoastmedia.us/how-effective-is-geo-fencing-for-local-advertising)). Check the platform's rules and local privacy law before using it.

Fence companies face a similar high-ticket homeowner funnel, so their [Google Ads approach](/blog/google-ads-for-fence-companies) can serve as a comparison point.

## What does cost per held appointment look like?

No solar-specific cost per click is quoted here. The table uses illustrative inputs so you can see how each filter compounds. Every figure in the input column is an assumption, not a benchmark. Replace each one with your own account data.

| Step | Input (assumption) | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly budget | $5,000 | $5,000 |
| Average CPC | $15 | 333 clicks |
| Click to lead rate | 6% | 20 leads |
| Cost per lead | | $250 |
| Leads that qualify (own the home, suitable roof) | 40% | 8 qualified |
| Qualified leads whose appointment is held | 75% | 6 held |
| Cost per held appointment | | about $833 |
| Held appointments that sign | 20% | about 1.2 sales |
| Cost per sale | | about $4,167 |

Qualification and show rate together multiply the lead cost by about 3.3, because 40% times 75% is 30%, and $250 divided by 0.3 is about $833. Two filters move the final number more than a cheaper click does.

Whether $4,167 per sale works depends on your gross profit per installed system, which only your own numbers can answer. A filter that removes leads who would never be held or sold can lower cost per sale even when it reduces lead count.

## What should you change this quarter?

1. Add homeowner status and a roof or monthly bill range to the first form fields. Measure how far the lead count falls and whether held appointments rise.
2. Remove or isolate tax-credit keywords and copy until an official source and a tax adviser confirm them.
3. Import offline conversions for held appointments and signed jobs, so bidding learns from outcomes rather than form fills. The [guide to AI-driven local SEO reports](/blog/ai-driven-local-seo-reports-with-google-data-studio) shows one way to build the reporting view.
4. If a Google sign-up offer applies, read its terms first. Google states that new advertisers receive the ad credit only after meeting the minimum spend for the offer they select ([Google Ads](https://business.google.com/en-all/google-ads/)), so budget that spend before counting the credit as free money.
5. Keep your local listing consistent with the ads. The [guide to AI for Google Business Profile optimization](/blog/ai-for-google-business-profile-optimization) covers the listing side.
6. Test audience changes against cost per acquisition. A marketing agency reported halving a client's cost per acquisition to $110 using lookalike audiences ([Brolik, secondary](https://brolik.com/blog/maximize-spend-facebook-lookalike-audiences)). That is one agency's result, not a solar benchmark, but it shows why audience tests are worth running.

This guide does not confirm current credit rules. Check the IRS's residential energy guidance and a tax adviser before quoting any credit or percentage.

### What cost per lead should I expect for solar?
