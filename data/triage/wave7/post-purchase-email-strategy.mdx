A post-purchase email strategy for a local business can start with three automatic emails: a thank-you with aftercare, a feedback or review request a few days later, and a rebooking or reorder nudge. Sources differ on length. OptiMonk suggests 4 to 6 emails over 30 to 60 days, while AdWave, citing Klaviyo, puts the strongest sequences at 3 to 5 emails over 30 to 90 days. Three emails sit inside both ranges, which makes it a sensible place to begin and test.

## What is a post-purchase email sequence?

A post-purchase sequence is a short run of automatic emails triggered by a customer action, such as a completed purchase or a service sign-up, rather than by a calendar date. One onboarding campaign in AdWave's guide is triggered when a customer completes their first purchase or service sign-up, and it sends 4 to 6 emails over the first 30 days of the relationship ([AdWave, email automation for small business](https://adwave.com/resources/email-automation-for-small-business-the-campaigns-to-set-up-first), secondary).

The trigger matters because it puts each message close to the experience it refers to. A mypos guide defines the customer journey as the complete series of interactions a customer has with a brand, from initial awareness to post-purchase engagement, and splits it into five stages: awareness, consideration, purchase, retention and advocacy ([mypos customer journey guide](https://www.mypos.com/en-gb/cashregister/blog/business-guide/customer-journey-101-stages-mapping-and-management)). A post-purchase sequence mostly serves the retention and advocacy stages. If you want to map the rest of that path, the [customer journey mapping guide for small businesses](/blog/customer-journey-mapping-small-business-guide) walks through each stage.

## Which three emails should you send first?

Three emails cover the job without overloading a small list. The timings below combine sourced examples with one assumption, so check them against your own booking or repeat-purchase data before you lock them in.

| Email | Timing | Job |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Thank-you and aftercare | Right after the visit or purchase | Confirm the experience and give one useful tip |
| 2. Feedback or review request | Around day 3 to day 7 | Ask for honest feedback with one direct link |
| 3. Rebook or reorder | Day 30, or your normal repeat cycle (assumption) | Give one clear next step |

AdWave's onboarding example sends its first email immediately and its second on day 3 ([AdWave](https://adwave.com/resources/email-automation-for-small-business-the-campaigns-to-set-up-first), secondary). Another AdWave page says post-purchase flows start 2 to 3 days after delivery, which suits products more than visits ([AdWave, ecommerce advertising](https://adwave.com/resources/best-advertising-for-ecommerce-stores), secondary). A gym guide sends automated emails at day 7, day 30 and day 60 after sign-up, including a referral ask at day 60 ([Vertical Impression](https://www.verticalimpression.com/guides/how-to-market-health-wellness-business-2026)). For a salon, the repeat cycle might be six weeks; for a café with a loyalty offer, a week or two. Use your own data to set that delay.

## How long should the sequence run?

Length is the first thing sources disagree on. OptiMonk recommends a post-purchase flow of 4 to 6 emails over 30 to 60 days ([OptiMonk](https://www.optimonk.com/email-marketing-frequency)). AdWave says the strongest sequences usually run over 30 to 90 days with 3 to 5 emails, and adds that for many small businesses five emails is enough to start ([AdWave, loyalty email sequences](https://adwave.com/resources/loyalty-email-sequences-keeping-customers-engaged-post-purchase), secondary).

Three emails fit within both ranges, so start there. If the first sequence gets clicks on email 2 but few rebookings from email 3, add a fourth email with a different angle rather than making email 3 longer. For inactive customers who stop buying, the win-back guidance of 3 to 5 emails, sent every 3 to 5 days, is described as an industry average rather than a rule ([Propello](https://propellocloud.com/blog/win-back-campaigns/)). Treat it as a starting line, not a schedule to copy.

For more email ideas beyond the sequence, see [20 email campaigns that drive revenue](/blog/email-marketing-ideas-for-small-businesses-20-campaigns-that-drive-revenue-2026).

## How often should you email customers?

Frequency is a separate question from length. Most businesses send between one and three marketing emails a month, according to a MailerLite study cited by Selzy ([Selzy](https://selzy.com/en/blog/email-cadence/), secondary). Litmus research cited by the same article found that 29% of small companies most commonly send marketing emails weekly ([Selzy](https://selzy.com/en/blog/email-cadence/), secondary). Omnisend's client data, cited by Snov.io, shows 54% of clients send 2 to 4 emails a month ([Snov.io](https://snov.io/blog/email-frequency-best-practices/), secondary).

The post-purchase sequence is separate from your newsletter. Once it finishes, OptiMonk suggests pulling lapsing subscribers back to one email a week when they show no engagement for 30 to 90 days ([OptiMonk](https://www.optimonk.com/email-marketing-frequency)). AdWave adds that customers who unsubscribe from a loyalty sequence should be removed from promotional emails and kept only on operational messages they still need ([AdWave](https://adwave.com/resources/loyalty-email-sequences-keeping-customers-engaged-post-purchase), secondary).

## What deliverability rules should you check first?

Two things can push a sequence into spam before anyone reads it. The first is complaints. OptiMonk describes a spam complaint rate above 0.1% as a hard ceiling that can lead Gmail to filter email into the promotions tab ([OptiMonk](https://www.optimonk.com/email-marketing-frequency)). Watch that number in your email tool every time you send.

The second is subject lines. AdWave lists subject lines that start with "Re:" or "Fwd:" to fake a personal connection as a deceptive tactic that can send an email to the spam folder ([AdWave, why your emails go to spam](https://adwave.com/resources/why-your-emails-go-to-spam-and-how-to-fix-deliverability), secondary). Write a plain subject line that says what the email is.

Authentication also matters. AdWave describes the SPF record as a public list of the servers and tools authorized to send email from your domain, and an email from an unlisted server fails the check ([AdWave](https://adwave.com/resources/why-your-emails-go-to-spam-and-how-to-fix-deliverability), secondary). Your email provider will usually give you the record to add; ask whoever manages your domain to confirm it is in place.

## Which numbers should you track?

Open rates are a weak guide on their own. Klaviyo recommends tracking business-impact metrics such as repeat purchase rate, customer lifetime value increase and email engagement rather than relying only on opens ([AdWave](https://adwave.com/resources/loyalty-email-sequences-keeping-customers-engaged-post-purchase), secondary). For context, AdWave's citation of MailChimp research puts the average open rate at around 20%, and the same page calls a 3% click-through rate among openers a good result ([CMG Local Solutions](https://www.cmglocalsolutions.com/blog/marketing-email-frequency-how-often-should-you-send-out-marketing-emails), secondary). Treat both as rough reference points, not targets.

For a loyalty or repeat-business program, AdWave recommends tracking four numbers weekly: enrollment rate, reward redemption rate, repeat purchase rate, and average spend of members versus non-members ([AdWave](https://adwave.com/resources/customer-retention-strategies-that-cost-less-than-new-acquisition), secondary). A simple weekly sheet covers those. The [marketing dashboard guide](/blog/how-to-create-marketing-dashboard-small-business) shows how to set one up.

## What does a worked budget look like?

This is a model, not a result. Every input below is an assumption, and you should replace them with your own figures.

A pet groomer has 120 clients a month, and the inputs are these:

- Clients with a consented email address: 80 (assumption)
- Share who rebook because of email 3: 10%, which is 8 rebookings a month (assumption)
- Gross profit per visit: $60 (assumption)
- Email tool cost: $20 a month (assumption, within the $0 to $20 send-cost range AdWave gives for sends through an existing platform, secondary)
- Setup time: 3 hours (assumption)

The calculation is 8 rebookings x $60 = $480 gross profit a month, minus $20 tool cost, for $460 a month. If the rebook rate is only half that, at 4 rebookings, the figure is 4 x $60 = $240, minus $20, for $220 a month. If the rate is near zero, the sequence is not paying, and the subject lines and send times are the first things to test.

Whether any of this pays off depends on your margins and your real rebook rate. The [email marketing ROI examples](/blog/is-email-marketing-profitable-for-small-businesses-roi-data-real-examples) show how other small businesses have measured it.

## How do you set it up without a large budget?

Any email tool that can trigger a message from a purchase or booking will work. Compare options by trigger support and cost before you pick one, using the [marketing automation tools comparison](/blog/best-marketing-automation-tools-small-business-compared). If your point of sale is Square, Square Loyalty integrates directly with Square Marketing, so you can send email and text campaigns to loyalty customers from the same system ([Square Loyalty](https://squareup.com/us/en/software/loyalty), primary).

Checklist:

1. Collect an email address at checkout or booking, and record that the customer agreed to receive email.
2. Write the three emails in plain language, signed by a named person in your business.
3. Set the triggers and delays from the table above, then adjust them after your first 30 days of data.
4. Review repeat purchase rate and unsubscribes after 60 days, and change one email at a time so you know what caused a change.

Reactivation also has a retention test. A Cleverly guide calls the 90-day retention rate after reactivation the most important metric, because a customer who returns and churns within two months has not really been won back ([Cleverly](https://www.cleverly.co/blog/customer-reactivation-campaign-strategies), secondary). Keep that window in mind when you judge the sequence.

## FAQ

### Is a post-purchase sequence worth it with a small list?

The sources do not give a minimum list size. The worked budget above is one way to test it: enter your own consented subscriber count, rebook rate and profit per visit, and see whether the monthly figure covers the tool cost. Start with three emails and measure before adding more.

### How many emails is too many?

There is no universal number. OptiMonk recommends 4 to 6 over 30 to 60 days, AdWave says 3 to 5 over 30 to 90 days, and it notes five is enough for many small businesses. Watch complaints, because a rate above 0.1% is a hard ceiling for deliverability.

### Do I need a dedicated email platform?

Not necessarily. You need a tool that can trigger messages from a purchase or booking. If your point of sale already offers loyalty and marketing, such as Square's integration, check whether that covers the sequence before adding another system.

### What should I do when a customer unsubscribes?

Remove them from promotional emails and keep only the operational messages they still need, such as booking confirmations or safety notices. This follows AdWave's guidance on loyalty sequences ([AdWave](https://adwave.com/resources/loyalty-email-sequences-keeping-customers-engaged-post-purchase), secondary).
