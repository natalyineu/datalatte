A dental website that books patients does four jobs: it states what you do and where, offers one clear way to book or call, shows proof that the practice is trustworthy, and loads well on a phone. A vendor page from Adwave reports that 84% of patients read online reviews before choosing a provider and 73% visit the practice website before booking. Adwave also reports that more than 60% of website visitors are on phones. Design around those moments.

## What Do Patients Check Before Booking?

Reviews come first. [Adwave's patient research page](https://adwave.com/resources/how-to-get-more-patients) reports that 84% of patients read reviews before choosing a provider, and that 73% visit the practice website before booking. Those are vendor-reported figures, so treat them as directional, but they point the same way: the website is checked after the reviews and map listing have done some of the selling.

The site still matters because it is where the decision becomes a booking. A vendor estimate in a [list of businesses without websites](https://b2bleadfinder.io/blog/list-of-businesses-without-websites) puts 10–20% of dentists and clinics without any website. That is a low bar, but a site you can book from in under a minute is a real edge.

## What Should the Top of the Homepage Show?

Visitors usually arrive with one of a few goals: find an emergency dentist, check payment options, learn about one treatment or compare practices. On a phone, the first screen should answer five questions in a fixed order.

1. What you do and where, such as "Family and cosmetic dentistry in your town."
2. How to act, with a "Book online" button and a tap-to-call number.
3. Whether you are taking new patients and when you are open.
4. Who the patient will meet, using a real photo of the team or reception.
5. One trust cue you can prove, such as your Google rating and review count.

Keep the copy plain. Our [guide to writing website copy for local businesses](/blog/how-to-write-website-copy-local-business-examples) has examples you can adapt. Avoid carousels that push booking below the fold.

## Which Pages Does a Dental Website Need?

Most practices need a small set of pages, each with one job. A single page that lists every treatment forces the patient to hunt, and a page per treatment gives search engines and patients a clear match.

| Page | What to include |
| --- | --- |
| Home | Booking button, location, hours, new-patient note, rating |
| One page per treatment | Check-ups, fillings, crowns, implants, whitening, emergency, children |
| New patients | What to expect at the first visit, forms, payment options, accessibility |
| Meet the team | Photos, qualifications, a short personal line for each clinician |
| Contact and location | Map, parking, transport, hours, short form, phone |
| FAQ | Costs, anxiety, aftercare, emergencies, insurance questions |

Each treatment page should say what the treatment is, who it suits, what happens at each visit, how long it takes, and how to book. Write for an anxious adult, not a colleague. If you show prices, label routine work with "from" and describe complex cases as varying.

## How Should Online Booking Work?

Patients expect it. In a 2025 Eliciting Insights survey of 384 U.S. adults who had visited a healthcare provider in the past year, [Adwave reports](https://adwave.com/resources/appointment-booking-page-design) that 95% had booked a medical appointment online or said they would if the option were available. Among those who had already booked online, 92% said they planned to use online scheduling again.

Awareness is not the same as use. A [JMIR-cited UK dataset of 1,327,693 respondents](https://adwave.com/resources/online-appointment-booking-making-it-easy-for-customers-to-schedule), summarised by Adwave, found that 45.11% of patients who tried to book were aware their practice offered online booking, but only 15.61% used it. Put the booking option where patients can see it, and test the wording.

- Offer online booking through your practice software if it supports it, and keep the phone number beside it.
- Keep forms short: name, phone, email, reason for visit and preferred time.
- Say what happens next, and only promise a reply time you can keep.
- Put emergency instructions on their own page, including out-of-hours advice.

Once the flow works, [A/B testing for local businesses](/blog/a-b-testing-for-local-business) shows how to compare button wording or form length without guessing.

## How Do You Show Trust Within Advertising Rules?

Trust is the product. Use real photos of reception, treatment rooms and the team. Add bios with qualifications, review ratings that link to your Google listing, plain-language infection-control information, and notes on support for anxious patients.

Testimonials and before-and-after images need more care. Dental regulators in the US, UK, Canada and Australia each set advertising standards, and state, provincial or regional rules can differ. Check your regulator's current guidance before publishing patient images, superlatives such as "best dentist in town," or outcome promises. Get written consent for every patient image.

Embed your map and write directions in text as well. Our [Google Maps embed guide](/blog/google-maps-embed-local-business-website) covers the setup.

## Does Speed and Mobile Layout Affect Bookings?

Yes, because most visits start on a phone. Adwave states that most patients will find a dental practice on their phone, so the site must work on a small screen, and its vendor page reports that more than 60% of visitors are on phones. Test your homepage and booking page on a real phone over a mobile connection, not only at your desk.

Slow dental sites usually suffer from oversized photos, auto-playing video and heavy sliders. Compress the largest images, remove anything that moves without being asked, and check your pages in Search Console and Google's own speed tools. Our [local business website checklist](/blog/local-business-website-checklist) includes a short technical pass.

## How Should Tracking Be Handled?

Tracking is where dental sites take on risk, so decide the rules before you add a pixel. Keep ad and analytics tags off patient portals and off any form that asks for health details or the reason for a visit. Those pages are the most sensitive, and they are the ones most likely to create a privacy problem.

Visitors from the UK and EU also need a consent banner where cookies are set, and the rules on health data are strict. The [GDPR compliance guide for local business websites](/blog/local-business-website-gdpr-compliance) covers the basics. This is general information, not legal advice, so take advice from a privacy lawyer or your compliance provider before sending form data to ad platforms.

## Who Should Build the Site?

The right route depends on how much control you need and how much time you have to maintain it. Each option has a trade-off that matters more than the price tag.

| Option | Fits | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Website builder | A small practice that needs something live quickly | You write all the content, and design options are limited |
| Dental website specialist | A practice that wants booking and dental-specific features built in | Ongoing fees, template look, and who owns the site |
| Freelancer or custom build | A practice that wants a distinct brand | A clear brief and a maintenance plan |
| No-code build with AI help | A practice with a clear content plan and someone to own it | Testing, accessibility and privacy still need a human check; see [building with Cursor AI](/blog/cursor-ai-build-local-business-website-no-code) |

Whichever route you take, own the domain and hold admin access yourself. A site your agency controls is hard to leave.

## What Is a Better Booking Rate Worth?

No trustworthy public benchmark exists for dental website conversion, so the figures below are an illustration built on assumptions. Replace every assumption with your own analytics and appointment book before you act on the result.

| | Tough | Base | Strong |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Monthly website visitors (assumption) | 800 | 800 | 800 |
| Visitor to booking request or call rate (assumption) | 2% | 4% | 6% |
| Requests and calls | 16 | 32 | 48 |
| Request to attended new patient (assumption) | 60% | 60% | 60% |
| New patients per month | 9.6 | 19.2 | 28.8 |
| First-year value per new patient (assumption) | $900 | $900 | $900 |
| First-year value of the month's new patients | $8,640 | $17,280 | $25,920 |

Moving from Tough to Base adds 9.6 new patients a month in this example, worth about $8,640 in first-year value per month of traffic. The point is the mechanism, not the exact figure. A fix that doubles the booking rate keeps paying every month the site is live.

Count your own inputs: visitors from Search Console or analytics, calls and forms from tracking, the share who attend, and the real average value of a new patient. The biggest drop in that chain is the thing to fix first. Often it is unanswered phones rather than the design.

## What Should You Do This Week?

1. Open your homepage on a phone and time how long it takes to find a way to book or call. If it is more than a few seconds, move the button up.
2. Count last month's visitors, calls, form requests and attended new patients. Write down your visitor-to-booking rate and the attendance rate.
3. Remove ad and analytics tags from any page that collects health details or appointment reasons until you have taken privacy advice.
4. Check that your name, address and phone number match your Google profile exactly on every page.
5. Compress your largest photos and test the homepage and booking page on a real phone.

## FAQ

### What pages does a dental website need?

At minimum: a home page, one page per main treatment, new-patient information, a team page, contact and location details, and a booking option. Add reviews and an FAQ for common questions about cost, anxiety and emergencies.

### Should I list prices on my website?

It depends on your regulator and your market. Many practices explain what drives cost and show "from" prices for routine care, which can reduce unqualified calls. Describe complex cases as varying and check your regulator's current advertising guidance first.

### Can I use testimonials and before-and-after photos?

Sometimes, with specific written consent and within your dental regulator's advertising rules. Standards differ by country and by state or province, so check current guidance before publishing.

### How often should I update the site?

Review hours, team, payment options and emergency information at least every three months, and update whenever something changes. Outdated booking details cost more bookings than a dated design does.
