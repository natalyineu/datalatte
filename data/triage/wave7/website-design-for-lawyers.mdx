## Which Pages Does a Law Firm Website Need?

A small firm site needs a short set of core pages. Each practice area also needs its own page rather than one long list, so the page can answer the search that brings the visitor in. The table below sets out what each core page should contain before you launch.

| Page | What it should contain |
| --- | --- |
| Home | Practice areas, city, contact button, short proof of experience |
| Practice areas | One page per area: who it is for, the process, what to bring to a consultation |
| Attorneys | Photo, bar admission, education, areas of focus |
| Contact | Phone, form, address, hours, map, parking |
| Results or reviews | Only statements your bar allows (see below) |
| FAQ or resources | Plain answers to the questions clients ask first |

Location detail helps too. A case study that Semrush lists for Buckley Lawyers says location-specific pages helped the firm capture targeted leads from local communities (secondary). Each location page needs real local detail, not a city name swapped into a template.

## What Do Bar Rules Say About Advertising Claims?

Rules vary by state. The ABA Model Rules of Professional Conduct, Rules 7.1 to 7.3, give a baseline, but each state has its own advertising rules (Adwave, secondary). Adwave advises lawyers to review advertising rules with their specific state bar, because the rules can vary dramatically from state to state (secondary).

State bar rules typically require disclaimers that clarify key points. Examples include stating that past results do not predict future outcomes, or that the client may be responsible for costs (Adwave, secondary). A classic line for law firm ads is "past results do not guarantee future performance." Whether that wording satisfies your bar depends on your state, so confirm it before the page goes live.

Keep disclaimers next to the claim they qualify. A footer note that few visitors read will not cover a headline result.

## Can You Offer Reviews or Testimonials?

Offering incentives for online reviews violates Google's policies and most bar rules, according to Adwave's law firm advertising guide (secondary). Asking clients for a review is different from paying for one, so leave out any gift, discount or fee reduction tied to the review.

Testimonials and results on the site carry the same disclaimer duty as any other claim. If a testimonial suggests a result a future client could expect, your bar's disclaimer rules apply to that page as well. Check the wording with your state bar before you publish it.

## Are Fee Claims Risky on a Law Firm Site?

Advertising a service for less than it really costs is prohibited, and so are bait-and-switch tactics. An ABA summary cited on Adwave's advertising pages says FTC rules cover both (secondary). For a firm, that means a headline fee should match what a client actually pays, with any extra costs stated beside it.

Avoid vague wording such as "affordable" or "no fee" without the conditions attached. If a fee depends on a recovery, say so on the same screen as the number. A short note near the price is easier to defend than a long disclaimer at the bottom of the page.

## Is a Law Firm Website Worth the Money?

The worked example below uses assumptions, not benchmarks. Replace each assumption with your own figures.

| Input | Value | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monthly site visitors | 400 | Assumption |
| Visitors who call or send a form (2%) | 8 enquiries | Assumption |
| Enquiries that sign a matter (25%) | 2 matters | Assumption |
| Average fee per matter | $2,000 | Assumption |
| Monthly fee income | $4,000 | 2 matters × $2,000 |
| One-off branding, low end | $5,000 | Adwave range (secondary) |

At these inputs, $4,000 of monthly fee income recovers the $5,000 low-end branding cost in about 1.25 months. That figure is gross, before overheads, and it ignores ad spend and staff time. The enquiry rate is the input to test first, because a small change in it moves the result more than a change in fees.

## FAQ

### Do I need a disclaimer on my law firm website?

State bar rules typically require disclaimers that clarify key points, such as stating that past results do not predict future outcomes or that the client may be responsible for costs (Adwave, secondary). Confirm the exact wording with your state bar.

### Can I offer a gift for a client review?

No. Offering incentives for online reviews violates Google's policies and most bar rules, according to Adwave (secondary). Ask for reviews without attaching a reward.

### Do the ABA rules apply to my firm's website?

The ABA Model Rules of Professional Conduct, Rules 7.1 to 7.3, provide a baseline, but each state has its own advertising rules (Adwave, secondary). Your state's version is the one that governs your site.

### Are location pages worth building?

A Semrush-listed case study for Buckley Lawyers says location-specific pages helped capture targeted leads from local communities (secondary). Each page needs genuine local detail to be useful.

### How much does branding for a law firm cost?
