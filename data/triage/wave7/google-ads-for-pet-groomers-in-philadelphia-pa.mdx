## What does Google Ads cost for a pet groomer?

One vendor benchmark puts dog groomer clicks at about $2–$8 each and warns that a few wasted clicks add up quickly ([AdTurbo](https://adturbo.ai/google-ads-for-dog-groomers)). The same page says most dog groomer advertisers spend around $800–$5,000 per month. That spread is wide, and it reflects business size as much as strategy. A mobile groomer working one van has different capacity from a salon with several stylists.

No Philadelphia-only cost-per-click figure is available for this niche, so check the Keyword Planner estimate for your own service area before committing a budget. Start at the lower end of the vendor range and raise spend only after bookings are tracked for at least a month.

If you are also weighing pay-per-lead options, compare them against standard search ads in [this breakdown of Google Local Services Ads for pet groomers](/blog/google-local-services-ads-for-pet-groomers).

## Which keywords should Philadelphia pet groomers bid on?

AdTurbo lists three keywords worth bidding on for dog groomers: "dog groomer near me," "dog grooming near me," and "puppy grooming near me" ([AdTurbo](https://adturbo.ai/google-ads-for-dog-groomers)). These phrases signal that someone wants a service now, which is why they suit a local business with a fixed service area.

Philadelphia-specific terms are a reasonable test, not a sourced recommendation. Add a city-level phrase such as "pet grooming Philadelphia" as a separate ad group and compare its cost per booking with the "near me" group. Keep the ad group that books cheaper, and pause the one that does not.

Avoid broad, generic terms such as "dog" or "pet groomer" early on. They pull in searches from people who will never book a visit.

## How wide should the targeting radius be?

Radius is the setting most likely to waste budget for a local groomer. Barklead, a groomer marketing blog, suggests targeting users within a 10–15 mile radius of the business, but that advice is written for Facebook ads, not Google Ads ([Barklead](https://barklead.com/post/using-facebook-ads-for-groomers)). Treat 10–15 miles as an upper test boundary and not a default.

Set the radius around your address, then run one campaign at a tighter radius and one at a wider radius for the same period. Compare cost per booking, not cost per click. Customers who will drive across a city for a groomer tend to be rare, so a wider radius that adds clicks but no bookings is a clear signal to shrink it.

## Which conversions should you track?

AdTurbo names booking a grooming appointment as the primary conversion to track for dog groomer campaigns ([AdTurbo](https://adturbo.ai/google-ads-for-dog-groomers)). Everything else is a supporting signal. Clicks and calls show interest, but only a booked appointment shows revenue.

A practical setup:

1. Mark a completed online booking as the primary conversion.
2. Add phone calls from ads as a secondary conversion, using a tracking number.
3. Count direction requests from your Google Business Profile as a secondary signal.
4. Each week, reconcile the conversion count against your booking system so the numbers match.

## Does Google Ads work for a mobile pet groomer?

One published case study suggests it can. Marketing 360 reports that a mobile pet groomer who advertised on Facebook and Google saw a 134% increase in impressions, a 696% increase in clicks, and a 682% increase in conversions, each compared with the previous six-month period ([Marketing 360](https://blog.marketing360.com/case-studies/pet-groomer-marketing-case-study-driving-success-with-digital-ads/)). The page was published on June 13, 2023.

Treat this as one case, not a forecast. The report does not establish the starting baseline or spend level in a way that transfers to a Philadelphia business, and the percentage gains are large partly because the earlier period was small. Use it to justify a test budget, not to set expectations.

## How do seasonal peaks change the budget?

Adwave cites Groomer to Groomer, which notes that November and December are traditionally a very busy time for groomers, sometimes too busy ([Adwave](https://adwave.com/resources/pet-grooming-advertising)). A full diary means extra ad clicks may not convert into extra revenue, because the appointment slots run out.

Plan around that. Check your booking calendar a few weeks before the peak, and if slots are scarce, lower the budget or narrow the campaign to your highest-converting keywords. Raise spend again in quieter months, when each new booking adds more capacity to use.

## Which negative keywords cut wasted spend?

GroomerGenie states that its own proprietary negative keyword list contains more than 500 groomer negatives ([GroomerGenie](https://www.groomergenie.com/services/google-ads)). That is a vendor claim about its list, so verify any list against your own search terms report before you rely on it.

Build your own list from real searches. Job and pay terms are common waste. A pet-shop marketing guide cites an average annual dog groomer salary of $40,000, which shows how much public interest exists in groomer pay ([PS Print](https://www.psprint.com/resources/definitive-pet-shop-marketing-guide/), secondary). Add "salary," "jobs," and "hiring" as negatives. Also exclude "how to," "course," and "training" for DIY searchers, plus any city names outside your service area.

Test a tighter radius against a wider one and compare cost per booking. Barklead's 10–15 mile suggestion was written for Facebook ads, so use it only as an upper boundary to test ([Barklead](https://barklead.com/post/using-facebook-ads-for-groomers)).

Yes, at least a starter list. Review your search terms report regularly and add terms that bring job seekers, DIY searchers, or out-of-area customers. A vendor's list can help as a starting point, but your own search data is the reliable reference.
