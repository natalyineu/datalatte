A remote marketing consultant can run your local campaigns without ever holding your passwords or account ownership. Grant them a limited role inside your accounts, keep ownership and billing yourself, and require a monthly report with cost per lead. Remote work suits most local marketing because the tools are cloud-based. The risks come from access settings, unclear ownership and reports that show clicks without outcomes. This guide covers each of those points for coffee shops, salons, groomers and studios in the US, UK, Australia and Canada.

## Does distance matter for a remote marketing consultant?

The tools behind local marketing are cloud accounts: Google Ads, Google Business Profile, Google Analytics and Meta's ad tools. Auditing a campaign, rebuilding a keyword list or fixing a profile category happens in a browser. Your consultant does not need to be in your postcode.

What does need to be local is knowledge: your market's competitors, your customers' search words, your opening hours and your busy seasons. You supply that context, and they supply method. A good remote consultant asks for it before touching anything.

Nobody publishes reliable data showing that remote consultants outperform local agencies, or the reverse. The better test is whether the person can show you, in your own account data, what changed and why. For a wider comparison of human and automated help, see [AI vs human marketing for local business](/blog/ai-vs-human-marketing-local-business-honest-comparison).

## Which access should you grant, and which should you keep?

Never share a password. Every major platform lets you invite someone with a limited role and remove that access later. Access level names and permissions change, so check the current list in each platform's help centre before you assign anything.

In Google Ads, give the consultant the level that lets them edit campaigns. Keep the highest level, the one that manages users and access, in your own name. Keep billing in your name too, so you control the card.

Google Ads lets you hold several campaigns in one account. Google's account prompt states that "You can create multiple campaigns in the same account without creating a new account." Ask the consultant to build inside your existing account rather than open a new one for you. That keeps your conversion history in one place.

For Google Business Profile, add the consultant as a manager and keep ownership yourself. For Google Analytics 4, an editor role is usually enough for campaign work, and a viewer role is enough if they only report. For Meta, give access to specific assets rather than the whole business, and confirm that your business owns the Page and the ad account.

The rule across all platforms is simple: you should own the accounts and see the billing. Ask in writing on the first call who owns each one. For vetting steps, read [how to hire a digital marketing consultant for a local business](/blog/how-to-hire-digital-marketing-consultant-local-business).

## What should the first month look like?

No official standard exists for this, so treat the following as a sensible pattern rather than a rule.

1. Week 1: access and audit. You grant the access above. The consultant reviews your Business Profile, website, ad accounts and tracking, then sends a written audit with the top priorities.
2. Week 2: tracking before spend. They confirm that calls, forms and bookings are counted. If tracking is broken, fix it before changing any bids.
3. Week 3: first changes. Profile corrections, negative keywords, ad copy tests or a landing-page fix. Each change is logged with a date.
4. Week 4: first report and call. A 30 to 60 minute call covers what changed, what moved and what comes next.

Campaign planning matters too. One seasonal planning guide describes a four-stage run of teaser, early access, main push and last-chance urgency (secondary, [AdWave](https://adwave.com/resources/seasonal-sales-strategies-promotional-planning-that-works)). Ask whether the first month includes a calendar like that for your next promotion.

## What do you need to provide?

Your consultant can see clicks and form fills. Only you know which enquiries became paying customers, so outcome data is your main contribution.

Provide context: your busiest days, average spend per customer, services you want more of and upcoming promotions. Agree a turnaround for approvals, such as one working day, because slow sign-off is a common reason remote projects stall.

Booking data matters for service businesses. A grooming marketing article cites Calendesk reporting that 90% of customers prefer booking online (secondary, [hibu](https://hibu.com/blog/industries/8-essential-dog-grooming-marketing-strategies)). If most of your work arrives as bookings, ask the consultant to confirm that online bookings are tracked as conversions.

For the brief itself, a short list of what you sell, who you want and what a good month looks like is enough. You can draft it with help from [ChatGPT prompts for local business marketing](/blog/chatgpt-prompts-for-local-business-marketing).

## What should a monthly report show?

A monthly report should be readable in five minutes and include:

- Spend, clicks, calls, form fills and bookings.
- Cost per lead, and cost per customer once you log outcomes.
- Changes made, with dates and reasons.
- Tests planned for next month.

Ask for channel-level detail too. Google announced that channel performance reporting "is now available in all Performance Max campaigns" (November 2025, [Google Ads announcement](https://business.google.com/us/accelerate/announcements/access-new-inventory-and-channel-performance-reporting-in-performance-max-campaigns/)). That lets you see where your ads ran, not just the total.

Attribution needs care. StackAdapt notes that "last-touch reporting can still help teams understand the final interaction before conversion" ([StackAdapt](https://www.stackadapt.com/resources/blog/last-touch-attribution)). It is one useful view, but it does not show the whole path a customer took.

Reporting tools have their own costs. One roundup lists a social scheduling tool's Advanced plan from $53 per month (secondary, [Sintra](https://sintra.ai/blog/ai-social-media/best-social-media-ai-agents)). Check whether any reporting tool fee is inside the consultant's fee or added on top.

A typical ad-reporting dashboard lists seven core metrics: impressions, clicks, click-through rate, average cost per click, average cost per thousand impressions, amount spent and conversions ([StackAdapt](https://www.stackadapt.com/resources/blog/how-to-advertise-on-chatgpt)). If a report shows only impressions and clicks, ask for the cost and conversion figures too.

## What are the red flags?

A report that is a screenshot of impressions and clicks with no cost per lead is a warning sign. So is a consultant who cannot say which account they work in, or who asks for your password instead of sending an invitation.

Ask how the consultant handles urgent issues such as a disapproved ad, an overspend or a billing error. Agree a response time in writing. Same business day is a reasonable request.

Give the engagement a review point. If you cannot see the work reflected in the numbers within three months, which is a rule of thumb rather than an industry standard, have a direct conversation or end the engagement.

## Does the fee pay for itself?

Use a simple break-even check. The inputs below are assumptions; replace them with your numbers.

| | No change | Cost per lead improves 20% | Cost per lead improves 35% |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Monthly ad budget (assumption) | $1,500 | $1,500 | $1,500 |
| Cost per lead (assumption) | $60 | $48 | $39 |
| Leads | 25 | 31.3 | 38.5 |
| Lead to customer rate (assumption) | 25% | 25% | 25% |
| New customers | 6.3 | 7.8 | 9.6 |
| Value per customer, first year (assumption) | $150 | $150 | $150 |
| Revenue from ads | $938 | $1,172 | $1,442 |
| Extra revenue vs no change | $0 | $234 | $504 |
| Consultant fee (assumption) | $500 | $500 | $500 |
| Extra revenue minus fee | -$500 | -$266 | +$4 |

The break-even rule is: minimum extra revenue must equal the monthly fee. At a $1,500 budget and a $500 fee, the consultant needs to cut cost per lead by roughly 35% to break even on these assumptions. The 20% and 35% improvements are illustrations, not benchmarks.

If your budget is small, a one-off audit and tracking fix often beats an ongoing retainer. The maths improves when the budget is larger, when a customer is worth well over $150, or when the consultant also fixes your Business Profile and landing page. ## What should you do today?

1. List every account your marketing touches: Google Ads, Business Profile, Analytics, Meta, website and domain. Write down who owns each one.
2. Check that you hold ownership or the highest access level on each. If a former agency or employee owns anything, request a transfer to you.
3. Work out your numbers: monthly ad budget, current cost per lead if you know it, value of a new customer and the most you would pay in fees.
4. Write a three-line brief covering what you sell, who you want and what a good month looks like in leads or bookings.
5. Ask five questions before agreeing to anything: who owns each account, what access level they need, what the monthly report contains, how fast they respond to urgent issues and what the notice period is.

## FAQ

### What access does a remote marketing consultant need?

For Google Ads, they usually need a level that lets them edit campaigns. Keep the level that manages users and access in your own name. For Business Profile, a manager role lets them edit without adding or removing users. Check each platform's current help page before assigning roles.

### Should I give a consultant my passwords?

No. Use each platform's invitation system so access is tied to the consultant's own login. You can remove that access at any time without changing your passwords.

### How long before I see results?

There is no official timeline. Tracking and profile fixes can show up within weeks. Ad optimisation needs enough clicks and conversions to read properly, so agree a review date, such as three months, in advance.

### Should I hire a remote consultant or a local agency?

Decide on evidence: relevant niche experience, clear monthly reporting, account ownership in your name and a fee that fits your budget. Location alone is not evidence of quality.

### What does a consultant cost?

Fees vary widely and there is no reliable published average. Use the break-even rule above: the extra revenue the work can realistically produce must exceed the fee.
