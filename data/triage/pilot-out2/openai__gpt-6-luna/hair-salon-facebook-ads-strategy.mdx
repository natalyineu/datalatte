Hair salon Facebook ads work best when the campaign has a specific service or offer, reaches people who can realistically visit, and tracks enquiries through to completed appointments. There is no salon-specific cost-per-lead figure in the available source, so a dependable budget starts with the salon’s own booking and profit data rather than an assumed industry benchmark.

Plan the campaign around four connected tasks: choose an offer, make the booking path easy, record which ads generate enquiries, and compare the cost of acquiring a client with that client’s gross profit. The worked example below uses clearly labelled assumptions, not a forecast or benchmark.

Before using any promotion, calculate its effect on service revenue and capacity. A discount that attracts attention but leaves too little margin may not be a suitable acquisition offer.

Start by deciding what the ad is meant to sell. A campaign promoting a specific service can be clearer than a general message about the salon. Match the image or video to the service, state what the customer receives, and link directly to the relevant booking information. Avoid presenting a promotional price unless it is accurate, available, and easy to understand.

## Which offer should a salon advertise?

Choose an offer that gives a potential client a clear reason to enquire while protecting the value of the service. A salon might promote a consultation, a defined service, or an add-on. These are options to test, not proven winners. The right choice depends on the salon’s margins, available appointments, staff skills, and the type of client it wants to attract.

An offer should answer the customer’s practical questions: what service is included, who provides it, how to book, and whether any terms apply. If the offer is limited to certain days or appointment types, say so in the ad and on the booking page. A mismatch between the ad and booking page can create avoidable confusion.

Use original salon imagery where possible, and make sure the salon has permission to use photographs of clients. Show the work related to the advertised service rather than relying on generic beauty imagery. Keep the main message focused, with one clear booking action.

A broad discount is not the only way to give an ad a specific hook. The salon can instead make the service, stylist, consultation, or appointment availability the focus. ## How should a salon set up its booking path?

Before paying to send people to a page, check that the page makes the next step obvious on a mobile device. Confirm that the advertised service can be found, the booking link works, and the contact information is correct. The ad, landing page, and booking system should describe the same service and offer.

If a customer has to search for the advertised service after clicking, the route from interest to booking is harder to evaluate. Use a direct booking link when that is practical. If bookings require a phone call or message, make that action visible and ensure someone is responsible for responding.

The campaign also needs a way to distinguish enquiries that came from ads from other enquiries. A unique offer code or a booking-form question about how someone found the salon can help connect ad activity with actual appointments. Keep the question simple enough that customers will answer it.

A tracking setup should reflect what the salon can verify. A click on a booking button is not the same as a completed appointment. Record the distinction in reporting, and check booking records alongside the ad platform’s reports before deciding whether a campaign is producing value.

## What should the salon measure?

Measure the steps between an ad and a paying client, not only reactions to the creative. Depending on the booking setup, the salon may be able to record clicks, enquiries, bookings, attended appointments, and revenue. A high number at an earlier step does not establish that the campaign is profitable.

Use consistent definitions. For example, decide whether a “lead” means a completed enquiry form, a phone enquiry, or another action. Decide whether a “new client” means a person who booked or a person who attended and paid. Mixing these definitions makes comparisons unreliable.

A simple record can include the campaign or offer, enquiry date, response, booking outcome, attendance, and sale. If the booking platform does not pass this information back to the ad account, a manual record can still help the salon assess results. Do not count the same person as multiple new clients because they contacted the salon through more than one channel.

Review the campaign against its business goal. If the objective is to fill appointments for a particular service, assess whether the resulting clients booked that service and attended. If the aim is to acquire new customers, separate new clients from existing clients responding to the promotion. ## How can a salon estimate an acceptable acquisition cost?

Work from gross profit rather than revenue alone. Revenue does not account for the costs of delivering the service. To estimate an acceptable acquisition cost, choose a period for evaluating a client, estimate the gross profit from that client during the period, and compare it with the cost of acquiring the client.

The formula is:

**Maximum cost per new client = estimated gross profit from that client during the chosen period.**

For a lead-based campaign, the maximum cost per lead depends on the proportion of leads that become clients:

**Maximum cost per lead = estimated gross profit per client multiplied by the lead-to-client rate.**

These formulas are planning tools, not guarantees. The result depends on the salon’s own prices, costs, booking outcomes, and client behaviour. Include the period being considered so that an initial appointment is not confused with repeat visits that have not happened yet.

### Worked example

Every input and result in this example is an **assumption** for illustration, not a benchmark or expected outcome.

Assume a salon spends **$300 per month on ads**. Assume **10 enquiries** are recorded, and **3** become attended new-client appointments. The assumed ad cost per attended new client is **$100**, calculated by dividing the assumed spend by the assumed attended clients.

Now assume each new client generates **$200 in revenue** during the evaluation period, and the assumed gross margin is **50%**. The estimated gross profit is **$100 per client**. Under these assumptions, the campaign’s ad cost per attended new client equals the estimated gross profit per client, before considering any other marketing or operating costs.

The example does not establish whether a real salon should spend $300 or expect 10 enquiries. Replace every input with the salon’s own figures. If the real lead-to-client rate is lower, the maximum affordable cost per lead will also be lower. If the salon has not yet recorded enough outcomes to estimate that rate, treat the calculation as a working assumption and update it as booking data accumulates.

## How should a salon improve a campaign?

Change one meaningful element at a time so that the result remains interpretable. A salon could test a different service, message, image, or booking route. If several parts change together, it becomes difficult to tell which change contributed to a difference in enquiries or bookings.

Keep a record of what changed and what outcome the salon intended to improve. If enquiries arrive but do not become appointments, investigate the offer, response process, and booking experience before assuming that the audience is the problem. If appointments are booked but clients do not attend, assess the booking and confirmation process separately from ad delivery.

Follow up promptly using the contact method the customer chose. A clear response should answer questions, explain any relevant offer terms, and provide a direct route to book. The ad account cannot complete that part of the customer journey for the salon.

Compare paid activity with the salon’s other acquisition channels, but keep the records separate enough to identify the source of a client. ## FAQ

### Is there a reliable Facebook cost-per-lead benchmark for hair salons?

The source used for this article does not provide a hair-salon Facebook cost-per-lead benchmark. Its salon-ad content includes a promotional example, not a measured advertising result. Use the salon’s own cost and booking data to set expectations.

### Should a salon copy the discount shown in an ad example?

No. The promotion on Trafft’s page is an example from that page, not evidence that the same discount will be profitable or appropriate for another business. Check the salon’s service costs, margins, and appointment capacity before advertising a promotion.

### Should the salon count booking-page clicks as new clients?

No. A click records an action on a page, not a completed appointment. Track enquiries and bookings separately where possible, and use attended, paying clients when assessing acquisition cost.

### What if the salon cannot connect bookings to its ads?

Use a consistent intake question or unique offer code, and compare those records with the ad reports. Record the limits of the method so that an enquiry is not mistaken for a confirmed booking.
