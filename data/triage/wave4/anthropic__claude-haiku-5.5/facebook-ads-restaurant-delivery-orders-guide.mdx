## Who Are You Actually Reaching on Facebook?

Pew Research's 2026 survey of 5,511 U.S. adults found that [70% use Facebook and 84% use YouTube](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/fact-sheet/social-media/). Those shares describe broad reach, not diners who order delivery. A ghost-kitchen or takeout menu still needs a tight audience, because a large pool of people who never order in will absorb budget without producing orders.

Start with a defined radius around your kitchen, usually the delivery zone your platform or staff can actually serve. Add interest and behaviour layers only where you can verify them against your own order data. If you run a noodle shop with a 4-mile delivery area, a 15-mile audience mostly buys impressions you cannot deliver food to.

Use your existing customer list as a seed for comparison. Export your last 90 days of delivery customers and check which neighbourhoods they came from. Those postcodes or ZIP codes become your first targeting zones, and you widen only after the first zones show a cost per order you can live with.

## How Do You Set a Break-Even Cost per Order?

Before you spend, calculate what one delivery order can afford to cost you. The formula is: break-even cost per order equals average order value, minus food cost, minus platform commission. Any ad cost above that figure loses money on each order.

That figure is for Google search, has no restaurant category, and is dated, so use it only as a rough reference point, not a forecast for Facebook.

## How Should You Schedule and Split a Small Budget?

Peak ordering windows matter more than total spend. Set your Facebook ad schedule to your lunch and dinner peaks, and reduce spend during hours with few orders. Use your own order timestamps to decide the windows rather than guessing.

A practical starting checklist for a $20 to $25 daily budget:

1. Pull your last 60 days of orders by hour and weekday.
2. Mark the two to four hours that produce most of your orders.
3. Set the ad schedule to those hours and keep a small share for the rest of the day.
4. Run one ad set on your highest-converting zone rather than splitting the budget across several small audiences.
5. Review cost per completed order after 7 days before changing anything.

Splitting a small budget into many ad sets slows learning. One focused ad set with a single offer usually gives a clearer answer than three thinly funded ones.

## How Do You Test Creative Without Guessing?

Meta's Help Center recommends [A/B testing with an equal budget for each version](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/1738164643098669), because the test then compares strategies on a cost-per-result basis. It also advises against testing informally by switching ad sets on and off manually, since overlapping audiences make the results unreliable.

For a restaurant, change one variable at a time. Compare a professional food photo against a phone photo of the same dish in its takeout container, or a short video against a still image. Keep the audience, offer and schedule identical so the only difference is the creative.

Judge each variant by cost per completed order, not clicks. A cheap click on a dish that customers never buy is wasted spend. Stop a losing variant only after the test has enough orders to be meaningful, which for a small restaurant may take several weeks.

## What Should You Track Before You Scale?

Track purchases, not link clicks. Install the Facebook pixel on your own ordering page and send a purchase event when an order confirms. If orders come through a third-party platform, ask the platform what order-confirmation data it shares before you rely on it for optimisation.

Before increasing spend, confirm three numbers: cost per completed order, average order value from ad-driven orders, and repeat rate after 30 days. If cost per order stays under your buffer and repeat orders hold, a modest increase is reasonable. If it climbs as you spend more, the audience or creative is saturating and you should return to testing.

Platform fees differ between services, and commission rates change how much room your ad budget has. Compare the fee structures before deciding which platform to send paid traffic to. ## FAQ

### What is a good cost per order for restaurant delivery ads?

It depends on your margin. Using the assumption inputs in this guide, a $22 order with 30% food cost and 20% commission contributes $11.00, so anything above that loses money. Plan against a lower buffer, such as $6.60.

### Should I optimise for clicks or completed orders?

Completed orders. Clicks can look cheap while producing no sales. Set up purchase tracking on your ordering page so the campaign optimises toward the outcome that pays.

### How many ad sets should I run on a small budget?

One focused ad set is usually enough at about $20 to $25 a day. Splitting into several small audiences slows learning and makes results harder to read.

### Is Facebook better than Google for delivery orders?

It depends on intent. Search captures people already looking for food, while Facebook builds awareness among nearby audiences. Many owners test both and compare cost per completed order.

### Do I need professional food photography?

Not necessarily. Test a phone photo of the actual takeout container against a professional image, and judge the winner by cost per completed order.
