A coffee shop social media strategy works when it puts your posts where local customers already spend time, keeps a fixed weekly publishing routine, answers every comment, and tests one change at a time. Usage data from Pew Research Center shows Facebook (70%) and Instagram (51%) reach the most US adults, so they are the default starting points. Add location tags to every post, batch your content, and measure visits, not just likes. The six steps below cover each part.

## Which platforms should a coffee shop use first?

Start with the platforms your neighbours use. Pew Research Center's 2026 survey of 5,511 US adults found that YouTube (84%) and Facebook (70%) are the most widely used, followed by Instagram (51%). The same survey lists TikTok at 37% and Nextdoor at 18%, the latter of which is built around neighbourhood posts, which suits a shop that depends on regulars nearby. ([Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/fact-sheet/social-media/))

Pick two platforms, not six. Facebook covers events and local groups well, and Instagram suits photos and short video. If your customers are mostly in the UK, Australia or Canada, check local usage before committing, and see the regional guide on [local marketing in Australia for small businesses](/blog/local-marketing-australia-small-business-2026). Usage figures in this article are US-only.

## What should a coffee shop post each week?

Batch your content once a week instead of posting when inspiration hits. A fixed set of three post types keeps the feed varied without a daily scramble:

- A welcome or menu post tied to a day of the week, such as a Monday drink special.
- A behind-the-scenes or sourcing post, such as a barista dialling in a new blend or a note about where the beans come from.
- A customer or community post, such as a repost of a tagged photo or a feature on a nearby bakery whose pastries you sell.

Schedule the week's posts in one sitting using a free tool such as Meta Business Suite. Mix in product shots, but keep them a minority, so the feed reads as a place to visit rather than a menu board.

## How do geotags and local hashtags bring in foot traffic?

Every post should carry a location tag. Tag your shop, then the neighbourhood or a landmark. Location tags tell platforms where your content is relevant, and they are free.

Build hashtags in three groups: neighbourhood (for example, a district name plus "coffee"), city, and niche terms such as "pour over" or "latte art." Rotate the groups by post type instead of pasting the same thirty tags under everything. Repeating identical tags across every post looks spammy and gives you no information about which tags work.

## How do you engage with comments without burning out?

Comments are where a feed turns into a relationship. Set one short daily block, for example 15 to 30 minutes, to reply to every comment and direct message. Reply with a question rather than a bare "thanks," such as asking which milk a commenter prefers. Spend part of the block liking and replying to posts from regulars and nearby businesses, which builds the local ties that bring people back.

Unanswered comments are a missed chance, so treat this block as part of opening the shop, not an optional extra.

## Should a coffee shop A/B test its boosted posts?

Yes, but test properly. Meta's help documentation says A/B testing splits the audience so that nobody sees both versions, and it recommends using the same budget for each version so the comparison is fair. It also advises against informal testing, such as switching ads on and off by hand, because that causes overlapping audiences and unreliable results. ([Meta Business Help Center](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/1738164643098669))

For a coffee shop, test one variable at a time. Compare two images of the same drink, or two offers, such as a morning pastry bundle and a free refill. Keep the budget equal and run the test long enough to gather a useful count of results before deciding.

## What does a social media budget need to earn back?

This worked example uses assumed inputs. Replace them with your own numbers before you rely on the result.

- Assumption: content time is 4 hours per week, and your time costs $20 per hour, so labour is $80 per week.
- Assumption: you boost one post per week at $10.
- Assumption: your average order is $5.50 in gross sales.

Total weekly cost is $80 plus $10, which is $90. Break-even requires $90 divided by $5.50, or about 16.4 extra orders per week. Round up to 17 visits. If your tracked social visits are below that, the routine is costing more than it returns, so reduce content time or drop the boost. Gross sales ignore cost of goods, so a stricter check would use your margin instead.

Track extra visits with a simple method: a weekly code word in stories, a "mention this post" prompt, or a count of new customers who say they found you online. Record it in the same spreadsheet each week so the trend is visible.

## FAQ

### How many times a week should a coffee shop post?

Three to five posts per week is a practical rhythm for most small shops. The aim is consistency rather than volume, so choose a number you can keep up every week.

### Is Facebook still worth using for a coffee shop?

Yes. Pew's 2026 survey reports that 70% of US adults use Facebook, though that is down from 71% in 2025. It remains useful for events and local group reach.

### Do location tags really help?

Location tags are free and tell platforms where a post belongs. Use them on every post, along with neighbourhood and city hashtags.

### Should I use the same hashtags on every post?

No. Rotating hashtag groups by post type looks more natural and lets you see which groups perform. Identical tags on every post add little.

### How do I know if my social media is working?

Track tagged photos, direct messages about visits, and codes redeemed in store each week. Compare the count against your break-even number before deciding to continue.
