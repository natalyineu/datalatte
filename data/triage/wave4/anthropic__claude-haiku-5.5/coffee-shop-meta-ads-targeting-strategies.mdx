Meta Ads targeting for a coffee shop means drawing a tight radius around the door, testing one audience variable at a time, and judging campaigns by store visits instead of clicks. A 1.5 to 3 mile radius is a common starting assumption for a neighbourhood café, and it should be tested against the shop's own sales data. Meta's guidance is to compare versions with the same budget and to avoid informal on-and-off testing. The steps below cover audiences, creative, testing, and a worked budget.

## Which Meta platforms reach the most customers?

Facebook and Instagram are the two Meta platforms most relevant to a coffee shop. According to [Pew Research Center's 2026 fact sheet](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/fact-sheet/social-media/), 70% of U.S. adults say they ever use Facebook and 51% say they use Instagram. TikTok (37%) and WhatsApp (33%) are smaller but still reach large audiences. YouTube, at 84%, is the most widely used platform in the survey.

These figures come from a survey of 5,511 U.S. adults conducted January to June 2026, with an overall margin of error of plus or minus 1.8 percentage points. They describe the whole country, not a single postcode. Before choosing placements, check which platforms the shop's regulars actually use. A café near a university may see different patterns from one in a suburban office park.

## How do you set a local radius and audience for a coffee shop?

Start with the people who can realistically walk or drive to the shop during a normal week. A radius of 1.5 to 3 miles is a working assumption for many urban and suburban cafés, not a Meta requirement. Widen it only if sales records show customers coming from further away. A very wide radius spends money on people who will never visit.

Layer location with one or two interest groups that match the regulars, such as local fitness studios, coworking spaces, or parent groups. Avoid stacking so many filters that the audience becomes too small for Meta to learn from. A small test audience is workable, but it should still be large enough to produce a steady count of impressions and clicks.

Create separate ad sets for distinct groups, such as parents, students, and office workers. Each group can then be compared on the same budget, which shows which group actually turns into store visits.

## How should you run an A/B test without wasting budget?

Meta's help page on [A/B testing for Meta Ads](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/1738164643098669) describes a test in which each version is shown to a separate segment of the audience, so nobody sees both. Before testing, Meta recommends writing a hypothesis, for example that a local-radius audience will outperform a broad interest audience for a particular shop.

Meta also advises using the same budget for both versions so the comparison is fair. Testing informally, by switching ad sets on and off by hand, can produce overlapping audiences and unreliable results. The test can be created either by duplicating a campaign or by comparing two existing campaigns or ad sets.

Change one variable at a time: the audience, the creative, or the placement. Changing several at once makes it impossible to know which change caused the result.

A simple test plan for a single café could look like this:

1. Write one hypothesis, such as "a 2-mile radius outperforms a 10-mile radius for cost per visit."
2. Duplicate the campaign, keep the budget identical, and change only the radius.
3. Let both versions run until each has a steady count of redemptions.
4. Keep the winning version and retire the other.
5. Start the next test with a new variable, such as the offer.

## What ad creative and offer should a coffee shop run?

Rotate creative on a schedule rather than leaving one ad running indefinitely. A rule of refreshing creative every one to two weeks is an assumption to test, and it works well alongside a simple tracker that records which image and offer each ad uses. Several variations should be prepared before launch so that a replacement is ready when performance slips.

Use real photos of drinks and staff, and keep the opening line of the primary text short so it reads on a phone before the "see more" cut-off. A clear offer with an end date, such as a free pastry with any drink this week, gives people a reason to visit now.

## How do you measure store visits and return on ad spend?

Click counts do not show whether anyone walked in. Each campaign needs a trackable route to the till: a unique offer code, a dedicated landing page, or a QR code shown on the ad. Redemptions can then be counted against spend.

Two numbers matter most. Cost per in-store visit is total spend divided by redemptions. Return on ad spend is revenue from redeemers divided by total spend. The worked example below uses assumed inputs, so replace them with the shop's own figures.

| Input or result | Value | Label |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monthly Meta spend | $600 | Assumption |
| Offer redemptions in the month | 150 | Assumption |
| Average spend per redeemer | $11.00 | Assumption |
| Cost per in-store visit | $4.00 | Calculated ($600 ÷ 150) |
| Revenue from redeemers | $1,650 | Calculated (150 × $11) |
| Return on ad spend | 2.75x | Calculated ($1,650 ÷ $600) |

The example shows that a 2.75x return is positive on revenue alone, but it does not yet cover the cost of ingredients and labour. Gross margin on the drinks sold should be applied before deciding whether the campaign is profitable.

## Which mistakes waste a coffee shop's Meta budget?

The most common error is optimising for link clicks when the goal is people at the counter. Clicks are cheap, but they do not show whether anyone came in. The objective and the tracked action should happen in the shop or at the register.

A second error is a radius so wide that most clicks come from outside walking distance. A third is leaving one creative running until it loses its effect. Fixing these three points usually makes the largest difference to cost per visit.

## FAQ

### Is Meta Ads worth it for a small coffee shop?

It depends on whether store visits can be tracked. Without a trackable offer or redemption route, it is not possible to tell whether spend is producing customers. With one, the cost per visit and return on spend show whether the budget is working.

### How long should a Meta Ads test run?

The Meta help page does not set a fixed duration. A reasonable team rule is to run each test long enough to gather a steady count of redemptions (assumption: about two weeks for a single café), then decide based on cost per visit.

### Should a coffee shop target by age?

Testing is more reliable than assuming. Separate ad sets can be set up for groups such as students, parents, and office workers, each with the same budget, and cost per visit compared. The groups that drive redemptions stay in the plan.

### Can store visits be tracked without a point-of-sale integration?

Yes. A unique offer code shown at the till, a dedicated landing page, or a QR code on the ad can all be counted manually. Redemptions can be logged daily so they can be compared with spend.
