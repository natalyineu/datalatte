## What Does Local Marketing Mean for a Small Business?

Local marketing targets customers within a set radius of your physical location, and HubSpot notes it is also called neighborhood marketing or local store marketing ([HubSpot](https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/local-marketing)). It suits any business whose sales happen in person, such as shops, salons, food sellers and repair services.

Salesforce describes local marketing as focusing effort on the areas where target customers are most likely to be found, using knowledge of local demographics, preferences and behaviour ([Salesforce](https://www.salesforce.com/marketing/local-marketing/)). The practical starting point is knowing which neighbourhoods your customers come from and what they ask for.

## Why Does Mobile Matter for Local Customers?

Most local searches happen on a phone, so the first test of your marketing is what a customer sees on a small screen. HubSpot reports that 61% of mobile searchers are more likely to contact a local business if it has a mobile-friendly site ([HubSpot](https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/local-marketing)). That figure is secondary, and it comes from a global source.

HubSpot's checklist starts with confirming the website is mobile-friendly, and it suggests Google's Mobile-Friendly Test to check. For a business that takes orders by phone, the same logic applies to your Facebook Page: the address, opening hours and contact number should be visible without scrolling.

## Which Local Discovery Habits Do the Sources Measure?

HubSpot cites research that 97% of people learn more about a local company online than through any other medium, and that 88% of mobile searches for local businesses end in a call or visit within one day ([HubSpot](https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/local-marketing)). Both are secondary figures from HubSpot's own summaries of other studies, and neither is broken down by country.

The useful takeaway is the order of events. People search first, then decide, then act. A business that is hard to find online loses the first step, even if its shop sign is strong.

## How Do You Run a Low-Cost Facebook Test?

Treat the test as an experiment with a fixed loss you can accept. The worked example below uses assumption figures only, because no sourced local ad costs are available. Replace every value with your own numbers.

| Input | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Test spend (assumption) | 300 local currency units |
| Duration (assumption) | 14 days |
| Paid orders (assumption) | 12 |
| Cost per paid order | 300 divided by 12, which is 25 units |
| Profit per order (assumption) | 10 units |

The cost per order of 25 units is above the profit of 10 units, so the test loses money on these inputs. The offer, price or audience needs changing before more spend. If the profit per order were 30 units, the same test would clear its cost. Keep the arithmetic simple and repeat it with real figures after each test.

A step checklist for the first month:

1. Confirm your Facebook Page shows your name, location, hours and a contact number.
2. Write one offer you can keep for several weeks, so customers can compare like with like.
3. Set a fixed test spend you can afford to lose entirely.
4. Ask every customer how they found you, and record the answer in a notebook or sheet.
5. Divide spend by paid orders at the end of 14 days, and compare the result with your profit per order.
6. Change one thing at a time before the next test.
