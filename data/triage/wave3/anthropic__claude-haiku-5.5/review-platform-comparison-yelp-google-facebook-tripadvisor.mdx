Build Google reviews first. Google's local ranking guidance says results depend mainly on relevance, distance and prominence, and that prominence draws on how many reviews a business has and how positive they are. Google also states that there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking, so reviews and an accurate profile are the levers a business controls. BrightLocal's 2026 survey of 1,002 US consumers found 71% read Google reviews and 45% had written one in the past year. Yelp, Facebook and Tripadvisor each follow different rules, so each needs its own approach.

## Why do Google reviews carry the most weight?

Google's ranking guidance lists three local factors: relevance, distance and prominence. Prominence covers how well known a business is, including how many websites link to it and how many reviews it has. More reviews and positive ratings can help a business's local ranking, according to Google's help page.

Relevance comes from complete, detailed business information. Google asks businesses to keep the address, hours, category and attributes such as parking or Wi-Fi accurate, because an inaccurate profile may not appear for relevant searches in the area.

The same help page says there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking. The work that moves the result is a verified, accurate profile and a steady flow of reviews.

## How should a business respond to Google reviews?

Google says replying to reviews shows that a business values customer feedback, and that positive reviews plus helpful replies can help it stand out. The guidance is short on specifics, so replies should be treated as part of the review process rather than a separate task.

Replies are the step most small businesses skip when busy. If volume is the problem, a drafting workflow helps. Each reply should refer to what the customer actually said. Generic thank-you lines add little to the page and do not answer the customer's point.

## How do Yelp's rules differ from Google's?

Yelp takes a stricter line on asking for reviews. A 2016 article quoted Yelp saying that most business owners only ask their happy customers, and that self-selected reviews create a bias consumers can detect. This is a secondary account from Search Engine Watch, so check Yelp's current guidelines before acting on it.

Yelp Ads also work differently from Google Ads. According to a Search Atlas comparison, a secondary vendor source, Yelp Ads target people browsing local business listings, while Google Ads captures demand from active searchers. The [comparison of Google Ads and Facebook Ads for local business](/blog/google-ads-vs-facebook-ads-local-business) covers the paid side in more depth.

Any Yelp approach should follow Yelp's own rules rather than a Google playbook. A claimed Yelp profile can still matter for customers who browse listings, even if the business does not ask for reviews there.

## Which logos and badges can you show on your website?

Logo rules are a common trap. A 2018 summary from AssetLab, a secondary source, sets out the rules for each platform. Check current brand guidelines before publishing anything, because these rules change.

| Platform | Logo on your website | Badge | Condition in the summary |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Yelp | Yes | Yes | Respect the logo and link to the Yelp page |
| Google | No | No | Needs specific written permission from Google |
| Facebook | "f" only | No | The image cannot be modified; other brand assets have their own rules |
| Thumbtack | No | Pro and Review badges | Badge must load live from Thumbtack with an HTML snippet |

The Google row matters most for local businesses. A site can link to the Google profile and include a "leave a review" link without using Google's logo.

## Where do Facebook and Tripadvisor fit?

The sources used for this guide do not cover current Facebook or Tripadvisor review policies. Any rule read about those two platforms should be checked against their current help centres before acting on it.

The sourced fact for Facebook is its logo rule: only the "f" may be used, and the image cannot be modified. That matters for anyone who wants a social proof badge on a website.

A reasonable order, based on the sourced rules rather than measured results, is Google first, then a claimed Yelp listing so the details are correct, then Facebook and Tripadvisor only where the business's customers use them.

## FAQ

### Which review site matters most for a local business?

Google. Its local ranking guidance names reviews as a factor in prominence, and it is the platform where most local searches happen, so it is the first place to build reviews.

### Can a business ask customers for Yelp reviews?

Yelp discourages it. A 2016 account quoting Yelp says self-selected reviews create bias, and Yelp may filter reviews. Check Yelp's current guidelines before running any request.

### Can a business use the Google logo on its website?

Not without specific written permission from Google. A 2018 AssetLab summary, a secondary source, says Google does not allow others to use its logo. Linking to the profile is the alternative.

### How do I keep my Google Business Profile accurate?

Edit the profile on Google Search or in Google Maps, using the account linked to the profile. Keep the address, hours, category and phone numbers current, and set a specific service area if the business serves customers at their location.
