## Which free sources should you check first?

Start with the data you already control. Google's Business Profile guidance says relevance is how well a profile matches what someone searches for, so the words customers use should appear in your business name, category, services and description. Google also says there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking. That makes accurate profile details the main lever a business can pull without spending money.

Global Genie, an Australian agency, lists Google Business Profile, Google Trends, Google Keyword Planner and Ubersuggest among its ten local keyword tools. That is a secondary list, not a test, but it shows which free options agencies often reach for first. It describes Google Trends as a free tool for seeing what people look for in a local area, which helps when you compare seasonal interest in different services.

Google's help pages do not describe autocomplete as volume data. Treat its suggestions as a phrasing check: type your service and town, then note what appears. The phrases are useful for page titles and headings. They are not a reliable way to size demand.

## What decides local rankings, and how do keywords fit in?

Google's local ranking guidance names three factors: relevance, distance and prominence. Distance depends on where the searcher is, so keywords cannot change it. Relevance and prominence are different. Prominence is based on how well-known a business is, including how many websites link to it and how many reviews it has.

Keywords therefore only help with relevance. If a profile says "plumbing" but customers search "emergency boiler repair," the match is weak. Google's edit guidance asks businesses to choose a specific primary category and to make the service area as specific and accurate as possible. Those two settings should match the phrases you plan to target, so the profile and the service pages tell the same story.

Prominence is a separate job. Press mentions can create outside links, and [how to get local press coverage for your small business](/blog/how-to-get-local-press-coverage-for-your-small-business) covers that route. Keyword research will not fix a weak prominence profile on its own, so it is worth working on both at the same time.

## What does a paid tool actually cost per year?

All inputs below are **assumptions** for a one-location service business. The prices come from the Wordtracker pricing page.

1. Assume one business that needs 100 tracked keywords, the Starter limit.
2. Monthly billing: $49 × 12 = $588 a year.
3. Annual billing: $392 a year, a stated saving of $196.
4. Cost per tracked keyword on annual billing: $392 ÷ 100 = $3.92 a year (assumption: all 100 slots used).
5. Assume the business stays on the free plan for three months, then pays for one year on annual billing: $392 total in year one (assumption).

The free sources cost $0, so the decision is whether tracked keyword positions are worth about $3.92 each per year. If phrases are checked only once a quarter, the free plan may be enough. If service pages change monthly and the effect needs to be measured, the Starter plan's annual price is the cheaper route. Compare that figure with the value of a single new booking from a page that ranks, using your own average job value as the assumption.

## How do you turn a keyword list into pages?

Work through these steps in order. Each one relies on the sources above and on the profile settings Google describes.

1. Collect 10 to 20 phrases from your Business Profile, Search Console queries if you have them, Google Trends and autocomplete.
2. Group phrases by the service behind them. "Emergency boiler repair" and "boiler service cost" may need separate pages.
3. Match each group to one service page with your town named in the title.
4. Check that your Business Profile primary category covers the same service.
5. Review your service area and hours so they match the pages you just built.
6. Re-check tracked phrases on a fixed schedule, such as monthly, and change pages only when the data shows a clear pattern.

If the business also runs paid search, [local service ads](/blog/google-local-service-ads-worth-it-local-business) are a separate channel with their own cost model. Keep organic keyword research and paid spend in separate budgets so one does not hide the results of the other.

## FAQ

### What is the best free local keyword research tool?

Your own profile and search data come first, then Google Trends and autocomplete for phrasing. Global Genie lists these as the free starting set, though it is a secondary source.

### Does Wordtracker have a free plan?

Yes. Its pricing page lists a free plan at $0 with 3 searches a day and 3 AI questions asked once at set-up.

### Do I need a paid tool for local SEO?

Not always. Paid tools help when a business tracks many phrases or several locations. Google says there is no paid way to buy a better local ranking, so a tool only helps with measuring and planning.

### How many keywords should a local business target?

The sources give no fixed number. A practical starting point is one primary phrase per service page and one primary category on the Business Profile.
