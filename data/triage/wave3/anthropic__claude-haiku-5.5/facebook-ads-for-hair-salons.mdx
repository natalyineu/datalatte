## Is There a Published Facebook Ad Cost for Salons?

No source reviewed for this article gives a verified Facebook cost per click or cost per lead for hair salons. The Meta Business Help Center page returned a 404 error when checked, so it cannot be used for figures or policy. The ClicksYou guide describes salon Facebook ads as a way to reach new clients and bring back old ones, but it does not publish cost benchmarks.

That gap matters. Without a benchmark, a salon should not set a budget from a competitor's number or a generic average. Set a small test budget, measure what it produces, and decide from the results.

The worked example later in this article uses a clearly marked assumed cost per click. Replace that number with the cost shown in Ads Manager once the test starts. The actual cost depends on the city, the audience and how well the ad matches the offer.

## Should a Salon Run Lead or Traffic Campaigns?

A traffic campaign sends people to a web page, such as an online booking form. A lead campaign collects a name and contact details inside Facebook, so the salon can call or message the person back. The right choice depends on how clients prefer to book.

Choose traffic if the online booking page works well on a phone and clients can pick a time without speaking to anyone. Choose leads if most bookings happen by phone or text, or if a stylist needs to confirm the service before the appointment. Pick one format for the test, because switching halfway through makes results hard to compare.

Whichever format is picked, the same rule applies. Count completed bookings, not clicks or form fills. The RŌZ salon advertising guide advises starting with one service, one audience, one channel and one way to count completed bookings, which is a useful standard for a first Facebook test. ## What Offer Should the Ad Make?

The offer should be something a new client can accept in one step. Zeely's salon ideas include a new-client offer such as "$20 off first color service," which reduces hesitation before a first visit. A first-visit price, a consultation or a gap-fill slot this week all work the same way: they give the viewer one clear reason to act now.

Show real work from the salon rather than stock photos, and name the neighborhood or town. Local details signal that the salon serves the people who see the ad. Keep the headline and the booking page consistent, so the client sees the same price on both.

Do not claim results that cannot be shown. Do not use invented reviews or testimonials. Before running before-and-after images, check Meta's advertising standards directly, because this article could not confirm the current rules. For a broader look at creator-led offers, the [influencer marketing guide for hair salons](/blog/influencer-marketing-for-hair-salons-how-to-get-free-bookings-from-local-creator) covers another low-cost route to first bookings.

## Who Should You Target?

ClicksYou advises choosing a specific audience before running a campaign, using age, behavior and interests. The aim is to reach people likely to book the service being advertised, rather than a broad crowd. A salon should start with a radius around the building, then add an age and gender range that matches its typical clients.

Keep interest targeting simple at first. Narrow settings can limit delivery, and a small test does not have enough data to show which narrow group works best. Once the test has run for a while, results can be compared by audience, and the groups that do not produce bookings can be cut.

Custom audiences built from the salon's own client list can help later, but only after there is enough data for the test to mean something. Until then, a local radius with a clear offer is the most practical setup for most salons.

If only 25% of leads book, the cost per new client roughly doubles to about $235, which uses most of the $340 first-year revenue before those costs. Test the booking rate first, because it has the largest effect on the result.

## How Do You Know It Worked?

Track three things separately: form leads or booking-page visits, phone calls, and completed appointments. Zeely points to tracking bookings, and RŌZ advises counting completed bookings, so both point away from clicks as the success measure. Ask every new client how they found the salon, and record the answer at the time of booking.

A promo code for the ad offer helps. Zeely suggests a promo code in the point-of-sale system so the salon can see exactly how many bookings came from the offer. Compare the cost per new client with the first-year value of a client, and keep the campaign running only if the cost is lower.

A simple checklist for the first month:

1. Choose one service and one offer, and write down the price.
2. Set the radius, age range and daily budget before launch.
3. Add a promo code to the offer and log every redemption.
4. Record how each new client found the salon.
5. Review cost per new client at two weeks, then decide whether to continue, change the offer or stop.

Facebook is one channel among several. Search ads capture people already looking for a stylist, and outdoor screens can support awareness near the salon. ## FAQ

### How much should a hair salon spend on Facebook ads?
No source reviewed sets a standard budget. Start with an amount that can be lost for a month, such as the $400 test above, and scale only if cost per new client stays below what a client is worth.

### Do Facebook ads work for hair salons?
They can bring new clients when the offer is clear and follow-up is quick. No source reviewed reports salon-specific results, so the only reliable answer comes from the salon's own booking data.

### Should I use lead ads or send people to my booking page?
Use lead ads if most bookings happen by phone or message. Send people to the booking page if online booking works well on a phone.

### How long should a Facebook test run?
Run it long enough to collect a meaningful number of leads and bookings. Judge the test on completed bookings and cost per new client, not on clicks.
