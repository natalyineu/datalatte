To set up Google Ads for a small business, create an account, build a Search campaign aimed at a specific local service, set a daily budget you can afford to lose while testing, and install conversion tracking before launch. Then review the search terms report every week and remove irrelevant queries. Google Ads does not need a marketing degree, but it does need tracking and regular checks. The steps below cover each part in order, with a worked budget example you can adapt.

## How do you create a Google Ads account?

Start at [Google Ads](https://ads.google.com/) and sign in with a Google account you will keep for the business. Use the same account family that manages your website and Business Profile where possible, because that makes tracking and access easier to manage later.

Google Ads will ask for a campaign goal and campaign type during setup. You can enter billing details before you have a finished campaign, but nothing runs until you publish. Confirm the business location, service area and the website URL you want to send clicks to.

You need a live page to send clicks to. Build a simple landing page for the one service you are advertising, with a phone number, a booking button and your city named clearly. Sending traffic to your homepage usually gives visitors more options than they need and makes it harder to measure which ads produced a booking.

## Which campaign type should a small business choose?

Most local service businesses start with a Search campaign because it shows ads when someone types a specific query, such as a service plus a city. Google's help documentation notes that for other automated campaign types, advertisers should consider [Performance Max or Search campaigns](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/2404190). Display campaigns are a different product: they serve ads across [35 million websites and apps](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/2404190) and on Google-owned properties such as YouTube and Gmail, so they reach people who were not searching for you.

Search is usually the better first test because intent is clearer. If you later want automated coverage across more inventory, read the [Performance Max guide for local businesses](/blog/google-performance-max-for-local-business-complete-guide-2026) before you move budget into it. Some service categories also qualify for [Local Services Ads](/blog/google-local-services-ads-complete-guide), which run on a different pay structure and are not the same as a standard Search campaign.

## How much should you spend per day?

A daily budget limits how much Google will spend in a day, but it does not guarantee a result. The best way to choose a starting number is to work backwards from the cost per click you expect and the number of leads you need. The example below uses one set of assumed inputs. Replace them with your own figures once you have data.

| Input | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Daily budget | $10 | Assumption |
| Days in month | 30 | Calendar |
| Monthly spend | $300 | Calculated |
| Average search CPC | $2.69 | WordStream all-industry average |
| Expected clicks | about 111 | $300 divided by $2.69 |
| Conversion rate | 5% | Assumption |
| Expected leads | about 5.6 | 111 times 5% |
| Average job value | $80 | Assumption |
| Expected revenue | about $447 | 5.6 times $80 |

On these assumed inputs, revenue exceeds the $300 spend, but only before your costs of delivering the service. If your conversion rate is 2% instead, the same budget produces about 2 leads, which is a much weaker result. Conversion rate is the input most worth measuring in your own account. Raise the budget only after you have confirmed the leads are real.

## How do you choose keywords and write ads?

Keywords are the search terms that can trigger your ads. For a local business, the strongest starting list contains a service, a location and a clear intent word such as "book," "repair," "near me" or a specific city. Build the list from your own customer questions, from a search for your service in your area, and from Google's Keyword Planner.

Write ads that state a clear offer and the city or area you serve. Avoid vague claims like "great service." Use a call to action that matches what the customer can do on your page, such as booking a visit or calling the office. Each ad group should contain closely related keywords so the ad text matches the search.

The [Google Ads service page](/services/google-ads) at DataLatte covers this setup for local businesses if you want a specialist to handle the build.

## What should you track after launch?

Conversion tracking is the first thing to set up. A conversion can be a form submission, a booking, a phone call from the ad or a completed purchase. Without it, Google Ads cannot tell which clicks led to revenue, and you will judge the campaign on clicks alone.

Check the search terms report weekly during the first month. Add irrelevant queries as negative keywords so you stop paying for them. Look at which ads and keywords produce conversions, and pause the weak ones once you have enough data to judge them fairly. Also check ad schedule and device performance, and only restrict them when your numbers show a clear pattern.

A simple weekly checklist:

1. Confirm conversions were recorded for the past seven days.
2. Review search terms and add negatives for anything off-service or outside your area.
3. Compare cost per lead and conversion rate to the previous week.
4. Change one thing at a time so you can tell what caused the result.

## When should you bring in outside help?

Doing setup yourself is reasonable for a single service in one area with a few hours a week. Outside help makes more sense when you have several services, several locations, tracking that does not match your bookings, or spend that is rising without more leads. Compare options before you commit, including [whether Google's Local Services Ads are worth it for your type of business](/blog/google-local-service-ads-worth-it-local-business).

If you are running campaigns in Canada, the [local business marketing guide for Canada](/blog/local-business-marketing-canada-guide-2026) covers regional factors that can affect your planning. Check the lead cost in your own market rather than copying a national average.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need to pay for Google Ads, or can I rely on organic search?

Organic search and paid ads measure different things. Organic results depend on your site's visibility and take time to build, while paid ads can show quickly once a campaign is live. Many small businesses use both, and the paid campaign can reveal which keywords convert before you invest in content for them.

### What is the minimum budget that works?

No budget is guaranteed to work. The right starting amount depends on your cost per click, your conversion rate and what one customer is worth to you. The worked example above shows how to test this with assumed inputs, and your own account data should replace those inputs as soon as it is available.

### How long before I see results?

Clicks usually appear soon after a campaign goes live, but conversions need time and data. Judge a new campaign after several weeks of tracked activity rather than after a few days. If you still see no conversions after that, check tracking, the landing page and the keyword list before changing the budget.

### Should I write my own ads?

Yes, if you can keep them specific and honest. Start with a clear offer, the city you serve and one call to action, then test variations. Ads that match the search query and the landing page usually perform better than generic copy.

### Can I run Google Ads without a website?

Google's guidance on campaigns is built around a destination for clicks, so a website or a dedicated landing page is the safest choice. Some local formats send people to a phone call or a business profile instead, but check the current requirements in the Google Ads help documentation before you plan around them.
